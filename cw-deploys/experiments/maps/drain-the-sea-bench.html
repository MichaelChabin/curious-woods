Drain the sea

Below is the whole Earth. Most of it is sea, and the sea hides a landscape nobody had seen until about seventy years ago, when ships began to measure its depth with sound. Drag the water line down the gauge at the map’s edge and the sea goes down. The shallow shelves round the continents dry first. Then a mountain range comes up out of the Atlantic, longer than any on land, running down the middle of the ocean like a seam.

The floor is young. Nothing on it is much older than 180 million years, while the oldest rocks on land are twenty times that. New floor is made along the seam and moves away on both sides, a few centimetres a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow. Turn on Age and the floor is coloured by when it was made: pale at the seam, darker the farther it has travelled.

The Earth’s magnetic field flips every so often: north becomes south, and a compass would point the other way. Floor that cools while the field is one way keeps that direction in its rock. Turn on Stripes and you can see it, in bands either side of the seam, mirrored. Tap the sea anywhere and a probe tells you how deep the floor is, how old it is, and which way a compass pointed when it was made.
