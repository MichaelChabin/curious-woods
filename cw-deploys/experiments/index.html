Ruling
A rectangle two wide and three tall, and five ways to rule the grid underneath it. At Units it holds six squares; at Halves, twenty-four pieces each worth a quarter; at Thirds, fifty-four each worth a ninth; at Tenths, six hundred each worth a hundredth. The count changes every time. The rectangle never moves, and the answer is six every time. Split unlinks the two axes, so x can run in fifths while y runs in halves — and then a piece is worth a tenth, because the worth of a piece is always the two grains multiplied.
Bench, not a lab. The companion to Multiply, and the answer to what that one found: there, grain is tied to zoom, so tenths need ~400px per unit and 6.3 is off every screen at that zoom. Here ruling is an act, so 6.3 × 2 is one stroke at the greeting. Its four questions are in the source. One thing it does that nobody designed: a ruling word goes dark while it cannot measure the side it would have to measure — at 3/5 only fifths and tenths are lit — and with x = 1/3 and y = 1/2 there is no single ruling among the five that measures both, so Link goes dark too. Divisibility, arriving in the panel.

Multiply
A plane, and one white tile at the origin: the 1. Touch it and drag — right for width, up for height — and the 1 stretches into a rectangle, with its twin following across the diagonal a little fainter, showing the same two numbers exchanged. Lift your finger and the rectangle goes; what stays is one tile at its far corner with the product written on it. Build a few and the map draws itself: the squares run white up the crease, everything else grey to one side or the other. Hide the duplicates and only the lower half remains. Tap any tile to see the rectangle it was made from.
Bench, not a lab. Step 3 of Review-Glass-Aug26: one tool, standing on plane.js alone, with no table code — so the grammar can be looked at before anything is built on it. Four questions it exists to answer are in the source, at the top. A fifth turned up on the first drive and was left standing rather than patched: tenths only exist above ~400px per unit, so at the zoom that mints a decimal the stroke reaches about x = 2 — the walk’s own example, 6.3 × 2, needs 2 500px of run and cannot be drawn on an iPad. 1.3 × 2 reads 2.6, exactly.

Frozen or Crawling
A loudspeaker seen edge-on, lit by a strobe. The cone is one bar sliding between { and }, and the strobe catches it in the same place every flash — so a note stops dead. A second note at a whole-number fraction lands in exactly as many places as the fraction’s bottom number, and joining the flashes in order draws a polygon or a star. Switch the tuning to the way a keyboard actually does it and nothing closes: the figure creeps round the rim, once per audible throb.
Michael’s proposal, and it works. Above every window is the circle the bar is a shadow of, which is a sine wave’s content without a sine wave — and the shadow visibly throws information away, so the caption reads e.g. “4 places · 3 shadows”. Fills the standing gap: a true ratio against equal temperament, seen as well as heard, with the drift rate equal to the beat rate to two decimals. Carries its own warning: 7:5 draws a clean pentagram and 45:32 a mess, and they sound identical — a picture of the arithmetic, not of the sound.

Twenty-Four to Forty-Eight
Eight click tracks at 24 : 27 : 30 : 32 : 36 : 40 : 45 : 48. Sped up, that is a whole major scale — and switched on three at a time it is one small chord, 4:5:6, planted in three places. A sieve of the numbers from 24 to 48 shows why those eight and no others; a floor readout falls out of hearing when you switch all eight on at once; and a walk mode steps the voices one at a time so the scale sounds like a scale.
Michael’s question, days after the fact: is there a simple rhythm that gives an entire scale? There is, and it is a sieve rather than a list — the numbers in one octave built from nothing but 2s, 3s and 5s, no two crowding each other. Two things fell out that were not designed: a chord has an audible floor and a scale does not, and the three scale notes carrying a 5 are exactly the three the keyboard misses. Design: Sound-Counting-Bench-Aug25.

Six Against Five
Two click tracks at a fixed whole-number ratio and one speed knob. At two a second it is a rhythm you can count; at two hundred it is a chord. Both voices lay down the identical click — only the rate differs — so nothing changes across the sweep but speed. A piano strip under the rows says where the two rates land on the instrument everyone already knows, to the cent: tap a key with 3:2 selected and the faster voice reads +2¢, which is equal temperament announcing itself without a word of explanation. Brightness is a lowpass and turns out to be the roughness control; click pitch is its own knob, and moving it shows that the note is the ticking rate and not the tick.
Bench for the Sound Series (Sound-Rhythm-Roll-Aug21): can the ratios be delivered by counting rather than by waves? No sine, no spectrum, no pressure — the wave material is deferred to timbre, where it is actually wanted. The reading below the bench is the first sample of the child voice for this material, and it was rewritten the same day after a listening pass disproved three things the page had asserted with confidence: 7:5 and 45:32 are one sound eight cents apart, so the simple fractions are where the sweet intervals live and not why; two notes a fifth apart stay two notes rather than fusing into one; and the roughness lives in the harmonics, which is what brightness is really moving. A ringing at high brightness turned out to be a click truncated at the loop seam, now wrapped. Open: no tapping, and whether 3:2 stays clean at full brightness on the fixed build.

Map Reveal
The coordinate grid constructed in front of you — two seed points, full circles rolling down the axes, every line earning its crossing circles, accelerating as the numbers grow. Knobs for duration, the legible opening, and acceleration; zoom in far enough and the tenths construct themselves.
Bench for the map reveal (Decisions-Controls-Aug12, §8, §11, §15). It settles the tempo by looking. Every step is in the log; speed compresses time, never content.

The Fog Map
Every pane shaded by the rank of its largest prime factor — white at 2, deepening toward black at the 256th. Drag and scroll out as far as you like: the map refuses to go dark, because almost everything is built from small primes.
A magnitude viewing rather than a factor-reading one. It asks how big, not which — and the stripes running the full width are the prime gaps.
Canvas Panes
A load test: how much of a frame does it cost to draw a table of leaded panes while panning and zooming? Reports draw time against the 16.7ms budget, because frame rate alone reads 60fps until things are already bad.
Bench for the plane extraction — the one that could have blocked moving Glass Multiplication to canvas. It didn't.
Fills and Light
Two declared palettes, a resting one and a lit one, both derived from the workshop's own colour so hue never moves. Showing a set — or selecting a pane — lights its members. Nothing is dimmed to make anything else stand out.
Bench for the plane extraction. Dimming was tried and abandoned: every procedural route to a quiet colour computes one nobody chose.
Prime Glass
Prime colours across four workshops, stripes ordered smallest prime to largest, with a monochrome toggle. Asks whether arrangement alone can tell 6, 10 and 14 apart when colour is switched off.
Bench for the plane extraction — if colour turns out to carry delight rather than information, that is a much stronger position than a special palette.
