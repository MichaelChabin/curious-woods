Above about twenty a second we stop saying clicks and start saying Hz. Hz just means per second — the number on screen never changes its meaning, only its name. If you hear nothing on an iPad, check the little switch on the side.

What note is that?

The note you hear is the ticking rate. Not the sound of the click itself.

The click in here is a tiny blip somewhere up around 1,850 a second — and you never hear 1,850. Move the brightness knob while a note is playing and listen carefully: the character changes completely, buzzy to soft, and the note does not shift a hair.

Worth doing once, because it isn’t what you’d guess. The click decides what the note sounds like. The rate decides what the note is.

A note is a fast rhythm

Start something ticking. Slowly — two ticks a second, about the speed of a dripping tap. Now start a second thing ticking a little faster, so that it gets six ticks in the time the first one gets five.

You can hear that. It’s a rhythm. If you pay attention you can find the moment where they both land at once, and you can count your way to the next one.

Now speed both of them up, keeping that six-against-five relationship exactly where it is.

Around eight ticks a second you lose the ability to count. Somewhere around twenty, something else happens, and it’s worth being ready for it, because it’s strange: the ticking stops being ticking. It turns into a note. A steady, held, musical note.

Nothing was added. Nobody put a note in there. There is no note in there. There’s a tick, happening a lot.

That’s the first thing, and most people never find it out: a note isn’t like a fast rhythm. It is one. Anything that repeats fast enough stops being events and becomes a pitch. Your ear has a speed limit, and on the other side of it, counting turns into hearing.

The two numbers survive the trip

Look at what your two tickers are doing now. The slow one is going 250 times a second. The fast one, 300.

300 against 250 is 6 against 5. It’s the same six and five you started with, back when it was a rhythm you could tap along to. You changed the speed and nothing else. And what you’re hearing now is a chord.

There’s a word people use for “times per second” — hertz, usually written Hz. It isn’t a new idea. 250 Hz means 250 times a second. That’s all. The number didn’t change; the name for it did, because it got too fast to count and people wanted a different word for the fast version.

Why some pairs sound sweet and others sound like a scrape

Six against five is easy on the ear. Here’s what’s actually happening.

After five ticks of the slower one and six of the faster, they arrive at the same instant, together. Then the whole thing starts over. The pattern comes back around, quickly and reliably, and your ear notices that and reports one sound rather than two things happening near each other.

Now try 45 against 32. That’s a real pair; it’s sitting on pianos right now. The pattern still comes back around — but not for 32 ticks. That is far too long ago for an ear to remember. Nothing ever seems to line up. And what you hear is not a chord, it’s a roughness, a scrape, something restless.

Small numbers, sweet. Big numbers, scrape. That’s most of what a chord is.

The third note nobody is playing

Both voices are ticking away at their own rates. But the two of them together also make a pattern — and that pattern repeats too, at a rate of its own.

With the slow voice at 250 and the fast one at 300, the whole arrangement comes back around fifty times a second.

Fifty times a second is a note.

So a pair of notes quietly produces a third one underneath them, that neither of them is playing and nobody put there. Usually it’s faint. Sometimes you can hear it outright, and once you have, you can’t stop hearing it.

Here is where it gets tidy. That bottom note is always the slow voice divided by how many of its clicks fit in one pattern. Six against five: 250 divided by 5. Three against two: divided by 2 — which lands close underneath, loud and easy to find. That is why two notes seven keys apart feel like they are standing on something.

Now 45 against 32. The bottom note would be the slow voice divided by 32, which comes out around eight a second. You already know what eight a second is. It isn’t a note, it is a flutter.

So there is no bottom. Nothing underneath to stand on.

Which means the lining-up, the size of the numbers, and whether a chord has a floor under it are not three separate facts. They are one fact, described three ways.

Nobody invented the numbers

You might reasonably suspect that six and five got chosen by some committee. They didn’t, and this is the good part.

Pluck a guitar string. The string is not vibrating at one speed. It’s vibrating at its speed, and at twice that speed, and three times, and four, and five, and six — all at the same time, getting quieter as they go up. Every one of them is happening in the same string at the same moment.

That stack of speeds is why a violin and a clarinet playing the same note don’t sound the same. They’re the same note with different amounts of each ingredient.

Which means six and five were never music theory. They’re two ingredients you’ll find inside a single plucked string, held up next to each other. The whole numbers were already there. Somebody just noticed.

A keyboard is a machine for multiplying

Here’s a thing about a keyboard that nobody mentions.

Go up four keys. Then go up three more. You’re at seven. That’s just adding: 4 + 3.

But in terms of what’s happening to the speed, four keys up is multiplying by 5/4, and three more is multiplying by 6/5. Multiply those together — 5/4 × 6/5 — and the fives cancel and you get 6/4, which is 3/2.

And 3/2 is exactly what sits seven keys up.

So: you added the positions and it multiplied the fractions. Every time. Walking up a keyboard is doing multiplication with your hand, and it works no matter which two you pick. Multiplying fractions is annoying. Counting to seven isn’t. The keyboard does the annoying part.

Where the colours come from

Take any of these fractions and break the top and bottom into the smallest pieces they’ll go into. 6/5 comes apart into 2 × 3, over 5.

The 2s aren’t very interesting — doubling a speed just moves you up an octave, which your ear treats as almost the same note. Throw them out. What’s left is the 3s and the 5s, and those are the parts that give an interval its flavour. Some intervals are made of 3s. Some are made of 5s. Six-against-five is made of one of each, which is why it sounds like two things at once, because it is.

That’s what the colours are doing. Not decoration. The 3s and the 5s.

One place where it doesn’t quite work, which is the best part

Three keys up on a real keyboard isn’t 6/5. It’s very slightly less. Six-fifths is 1.2 exactly. The keyboard gives you 1.1892.

That’s about one per cent off, which sounds like nothing, and isn’t. Play both at once and you can hear a slow throb, a wobble, like the sound is breathing. That throb is the size of the mistake, out loud.

Somebody decided, a few hundred years ago, that it was better to be slightly wrong everywhere than perfectly right in one place and badly wrong everywhere else. That decision is sitting inside every piano you have ever heard. You can hear the seam if you know where to listen.

And one that’s simply broken

Go up six keys — exactly halfway up the octave, the middle of the middle.

There’s no good fraction for it. Not 3/2, not 5/4, not 6/5, not anything small. Every candidate is either far away or made of unpleasantly large numbers. The arithmetic has nothing to offer.

You can hear that too. Six keys up is the interval that sounds like a question nobody answered. It’s the one they used to call the devil’s interval, mostly because it wouldn’t sit still.

The interval that sounds unsettled is the one the numbers can’t name. That’s not a coincidence, and it’s not a flaw in the keyboard. It’s the keyboard telling you the truth.
