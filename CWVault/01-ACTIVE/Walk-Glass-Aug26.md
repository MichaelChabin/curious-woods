# Glass — the walk

Ruled before the walk began (Michael, 26 Aug): **Glass is a plane she builds
on, not a table she looks at.** The founding question is what can be
discovered about spacetime by exploring a thin slice; a coordinate plane was
built; the work now is what can be done with it. The table, if it appears, is
a viewing.

Standing rule found at step 1: **the number is the shape; the numeral is its
name.** Names are small and earned; shapes carry magnitude (4 and 7 are the
same size as digits and nearly a factor of two apart as strips — Michael).

## Step 1 — before she touches anything

The plane as Geometry draws it: origin, the ambient lattice with its ladder
labels, positive in all four directions, opened so the north-east quadrant
fills the glass. On it, **one white unit tile at the origin** — the 1.

- The 1 is the second named location, not a table entry. 0 is the origin;
  1 is the unit tile standing on it. Every number she builds is that tile
  repeated. It is the first rectangle, the one with nothing chosen yet, and
  it has no sections because it is what sections are made of. [C, from M's
  Euclid argument]
- Primes come for free: 7 is the 7×1 strip, corner at (7,1). The axis strips
  are where primes live because a prime's only rectangle is a strip. [M]
- No numeral on the tile. The lattice's 1 already names that line; the tile
  is the thing named. [C, unobjected]
- **One tile, north-east only.** Once there is one anywhere the others can be
  constructed, and four would be messy, messier still when negatives arrive.
  [M]
- Nothing else: no chips, no cell numbers, no coloured panes. Membrane holds
  one word.

*Held for later, not decided:* zero on this plane is a place (the origin,
where you stand); 1 is a thing (what you hold). Zero-as-number arrives with
the four directions; zero-as-digit is base ten, T8. Three zeros the
traditional treatment blurs into one word. Michael is deliberately
non-traditional about 0 and this is the frame to return to.

## Step 2 — her first touch: Multiply

**She touches the 1 and drags it.** Right along the axis and the tile
stretches, tiles laid down one by one, the number reading live; up into the
field and the strip grows into a rectangle. Every number is the unit
repeated, and she does the repeating with her hand. Michael: "if she touches
one and drags, she is multiplying." The bar of the 24 Aug spec is gone — the
strip's leading edge is the bar. The first-number-is-x rule dissolves: width
came first because that is the way the 1 stretched. [C proposed, M adopted]

- Two rectangles follow her finger: the one she is drawing and its twin
  reflected across the diagonal. On the diagonal they coincide — squares are
  their own twin. [M]
- Release leaves the area in place. Rectangles **persist** — this answers the
  24 Aug spec's biggest open question. [M]
- Touch on the tile builds; touch anywhere else pans. A tap on the 1 is a
  drag of zero length: nothing happens, which is the honest answer to 1 × 1.
  [C, unobjected]
- Products are **not shown on the grid** in Multiply. [M]
- Cost accepted: the same-axis case (6 and 7 both from x, the pronic
  doorway) is lost with the bar. [C, noted]

**Clean slate, the membrane (M):** the grid and axes are always visible.
Gone: Fill, Clear, Show/Hide numbers, Hide map, grid follows zoom, axes
shown. The left panel holds *The Curious Woods* at the top and, below it,
one list: Multiply · Divide · Make a square · Primes · Properties ·
Fill/Clear. Each opens a panel — text and a few controls — movable,
resizable, closable, opening **below the list, in the membrane**, not over
the map. (Whether opening a panel changes what the drag does, or the drag
always multiplies and the panel only explains, is open — see below; it is
the modes question in a new costume and the Divide step will answer it.)

**Multiply panel, M's draft (voice pass applies):**

> Touch the number 1 and drag up or right or both. Two rectangles will
> follow your finger across the space. Those rectangles are identical,
> except that one is rotated compared to the other.
> When you know the height and width of a rectangle, multiplication gives
> you the area. Lift your finger to leave the area in place. Tap an empty
> space to undo.

One control at the foot: **Show/Hide Duplicates** — hides the twin.

*Open at this step (raised 26 Aug, awaiting M):*
- **What remains after release** — the whole white rectangle, or its
  outline with the corner cell filled as "the tile marking the grid"?
  Rectangles from the origin nest, so ten of them as solid white are a
  staircase; ten corner tiles with faint outlines are a map she built.
- **"Replacing an existing tile can make it disappear"** — the same
  rectangle drawn again toggles it off? Or a new one landing on an old one
  replaces it?
- **"Tap an empty space to undo"** — a hidden gesture, against the standard
  (controls are words), and it spends the tap that the asking gesture
  (tap-a-spot) will want. Proposed: Undo is a word; tap-on-a-spot is reserved.
- **"Touch the number 1"** — the tile carries no numeral (step 1). Either the
  copy says *the white square* or the tile earns its 1.
- Panel copy explains before she acts ("multiplication gives you the area").
  The 24 Aug story says the opposite in the right voice: *you didn't multiply
  anything; the multiplying was the shape all along.* Voice pass.

### Step 2 settled (26 Aug, second pass)

- **Release leaves only the tile** — the far corner cell — two of them when
  the twin is active. The map of products is a thing she is building. [M]
- **Undo is a word in the panel.** Tap-empty-to-undo was an homage to
  Geometry and does not belong here; the tap stays reserved for asking. [M]
- **The 1 tile shows its numeral, black** (contrast against the white
  glass). [M — reverses step 1's no-numeral note. Consequence adopted: since
  products are not shown in Multiply, the 1 is the only numeral written on
  the plane; everything else is shape.]
- **During the drag** the rectangles are the 1's glass stretched: same
  outline, transparent fill. [M] Proposed: the twin rides slightly fainter,
  so which one she is making is never ambiguous; both snap to their corner
  tiles on release. Show/Hide Duplicates governs the twin's tiles too. [C,
  awaiting M]

### Tile colour — open, M asks for thoughts

Options M named: darken multiples of 5; child-chosen highlights for
multiples of 2, 3, 5; prime colour slices. C's recommendation, standing on
the 24 Aug shelf ruling (*what looked like display options are catalog
tools*) and the colour ruling (*within one viewing colour means one declared
thing*):

**In Multiply, the tile is unity glass — colour says nothing but "built by
her".** Same white as the 1, a shade apart from parchment so it reads as
made. Colouring multiples of k is T1, an act; prime slices are the Primes
step's payoff. If Multiply pre-colours, Primes has nothing to reveal. The
satisfying arc is that the same tiles transform as she asks questions: she
builds white; Primes presses 2 and the tiles containing 2 take blue;
Properties re-colours them again. Colour arrives as the answer to a question
she asked, never as decoration. [C, awaiting M]

### Resolution — the question that stopped the 24 Aug build

M asks: should there be a resolution control? C's recommendation: **no
control in Multiply — resolution is zoom, shown by the lattice, and her
finger snaps to the lattice the plane is currently drawing.** The vault
already owns every piece of this answer:

- The 13 Aug ruling: Multiplication's grid follows zoom, no toggle.
- The Divide spec: fractional divisors unlock by zooming until whole units
  are wide on screen — finer places arrive when she goes looking, never by
  hand-tremor.
- Make This Square: the tool inherits its grain from the map (T8), not from
  a special case.
- The pixel floor speaks the end of refinement.
- The readout never speaks more precision than the act showed (13 Aug §6).

So: at the greeting zoom she snaps to integers. Zoom in and the lattice
refines to tenths; her finger follows; 6.3 × 2 becomes buildable and its
tile reads 12.6, exact. The 1 is still the thing repeated — pieces of the
unit arrive by zoom, Euclid's unit sliced, not replaced. A *declared* grain
control — "what is a cell worth" — is T8, a tool with its own payoff, and
must not sneak into Multiply as a settings knob (the shelf ruling: what
looks like a setting is a tool). [C, awaiting M]

## Step 3 — Divide (opened early, 26 Aug)

M, working through 5 ÷ 7: the child chooses fractions, decimals, or
remainder (per Divide-Spec-Aug23). Decimals stop at pixel limits — and that
means **tiles can be placed off the grid, which is probably exactly right**.
[M]

C, drawing the consequence: division is what puts tiles *between* the
lines. Multiplication can only mark lattice crossings; divide 5 by 7 and the
stack of width 7 closes at height 5/7, so the tile lands at (7, 5/7) — the
first number she meets that lives off the grid. The rationals arrive as
places between the lines, made by her. And every division of the same N
lands on N's hyperbola: divide 5 by 2, 3, 4, 6, 7 and the tiles trace the
curve through (1,5) and (5,1) — T3's constant-area curve accumulates for
free, before any corner-drag tool exists. The pixel floor is zoom-dependent,
consistent with resolution-is-zoom: decimals stop where *her current zoom*
can no longer show a slice, and zooming in buys another digit.

*Open:* what the off-grid tile's numeral reads. (7, 5/7) is one place with
two names — 5/7 exact by the fraction route, ≈0.71 by the decimal route.
Does the name follow the route she took? Same spot divided both ways: one
tile, two names, or the second name joining the first?

### Step 3, M's Divide draft (26 Aug, verbatim in essentials)

- In Divide, **every grid square is marked with its number** — the map is
  the number-picker. (A deliberate per-operation exception to the empty
  grid; "products may or may not be shown depending on the operation" was
  declared at step 2.) She picks N; that many overlapping unit tiles appear
  above-right; N becomes the numerator of a **properly stacked fraction**
  (never slashes); the second touch sets the width, which becomes the
  denominator.
- Whole-number case: tiles reassemble row by row; the readout walks
  12/4 → 1 + 8/4 → 2 + 4/4 → 3; the tile is placed as in multiplication.
- Otherwise the stack proceeds per the remainder handling (fractions /
  decimals / remainders). Non-terminating decimals stop when a slice is
  smaller than a pixel; = becomes ~ with … after the number.
- The tile is placed where its value implies, on or off the grid.
- Panel: pick a number → cursor becomes the vertical bar → set width →
  lift to mark. Controls: Fractions · Decimals · Remainders, and Undo.
- Route naming: M ruled the tile keeps the name of the route it was made
  by, **even if tiles get stacked** at one spot.

### The step's open question — what a tile's numeral means

Conflict now on the table. Multiply's tile reads the **area** — the place's
number: (4,3) reads 12. If Divide's tile reads the **answer** in route
notation (3, or 5/7), the same place can wear numbers that disagree, and
place-is-the-number breaks; stacking makes it visible.

C's proposed resolution, offered 26 Aug: **tiles always read the place;
answers are heights, and heights live on the axis.** 5 ÷ 7 leaves the tile
"5" at (7, 5/7) — area conserved, division just reshaped it — and writes
**5/7 on the y axis at the height where the stack closed**, in the notation
of the route (5/7 by fractions, ≈0.714 by decimals). Division then
populates the number line with the numbers she has made — the axis
densifies with her fractions — and stacking becomes route-names collecting
at one axis height. This also meets the fraction-triangle plan head-on: the
height at x = 1 of the ray through (7,5) *is* 5/7, so the axis marks and
the triangle tool will later be the same object seen twice. [Awaiting M]

## Step 4 — Make a square (opened 26 Aug)

Noted ahead of M's draft, both consequences of the settled conventions,
neither invented:

- **Every square's tile lands on the diagonal.** The corner of an s × s
  square is (s, s), so Make a square populates the map's crease: the tile
  for 18 sits at (√18, √18) reading 18, off-grid, on the fold. Squares are
  visibly the numbers whose home is the crease — the flip that "goes
  nowhere" said this once; now the tiles say it permanently.
- **The axis mark that never closes.** The answer is a side, so it goes to
  the axis like Divide's heights. Divide's marks always have an exact name
  by some route (5/7). Make a square's mark for 18 has none — every route
  ends in ≈ and …, and refinement approaches a place it never names
  exactly. The rational/irrational difference lives on the axis itself:
  some heights have exact names, some don't — beside the two-words-where-
  Divide-has-three gap already in the spec.

### Step 4, continued — what Make a square leaves behind

M's two candidates: keep the square bodies, nested biggest-on-bottom so all
show; or mark the diagonal with a labelled mark (17) that, when tapped,
re-shows the square with its sides (~4.12…).

C's recommendation: **the mark, not the body** — Multiply already settled
this shape when the nested-slab staircase was rejected in favour of the
corner tile. The mark at (√17, √17) reads 17, tiles-read-the-place holding.
And M's tap-to-reshow generalises into the rule the walk has been circling:

**The mark is the memory; tapping a mark replays its making.** Tap a
Multiply tile → its rectangle (and twin) re-show with side numerals. Tap a
Divide mark on the axis → the stack re-shows at its width. Tap a diagonal
mark → the square re-shows, sides named ~4.12…. One at a time, never
everything at once. This is the asking gesture (the tap held in reserve
since step 2) and the operation log meeting: asking = replaying one entry.
The old "tap-a-number branch — undesigned, half the tool" dissolves into it.
[C, awaiting M]

- Crowding near the diagonal (√17 to √18 is 0.12) is solved by
  resolution-is-zoom — zoom in and the marks separate. What a mark looks
  like off-grid (unit tile scaled to grain? small pane?) is a **bench
  question**, not an argument.

## Step 5 — Primes (resumed 27 Aug, M at the Subaru dealer)

C's framing question — what does "let a prime into the world" act on, the
table assembling or only her tiles — met M's list coming the other way.
Given prime chips, M names four things:

1. Tap primes in any order, then **=**, to build a number.
2. Tap a prime to see all its multiples.
3. Tap **fill** to fill the plane with products.
4. Be given a target; tap primes to factor it; on the right combination a
   ding, and a new number appears.

C's sort, by M's own gates (one sentence per gesture; four meanings for one
tap is the mode bar in miniature):

- **#1 is the Primes panel.** Chips plus a running stacked product —
  1 × 2 × 2 × 3 = 12 building live — and **= is the commit gesture**, the
  chip world's "lift your finger": the assembled number takes its place on
  the map as a tile, slices showing what it is made of. Before =, the
  product is a ghost. (This revises 24 Aug's "a press means let this prime
  into the world" — the press now means *multiply it in*, M's #1. The
  sweep survives inside #3.)
- **#2 is T1** — light every cell that… — and belongs to Properties, where
  the lens family lives. Same chips, different room.
- **#3 is the table viewing** — the Fill/Clear word already on the membrane
  list. Filled with everything, or with only what the let-in primes can
  build, which is where the 24 Aug sweep and asymmetry exhibit live on.
- **#4 reshaped:** no given target, no ding. She picks the target herself —
  taps any number on the map — and presses chips; the running product
  stands beside the target and the tile's slices complete when the
  factorisation does. The picture announces the equality; nothing else
  does. As drafted, #4 carries two school tells (asks a question it knows
  the answer to; congratulates) and contradicts the standard's "labs never
  quiz". The 24 Aug order also stands: the endless workshop ships before
  any puzzle form. [C, awaiting M]

### Step 5, continued — adding primes (27 Aug)

M: give her the option of adding primes — she might actually use 11, but
the point is **the sense that more primes exist**. (This is the 24 Aug open
item — "a child could reasonably conclude from four buttons that someone
has the complete list" — coming due.)

C's two candidate mechanisms, one question pending:

- **The dispenser:** the chip row always ends with a blank chip and an
  ellipsis. Tap the blank and 11 arrives; tap again, 13. However many she
  adds, the blank and the ellipsis remain — the row is never complete, and
  the ellipsis is the endlessness statement. Simple, always available,
  never exhausts.
- **The mint:** primes are *found*, not dispensed. In fill-with-let-in-
  primes the unreachable numbers stand as holes; the factoring game's
  slices refuse to close on 22 with only 2, 3, 5, 7 in the world — "you
  found a hole." A number she isolates that her chips cannot build (11 by
  dividing 22 by 2, or a hole on the axis strip) offers itself as a chip.
  Her discoveries become tools. Slower, and the endlessness arrives as
  experience rather than punctuation.

They compose: the blank chip ships (the workshop must not dead-end), the
mint arrives with the factoring game. [C, awaiting M]

### Step 5, continued — the colour floor (27 Aug)

M adopts the blank chip + ellipsis. On late primes: wants distinguishability
even on a large map, and floats saying to the child: *we can't colour all
the primes — there are not enough colours.*

C: the sentence is true, honest, and belongs to a family the lab already
has. Distinguishable colours are finite — a dozen at generous sizes, fewer
in thin slices, fewer again for the colourblind workshop — and primes are
not. That is a **colour floor**, sibling to the pixel floor: the interface
admitting its finiteness against a mathematics that continues. Said flatly
as fact (never apology), it is a stealth encounter with infinity, same
shape as the pixel floor's "too small to see."

Proposed resolution, three parts:
- **Colours as far as the workshop honestly goes** — the declared set,
  maximally distinct for the workhorses (2 is in half of everything; the
  frequent primes collide in adjacent slices constantly; the rare ones
  almost never meet).
- **Clear glass beyond, declared once in the child's hearing** — with the
  sentence spoken, clear glass stops being a quiet omission and becomes a
  named thing: *a prime we didn't colour*. Two clear slices in one tile are
  two different uncoloured primes; the ambiguity is admitted, not hidden.
- **The tap always knows.** Colour is recognition at a glance; identity at
  the exact level is the replay rule — tap the tile and the readout names
  1 × 11 × 13 = 143. Nothing depends on colour alone (the accessibility
  ruling kept intact).

Note against M's "distinguishable even on a large map": zoomed out, tiles
shrink and the distinguishable set *shrinks with them* — the want and
perception collide exactly there; the floor sentence and the tap are the
honest resolution. **Bench question, owed:** how many slice-colours
survive at realistic tile sizes, per workshop, the colourblind workshop
included — this also bounds how far the declared set can extend past six.
Voice pass owns the sentence's wording.

### Step 7, continued — merging Squares into Gaps (27 Aug)

M proposes merging Squares and Same gap: gap 0 is the crease, gap 1 the
pronics, and the pattern should be obvious. Asks how far gaps stay

Computed, not remembered:
```
gap 1:  2, 6, 12, 20, 30        sides always coprime (gcd divides the gap)
gap 2:  3, 8, 15, 24, 35        = 4−1, 9−1, 16−1, 25−1, 36−1
gap 4:  5, 12, 21, 32, 45       = 9−4, 16−4, 25−4, 36−4, 49−4
gap 6:  7, 16, 27, 40, 55       = 16−9, 25−9, 36−9, 49−9, 64−9
gap 12: 13, 28, 45, 64, 85, 108 = 49−36, 64−36, 81−36, 100−36, 121−36, 144−36
```
The law is uniform: **every even-gap rectangle is a square missing a
square bite of side gap/2** — n(n+2k) = (n+k)² − k², and the rearrangement
is geometric (slide the overhang; T4's gnomon). Gap 12's bite is 6×6,
forever. Bonuses on gap 12: 64 sits on it (4×16 — a square number with a
second home twelve apart); every gcd of sides divides 12, so the line's
rectangles simplify only by 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12. Gap 1 is the all-coprime
line (the orchard's visible trees, every one). Difference-of-squares
factoring, a² − b² = (a+b)(a−b), is this lens read backwards. Interest
does not run out; the rule survives every change of gap, which is the
shape M's tools keep finding.

C's merged-lens proposal: **Gaps** opens lighting the crease — *gap 0:
the squares* — with a stepper (or drag) walking the gap upward: with
duplicates visible each gap is a mirrored pair of lines that sweep outward
from the crease like a compass opening. Tapping any cell lights its gap
directly. Squares leaves the top-level list; the list becomes Primes ·
Evens · Count by… · All the ___s · Same fence · Gaps · More… The button
note carries gap 0 = squares, gap 1 = neighbours; the bite law is left to
be found. [Awaiting M]

### Step 7, continued — the lens notes (27 Aug, C draft, voice gate applies)

M's register ruling: explanations are critical; not "kid friendly" —
**serious and seriously simple, almost zen. Definitions can include
questions.** Many properties will get their own story, which calls this
tool. And a number's tap-card should list the properties it has (ties to
the tap-replay card: the replay plus its property lines).

Draft notes, one per button — definitions only; what the light will show
is never given away:

- **Primes** — A prime cannot be made by multiplying smaller numbers.
  7 is one. Where can numbers like that live?
- **Evens** — Every second number is even. That is half of everything.
  What does half the map look like?
- **Count by …** — Pick a number and count by it. 3, 6, 9, and on
  forever. The lights are the counting.
- **All the ___s** — Pick a number and light every place it lives. Some
  numbers live in two places. Some live in eight. Count them.
- **Homes** — How many places a number lives is how many homes it has.
  Pick two homes, or three, or four. Which numbers have exactly two?
- **Same fence** — Every rectangle on this line uses the same length of
  fence. Their insides differ. Which one holds the most?
- **Gaps** — The gap is how far apart a rectangle's two sides are. A
  square's sides are the same: no gap. Step the gap wider and see who
  lives one apart. Two apart. Twelve.
- **One colour** — Some numbers are made of one prime used again and
  again. 4 is 2 × 2. 8 is 2 × 2 × 2. One colour, deeper each time.
- **More …** — There are more properties than buttons. There always
  will be.
