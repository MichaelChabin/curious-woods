# The stories — first set

Written together, in one voice, as agreed. Each is about the tool: authored
once, always true, requested rather than pushed.

Every number in these has been computed.

---

## Multiply — the story

Pick a spot on the edge and slide. A line follows your finger, and a number
goes with it — 5, 6, 7. Stop wherever you like.

Now move out onto the plane. A rectangle grows behind you, as wide as the line
you left and as tall as you've gone. Let go.

The rectangle fills in, one row at a time, and counts itself as it goes. That's
the number that lives at that spot.

Here's the part worth sitting with. You didn't multiply anything. You drew a
shape and then found out how much was inside it. The multiplying was the shape
all along.

Every number on this plane got there the same way. 12 is at the spot where a
3-wide, 4-tall rectangle lands. It's also at the spot where a 4-wide, 3-tall
one lands, which is a different spot and exactly the same amount of tile. You
can watch that if you want — the rectangle will turn over and walk across to
its other home.

Some numbers have a lot of homes. 24 has eight. Some have exactly two, and
those turn out to be the interesting ones, and there's more to say about them
later.

And the ones sitting on the diagonal have only one home, because turning them
over doesn't take them anywhere.

## Divide — the story

Pick a number. It turns into a rectangle, and then falls apart into square
tiles — that many of them, sitting there.

Now slide the bar. It's asking how wide you want to stack them. The number
under your finger changes as you go: 3, 4, 5. Pick one.

The tiles march over and stack up. Rows fill in one at a time, and a number
counts them as they land. When the tiles run out, however tall the stack got is
your answer.

Sometimes it comes out flat and finished. 20 tiles stacked 4 wide makes 5 rows
exactly, nothing left over.

Sometimes it doesn't. 20 tiles stacked 3 wide gives you 6 rows and 2 tiles
standing there with nowhere to go.

You get to decide what happens to those two.

You can leave them. Six rows and two tiles. That's true, and it's finished.

You can cut them into thirds — three, because there are three stacks waiting —
and every stack takes two slices. Now the top is flat. Six and two thirds.

Or you can cut them into tenths, which is what most people mean when they say
"the answer". The odd thing about tenths is that nothing asked for them.
Nothing about three stacks suggests cutting into ten. Sometimes it works out
flat anyway. Sometimes you can keep slicing forever and never get a flat top.

## Make a Square — the story

Pick a number. Say 18. It comes apart into 18 tiles.

One tile goes to the corner. Then three more wrap around it in an L, and now
it's a 2 by 2 square. Then five more wrap around that, and it's 3 by 3.

1, 3, 5, 7. You can probably see where this is going, and you're right. It
keeps working, and it keeps working for any number you pick.

Seven more gets you to 4 by 4. That's used up 16 tiles, and you have 2 left.

Stop and look at that. Four is too small. Five is too big — five would need 25.
There is no whole number that squares to 18. Not a hard one, not an ugly one.
There isn't one.

You can cut the last two tiles up if you want. Each one slices into a hundred
smaller ones, and the wrapping starts again: 81, then 83. Still odd numbers.
Still going up by two. The rule didn't care that the tiles got smaller.

That gets you to 4.2, with 36 small tiles left over.

Do it again and you're at 4.24. Again and it's 4.242. The tiles get too small
to see long before the number decides to stop — and it never does decide to
stop.

So the side of that square gets written √18, which means exactly the length
you've been chasing. It's about 4.24.

## Notes for Michael

**Gate 3 is a comprehension gate, not only a voice gate.** If any of these
loses you, the tool is not ready and it does not matter whose fault that is.

Three things to check specifically:

- **Multiply** ends by pointing at primes without naming them — "some have
  exactly two, and those turn out to be the interesting ones". Deliberate hook,
  no explanation. Check that it reads as an invitation rather than a withheld
  answer.
- **Divide** treats decimals as the strange option rather than the grown-up
  one, which is the point of the whole design but is the opposite of what most
  adults expect. Worth reading with that in mind.
- **Make a Square** asks her to stop and look at the leftover two tiles. That
  is the moment the tool exists for. If the paragraph rushes past it, the
  paragraph is wrong.

**Verified:** 24 has eight divisors, so eight cells. 20 ÷ 3 = 6 remainder 2,
and 6 + 2/3. 18: rings of 1, 3, 5, 7 → side 4, 2 left; then 81, 83 → 4.2, 36
left; then 4.24; √18 = 4.242641.
