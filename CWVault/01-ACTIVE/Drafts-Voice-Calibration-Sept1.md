### Mary Shelley Begins Frankenstein

You probably know the story of Frankenstein. It is about a student who stitched a body together out of parts stolen from a graveyard. When it was finished, he used electricity, in some mysterious way, to bring his creation to life.

The book has been a classic since Mary Shelley published it anonymously in 1818.

She began the story two years earlier, when she was 18 years old. That was in June of 1816, "the year without a summer," a time as frightening as Frankenstein's monster.

That winter was bitter, among the coldest in a century, but even when it passed, the cold and fog and rain never stopped. Snow fell in June, and frosts in July and August destroyed crops as soon as they were replanted. Famine and epidemics were widespread. The sun itself seemed to dim, and when it set, the colors were like fire.

There was a reason for all of it, though no one knew it at the time. The strange weather was caused by a volcano on the other side of the world.

### Mary's Book

In England the summer of 1816 was so cold and wet that Mary and her stepsister Claire joined the poets Byron and Shelley, and Byron's young doctor John Polidori, at a villa above Lake Geneva in Switzerland.

Even there they found the weather so dismal that they decided to entertain each other by writing ghost stories. Byron wrote the poem "Darkness," in which the sun mysteriously stops shining. Polidori wrote *The Vampyre*. Until then, vampires were thought to be peasants who rose from the dead; Polidori's vampire, Lord Ruthven, was rich, powerful, and cruel. According to Polidori, he was modeled on Byron.

For all that, it is Mary's contribution that we remember. Her story was about a student named Victor Frankenstein, obsessed with uncovering the secret of life itself. That obsession led him to assemble a collection of body parts, stitch them together, and use electricity to bring it all to life. At the time, the idea seemed almost plausible.

But Mary's story is not about a monster. The being Victor assembled *looked* like a monster, and terrified those who saw him so badly that they attacked him and drove him away. Even Victor abandoned him. He was human, though, and like any human, that treatment made him angry and bitter.

Mary wrote the book because, at eighteen, she saw something the brilliant men around her largely didn't: creation entails responsibility. Victor's great failure isn't that he discovers how to create life. It's that the instant he sees the being he has created, he rejects it. Mary gives the creature a voice, and lets him tell us what the world looks like to an outsider who is abandoned by the very person who made him — merely to satisfy his own curiosity.

Frankenstein is worth reading.

### Mary Shelley

Mary Shelley was a remarkable woman. She was also tough.

She started writing Frankenstein when she was 18. Over the next six years she endured poverty and social ostracism. Three of her children died, her half-sister committed suicide, and her husband drowned. By the time she was 24, Mary Shelley was a widow with a young son, supporting herself as a writer.

In all, she wrote seven novels, numerous short stories, essays, travel writing, and biographies. She also edited and promoted her husband Percy's work after his death.

Writing may have come naturally to her. Her father was a philosopher, a novelist, and a radical who argued that inherited wealth and power undermined society. Her mother was Mary Wollstonecraft, author of *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* — a book still in print, still a classic.

Mary did not know her mother. In those days childbirth was dangerous, and Mary Wollstonecraft died eleven days after her daughter was born. Even so, Mary felt close to her. She learned her letters by tracing the words on her mother's gravestone, a place she visited all her life, and she admired her mother's work enormously.

Accounts describe her as reserved, serious, intelligent, and somewhat shy. She could seem cool to people who didn't know her well. But underneath the reserve was an intense intellectual life. She read voraciously, in several languages, kept detailed reading lists, and grew up around some of Britain's most formidable minds. If you met her today, you'd quickly realize you were talking to someone who had read an enormous amount and remembered all of it.

### Electricity in 1816

The world was an interesting place in 1816. Beethoven was writing music. Jane Austen was writing novels. The Brothers Grimm were collecting fairy tales, and Turner was painting fiery sunsets. Within a year, someone would invent the first bicycle. It had no pedals, but it was faster than walking.

The world was mysterious in 1816. Oxygen was a new idea. No one knew what the sun is made of, what causes earthquakes, or what a germ is. Dinosaur fossils had been found, but they baffled everyone. So did meteorites.

But of all the strange and interesting things in Mary's world, none was more mysterious than electricity. No one knew what it was. It fascinated everyone.

Thirty years before Frankenstein, an Italian named Galvani made the leg of a dead frog twitch by touching it with certain metals. He concluded that electricity was the force of life.

When Mary was two, another Italian, named Volta, discovered that a stack of alternating zinc and silver discs, separated by saltwater-soaked cloth, would produce a continuous electric current. He could explain it too, but he was wrong. Still, that stack of metal and wet cloth is considered the first battery.

Eventually, Michael Faraday would know more about electricity than anyone alive. In 1816 Faraday was 24, and he knew how to store electricity, make sparks, produce a steady current with a battery, and even use it to cause chemical reactions.

But that was about all he knew. In 1816, when Mary Shelley began writing Frankenstein, electricity was easily the most mysterious stuff in the world.

### The Eruption of Mt. Tambora *(your opening, finished)*

In April of 1815, in a single eruption, the top third of Mt. Tambora exploded into a jet of fire, smoke, and ash that shot into the atmosphere. It was the dust and gas from that volcano that dimmed the sun and lowered temperatures around the world for more than a year.

The explosion was heard sixteen hundred miles away — about the distance from London to Moscow. Soldiers who heard it thought it was cannon fire and turned out for battle. For two days, islands hundreds of miles from the mountain sat in total darkness.

The finest ash and gas rose higher than any storm, above the weather itself, where no rain could wash it out. Up there, the winds carried it around the whole Earth, and it stayed for more than a year — a thin haze, too thin to see from the ground, just thick enough to dim the sun everywhere.

No eruption in recorded history has been larger. And here is the strange part: almost nobody on Earth connected it to the terrible year that followed. The news of the eruption reached Europe eventually, months late. The cold arrived the next summer. Nobody put the two together. How could they? The idea that a mountain in Indonesia could freeze a garden in Vermont was not just unknown — it was unthinkable.

It took more than a hundred and fifty years, and evidence buried a mile deep in the ice of Greenland, to prove what had happened. But that is another story.

## C. Ahmes the Scribe *(new, in your register — a first draft for your red pen)*

**ahmes** — image: the Rhind Papyrus itself, British Museum.
*Hook:* The oldest math book in the world begins by promising to teach you every secret. Its author signed his name.

### The story

More than three thousand years ago, in Egypt, a scribe named Ahmes sat down with ink and a long roll of paper made from reeds, and copied out a book of mathematics. The book was already three hundred years old when he copied it. He tells us so himself, right at the start — and then he makes a promise. This book, he writes, is the entrance into the knowledge of all existing things, and all obscure secrets.

Ahmes is the earliest person in the history of mathematics whose name we know. Not a king. A working scribe, proud enough of his work to sign it.

His book holds eighty-four problems: how to divide loaves of bread among ten men, how to measure a field, how to figure the strength of beer. But the best thing in it is how Ahmes multiplies. He has no times tables. He never learned them. Nobody in Egypt did.

Instead, Ahmes doubles.

Say he needs 13 × 12. He writes two columns. The left column starts at 1 and doubles: 1, 2, 4, 8. The right column starts at 12 and doubles: 12, 24, 48, 96. Then he looks at the left column and finds the rows that add up to 13. That's 8 + 4 + 1. So he adds up those same rows on the right: 96 + 48 + 12. That's 156. And 13 × 12 is 156.

It always works. Any two numbers. All you ever need to know is how to double, and how to add.

Here is the reason it always works, and it is worth sitting with: every number — every single one — can be built by adding numbers from the doubling row: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32. Thirteen is 8 + 4 + 1. Twenty-nine is 16 + 8 + 4 + 1. Try to find a number that can't be built this way. There isn't one, and there's exactly one way to build each.

One more thing. The computer you are reading this on cannot really multiply either. Deep down, it knows how to double, and how to add, and that is all. It multiplies the way Ahmes did, three thousand years ago, on paper made of reeds.

## D. The Numbers That Won't Break *(new — primes)*

**primes** — image: a long thin strip of tiles, one tile wide.
*Hook:* Some numbers break into pieces. Some refuse. Everything is built out of the ones that refuse.

### The story

Take 24 tiles and break them into equal piles. You have choices: two piles of 12, or three piles of 8, or four piles of 6. Now keep going. Break the 12s, break the 8s, break the 6s, and don't stop until nothing will break anymore.

Something odd happens. It doesn't matter which way you start. Two 12s or three 8s or four 6s — when nothing will break any further, you are always holding the same pieces: three 2s and a 3. Every time. Try to end up with different pieces. You can't.

Now try to break 7. Two piles of three and a half? Those aren't equal piles of tiles. Three piles? Doesn't work either. Nothing works. Seven refuses to break. On the number map you've seen this already: most numbers have several homes, but 7 has only two, and both of them are strips — 1 wide and 7 long. A number that refuses to break is called a prime.

Here is the part worth sitting with. The pieces you're holding when nothing will break anymore — they are always primes. So every number is really a stack of primes multiplied together: 24 is 2 × 2 × 2 × 3. And each number gets exactly one stack. Not one of several. One. The number 720,720 breaks into its own pile of primes and no other, and it doesn't matter who does the breaking, or in what order, or in what century.

Primes are the atoms of numbers. Everything else is built from them, the way every material in the world is built from atoms. Chemists have a hundred or so atoms to work with and they run out. Primes never run out — but that is another story, and it's a good one.
