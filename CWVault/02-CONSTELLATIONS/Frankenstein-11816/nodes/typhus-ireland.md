## Panel

In Ireland the wet cold summer of 1816 rotted the potatoes in the ground. By 1817
people were on the roads looking for food, sleeping crowded in whatever shelter they
found, and typhus — a disease carried by body lice — went with them. It ran until 1819.
Estimates of the dead run to tens of thousands and are disputed; a doctor in Dublin
guessed that a million and a half people had been ill.

## More

Typhus is the disease of crowding and cold: lice spread it, and lice spread where
people cannot wash or change clothes and huddle together. Every famine in Europe
before the twentieth century carried it. What makes 1817 belong to this cluster is
that the chain is unusually clear — a volcano, a haze, a wet summer, a failed crop,
migration, a fever — and that nobody in it could see more than one link at a time.

This is also the cleanest door into the disease stories. A child who follows the
epidemics thread from here reaches cholera in the 1830s and John Snow's map, which is
where disease becomes a counting problem (i11): the pump on Broad Street was found by
plotting deaths on a street plan.

The numbers are honestly uncertain and should stay that way on the timeline: a
`disputed` precision and a soft-edged bar.
