## Panel

Volcanic haze scatters light, and for two or three years after Tambora the sunsets
across Europe were long, vivid and oddly coloured. J. M. W. Turner was then the most
attentive painter of skies alive. In 2007 a group of atmospheric scientists measured
the red-to-green ratio in hundreds of sunset paintings from 1500 to 1900 and found the
skies went redder in the years after big eruptions. Turner's did.

## More

This is a contested link, and it is in the cluster because it is contested. The study
(Zerefos and colleagues) treats old paintings as instruments — a record of the
atmosphere made by people who did not know they were recording it. Critics reply that
painters paint what they like, that Turner was getting redder anyway, and that a
handful of canvases is thin evidence. Both sides are right that the sunsets were
strange; diarists say so.

For the platform the interest is the *method*: taking something not meant as a
measurement and reading a measurement out of it. It rhymes with the ice cores (see
`how-we-know-tambora`) and with the child counting pixels (i11). Turner's skies also
open onto the Perception Lab by the back door — what "redder" means, and how you would
count it.

The paintings themselves are public domain and the Tate has photographed most of them.
