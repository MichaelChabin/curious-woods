## Panel

In the summer of 1837, back a year from the Beagle voyage, Charles Darwin opened
Notebook B and drew a spindly branching tree: one stem, forking and forking, some twigs
ending, some carrying on. Above it he wrote "I think." The drawing says what the
*Origin of Species* would take twenty-two years and five hundred pages to say: species
are related like branches, the living ones are twig-tips, and the dead branches matter
as much as the live ones.

## More

The diagram is a mathematical object — a tree, in the graph-theory sense — and reading
it is a mathematical act. Any two twigs trace back to a shared fork: that is common
ancestry, as a path. A fork is one species becoming two. A branch that stops is an
extinction, and Darwin's sketch is full of stopped branches, which is the honest part;
most branches stop. The single figure in the *Origin* itself, the only illustration
Darwin allowed it, is this sketch grown formal.

The same structure is everywhere once seen: family trees, the folders on a computer,
the repeated forking of the biomorph simulations — which grow by exactly the rule the
sketch proposes, small change plus branching plus pruning. A child who has bred
biomorphs has drawn this diagram many times without knowing it, and the node's job is
the recognition.

His grandfather Erasmus had guessed at the idea in prose two generations earlier —
"one living filament" — with no mechanism and no tree. The sketch is the moment the
guess became a structure that could be checked.
