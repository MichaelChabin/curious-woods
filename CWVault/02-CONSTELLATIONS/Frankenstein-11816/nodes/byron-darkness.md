## Panel

"I had a dream, which was not all a dream. / The bright sun was extinguish'd." Byron
wrote "Darkness" in July 1816 at Diodati, in a summer when the sky was hazed and the
harvests were failing. The poem imagines the last people on a cold earth burning their
houses for light. He did not know about Tambora. He was describing the weather.

## More

The poem is eighty-two lines of blank verse and has no plot beyond the sun failing and
people getting worse. It reads now as the first climate-catastrophe poem, though that is
our category, not his; for Byron it was a dream-vision in the tradition of the last-man
poems then fashionable. What makes it belong in this cluster is the date. The summer's
dimness, the endless rain, and the newspaper reports of famine were all in the room.

It is also a good first piece of dense text for a ten-year-old who is ready (i12): the
sentences are long, the vocabulary is plain, and the images are concrete — a dog
guarding its dead master, two enemies meeting at an altar to scrape at ashes.
