## Panel

In Bologna in the 1780s Luigi Galvani noticed that the leg of a dissected frog twitched
when touched with a scalpel while a nearby machine sparked. He spent a decade on it.
Legs hung on brass hooks from an iron railing kicked with no machine at all. He decided
the animal itself held an electricity — "animal electricity" — that the metals merely
released. He was wrong about that, and the wrongness changed the world.

## More

Galvani published in 1791. His nephew Aldini carried the work to Paris and London;
Alessandro Volta in Pavia read it, repeated it, and disagreed. Volta argued that the
electricity came from the two different metals touching, and the frog was only a very
sensitive detector. The argument between them ran through the 1790s and was, for the
public, the most exciting science of the day: it seemed to be about whether life was
electrical.

Both men were partly right. Volta's answer produced the battery. Galvani's produced, in
the long run, electrophysiology — nerves do carry electrical signals, just not the way
he thought. His name survives in "galvanize," which first meant to shock a corpse into
motion and now means to shock anyone into action.

The plates in his 1791 book, of frog legs hung from railings and laid on tables with
wires, are the images the child should see. They are odd and careful and beautiful.
