## Panel

In 1816 it snowed in New England in June and frosted in every month of the summer. In
Switzerland the harvest failed and people ate moss. Sunsets everywhere were a strange,
long-lasting orange. Farmers, preachers and scientists argued about sunspots, about
lightning rods, about God. The actual cause was a volcano on the other side of the
world, and nobody would connect the two for more than a century.

## More

The haze from Tambora lowered the average temperature of the northern hemisphere by
something under a degree. That sounds like nothing. It was enough to shift the frosts a
few weeks into the growing season at both ends, which is enough to lose a crop. Grain
prices in Europe roughly doubled; there were bread riots in England and France; in
Ireland the failed potato harvest was followed by typhus. Families in Vermont and New
Hampshire gave up and moved west, one of the pushes behind the settlement of Ohio and
Indiana. In Yunnan, in China, a rice famine ran three years.

What is most striking now is how invisible the cause was. Reports of the eruption had
reached London by late 1815, but nothing linked an Indonesian mountain to a Swiss
harvest. The idea that a volcano could change the weather of the whole world was first
argued seriously in the twentieth century, and the ice-core evidence that clinched it
came in the 1980s. The people living through 1816 were living inside a global event
they had no way to see.
