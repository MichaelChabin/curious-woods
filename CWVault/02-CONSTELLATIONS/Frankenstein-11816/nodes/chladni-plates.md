## Panel

Ernst Chladni, a lawyer who preferred acoustics, scattered sand on a brass plate and
drew a violin bow along its edge. The plate sang, and the sand jumped away from the
parts that were moving and gathered on the lines that were still. Each pitch made a
different figure — stars, crosses, rings — repeatable, and unexplained. He published
the figures in 1787. In 1808 he demonstrated them in Paris, and the French Academy
offered a prize to anyone who could explain them mathematically.

## More

The sand is doing measurement. A vibrating plate moves everywhere except along its
nodal lines, and the grains, shaken off the moving parts, collect where the motion is
zero. The pattern *is* the mathematics of the plate made visible — no instrument in
between, just sand finding the quiet. Higher pitches make finer patterns, and the
same plate holds a whole family of figures, one per resonance.

Explaining them was brutally hard. A plate is not a string: it bends in two directions
at once, and its edges are free. The prize had to be extended twice, and the only
person who ever submitted was Sophie Germain (see her node). The full theory took
another generation.

The figures never stopped being useful. Violin makers still tune the carved plates of
instruments by watching the patterns; they appear on the backs of guitars and in the
engineering of loudspeakers and cymbals. This node is the Sound and Perception labs'
front door from the history side: pitch on one axis, geometry appearing out of it.
