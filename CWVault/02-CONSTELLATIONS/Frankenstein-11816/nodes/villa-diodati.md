## Panel

In June 1816 Byron rented a house above Lake Geneva. Shelley, Mary Godwin, Mary's
stepsister Claire, and Byron's young doctor, John Polidori, were in and out of it daily.
The weather was foul — it was the year without a summer — so they read German ghost
stories aloud by candlelight, and Byron said: we will each write one. Mary's became
*Frankenstein*. Polidori's became *The Vampyre*, the first vampire story in English.

## More

The group was not glamorous at the time. Byron had left England in disgrace two months
before. Shelley had abandoned a wife. Mary was eighteen, travelling unmarried with him,
and had already lost a baby. Claire Clairmont was pregnant by Byron, who was tired of
her. Polidori was twenty, clever, and bullied. The talk ran to science: whether the
principle of life could be discovered, whether a corpse could be reanimated, what
Erasmus Darwin was supposed to have done. It is the talk, more than the dare, that
produced the book.

Byron's own story was a fragment he abandoned. Polidori took the fragment's idea — an
aristocratic predator — and finished it; *The Vampyre* was published in 1819 under
Byron's name by mistake, which annoyed both of them. Shelley wrote nothing usable.
Mary, who had been anxious for days about having no idea, was the one who did.

The house is still there, in Cologny. Nothing marks the dare.
