## Panel

On 29 August 1831, in the basement of the Royal Institution, Michael Faraday wound two
coils on an iron ring, connected one to a battery, and saw the needle on the other side
kick — once when he closed the circuit, once when he opened it. Moving magnetism made
electricity. Within weeks he had a copper disc spinning between the poles of a magnet
and a steady current coming off it: the first generator.

## More

Volta had made electricity from chemistry, which is expensive and runs out. Faraday made
it from motion, which can be had from falling water or burning coal for as long as you
like. Every power station since is his disc grown large. The electric motor is the same
discovery run backward, and he had built a crude one ten years earlier.

Faraday was a bookbinder's apprentice who got into science by attending Davy's public
lectures and sending him his notes. He never learned much mathematics, and thought in
pictures — lines of force filling space — that Maxwell later turned into equations.
That way of thinking is one of the platform's own (i03, i01): a field is geometry
before it is algebra.

The telegraph, twelve years on, is the first machine to use electricity to carry
information faster than a horse — which is why this node also sits on the
speed-of-information thread. Tambora's news took months; by 1866 a cable under the
Atlantic carried news in minutes.
