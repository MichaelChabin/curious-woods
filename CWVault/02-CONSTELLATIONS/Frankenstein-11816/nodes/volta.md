## Panel

Volta thought Galvani's frog was a detector, not a source: put two different metals
together with something wet between them and you get a current, frog or no frog. To
prove it he stacked discs of silver and zinc with brine-soaked cardboard between them —
a pile — and in 1800 wrote to the Royal Society that it gave a steady current for as
long as you liked. It was the first battery. Electricity stopped being a spark and became
a supply.

## More

Before the pile, electricity came from friction machines and Leyden jars: a charge, a
crack, and gone. The pile delivered current continuously, which meant it could be used
to *do* things. Within weeks of Volta's letter, Nicholson and Carlisle in London used a
pile to split water into hydrogen and oxygen. Within a few years Humphry Davy was using
a very large one to isolate potassium and sodium. Aldini used piles in his
demonstrations. Faraday's whole career sits downstream.

The argument with Galvani was never quite settled between them and did not need to be.
Volta's account of the metals was right; Galvani's instinct that living tissue is
electrical was also right; each was wrong about the other's evidence. Napoleon made
Volta a count. The unit of electrical pressure, the volt, is his; the word for shocking
a corpse, galvanism, is Galvani's. The names are a fair record of who won which half.
