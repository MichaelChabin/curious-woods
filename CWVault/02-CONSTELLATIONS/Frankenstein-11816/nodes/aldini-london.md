## Panel

On 17 January 1803 Giovanni Aldini, Galvani's nephew, was given the body of a man just
hanged at Newgate for murder. Before an audience of surgeons he applied wires from a
voltaic pile. The jaw quivered, the left eye opened, a hand rose and clenched. Some
present thought the man was about to get up. Mary Godwin was five years old and living
in London. The story was in every paper.

## More

Aldini was touring Europe to defend his uncle's animal electricity against Volta, and he
had already worked with ox heads and dogs. The Newgate demonstration was legal — the
bodies of executed murderers were given to anatomists by statute — and it was, by his
own account, a study of whether electricity might restart a stopped heart, with drowning
victims in mind. That is a real question, and modern defibrillators are its answer. But
what the audience saw, and what the papers printed, was a corpse that moved.

Mary Shelley does not name Aldini anywhere. She names galvanism, and she grew up in the
city where galvanism meant this. The link is a *likely* one: the demonstration was the
most famous of its kind, in her city, in her childhood, and the novel's central image is
a body that moves when it should not. Historians treat it as background rather than
source, which is the honest weight.

This one should be told plainly and not dwelt on. The man had a name — George Forster —
and had done something terrible, and the honest version keeps both facts.
