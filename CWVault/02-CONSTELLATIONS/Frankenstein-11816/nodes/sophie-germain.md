## Panel

In January 1816 — the year of this constellation — the French Academy awarded its
grand prize for the mathematics of Chladni's sand figures. The winner was Sophie
Germain, who as a girl had studied secretly at night after her parents confiscated her
candles, who could not attend the École Polytechnique because she was a woman, and who
had borrowed an enrolled student's name — M. LeBlanc — to submit work and, later, to
write to Gauss himself. It was the first prize of its kind ever won by a woman.

## More

Germain entered the plate competition three times over five years; hers was the only
entry each round. The judges — Lagrange, Laplace, Poisson among them — found errors in
the first two attempts, some of them gaps in her self-taught training that the
Polytechnique would have filled, and Poisson, her rival on the problem, gave her little
help and less credit. The third attempt won, though she did not attend the ceremony.
Her equation for the vibrating plate was not the last word, but it was the first
serious one, and the modern theory grew from the argument around it.

When Gauss learned, in 1807, that his correspondent LeBlanc was a woman, he wrote back
that someone who overcame such obstacles to master "the most obscure parts of number
theory" must have "the noblest courage, quite extraordinary talents, and superior
genius." They never met. Her other great work was on Fermat's Last Theorem, where
"Germain primes" still carry her name — primes p where 2p+1 is also prime, which a
child on the multiplication plane can hunt for.

The best story about her is the one Michael would open with. In 1806 Napoleon's army
marched into Prussia, toward Brunswick, where Gauss lived. Germain remembered how she
had first fallen for mathematics as a girl: reading how Archimedes died, killed by a
soldier while drawing figures in the sand at Syracuse. Afraid history would repeat, she
wrote to General Pernety, a family friend commanding French artillery, and asked him to
see that Gauss came to no harm. An officer duly arrived at Gauss's door to guarantee
his safety, explaining he was sent thanks to Mademoiselle Germain — and Gauss, baffled,
answered that he knew no such person. He knew only M. LeBlanc. That is how the name
unravelled, and the famous letter followed.

Voice note (Michael, 31 Aug): if this becomes a story, consider opening on the army

She published, when she published at all, into a world that would not admit her; the
same year, an eighteen-year-old by Lake Geneva was writing a novel she would publish
with no name on it.
