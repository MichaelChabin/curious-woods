## Panel

Ada Byron was born in December 1815. Weeks later her parents separated; in April 1816
Byron left England for the summer of Villa Diodati and never saw her again. Her mother,
convinced poetry was a dangerous inheritance, raised her on mathematics. In 1843 Ada
published a description of Charles Babbage's unbuilt Analytical Engine that included,
in its Note G, a step-by-step procedure for the machine to compute a sequence of
numbers — the thing we now call a program. She signed the work only A.A.L.

## More

Babbage's engine was a machine of brass gears that was never finished, and Ada's Notes
— three times the length of the paper she was translating — are where its meaning got
worked out. Her deepest observation was not the program but what the machine was *for*:
it acted on symbols, not quantities, so it might, she wrote, compose music or weave
patterns "just as the Jacquard loom weaves flowers and leaves." A calculating machine
was a special case of something larger. That is the idea the twentieth century built.

She called her method "poetical science," which reads as a reconciliation of her two
parents. She knew Mary Somerville, corresponded with Faraday, and worked openly in a
world that, like Germain's, did not put women's full names on mathematics — hence
A.A.L. The intuition her node carries for the platform is i19: encoding a procedure so
something else can run it, which is what a child does every time she saves a
construction log and replays it.

She died at thirty-six. The engine was never built; the program was never run; both
were right.
