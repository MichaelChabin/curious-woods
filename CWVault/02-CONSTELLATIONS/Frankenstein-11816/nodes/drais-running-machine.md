## Panel

On 12 June 1817 Karl Drais, a forestry official in Mannheim, sat astride a wooden frame
with two wheels in a line and pushed himself along the road with his feet. He covered
about fourteen kilometres in under an hour, faster than the mail coach. It had no
pedals; those came fifty years later. It is the bicycle's grandfather, and it appeared
in the first summer after the harvest failed and the horses were sold or eaten.

## More

Whether the famine caused the machine is a *likely* rather than a *proved* link. Drais
had been working on human-powered vehicles since 1813, and his own writings stress
speed and cheapness rather than the shortage of horses. But 1816–17 saw oat prices
double, horses slaughtered across Germany, and Drais's patent application and public
demonstrations fall exactly in that window. Most historians of the bicycle accept the
connection as at least part of the story.

The machine spread fast — "dandy horses" in England, "draisines" in France — and was
banned in several cities within two years for being ridden on pavements. Drais himself
made little from it. He was a liberal in a reactionary state, was stripped of his
title, and died poor.

The engineering is worth a child's attention: two wheels in a line do not fall over
when moving, and nobody in 1817 could say why. The full explanation of why a bicycle
balances is still argued about.
