## Panel

Mary Shelley's 1831 preface recalls the Diodati talk turning to "Dr. Darwin," who was
said to have kept a piece of vermicelli in a glass case until it began to move on its
own. She is careful: *said to have*. Erasmus Darwin — doctor, poet, inventor — had died
in 1802. He had written that life might arise from a single living filament and change
over ages. His grandson Charles would work out how.

## More

Erasmus Darwin was a Lichfield physician with a large practice, a stammer, an
enthusiasm for machines, and a habit of writing his science in rhyming couplets. His
*Zoonomia* (1794–96) proposed that all warm-blooded animals had arisen from "one living
filament" and had changed over "millions of ages" by their own efforts. It is
evolution without a mechanism, and the Church noticed. He was also a founding member
of the Lunar Society in Birmingham, with Watt, Boulton, Wedgwood and Priestley, which
met on the Monday nearest the full moon so members could ride home by its light.

The vermicelli story is probably a garbled version of experiments on *vorticellae* —
microscopic animals that seem to revive after drying out — which Erasmus discusses. The
garbling is itself the point for this cluster: what the Diodati party had was rumour
about science, which is what most people have, and it was enough.

This node is the door to the Blind Watchmaker simulations. Charles Darwin read his
grandfather's book as a student and found it, he said, disappointing on second reading
— and then spent his life supplying what it lacked.
