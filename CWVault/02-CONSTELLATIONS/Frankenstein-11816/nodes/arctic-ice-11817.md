## Panel

In 1817 William Scoresby, a whaling captain from Whitby who kept scientific notes,
reported that the sea off east Greenland was open where it was normally packed with
ice. He wrote to the Royal Society. The Admiralty, excited, sent two expeditions north
the next spring. It seems backwards: less sun, less ice. The likeliest answer is that
the ice had not melted but *moved*.

## More

The cooling from Tambora was not even. The haze changed where the winds blew, and one
reconstruction is that stronger northerlies pushed the pack ice out of the Greenland
Sea and south past Iceland toward Newfoundland — where, in 1816 and 1817, ships
reported unusual numbers of icebergs. Open water in one place, more ice in another,
and a colder world overall. Climate scientists still argue about the mechanism, and the
argument is part of the node: this is a real puzzle, not a settled fact.

The expeditions of 1818 — John Ross toward the Northwest Passage, David Buchan and a
young John Franklin toward the pole — found the ice back. But the burst of polar
interest they started ran through the century.

The novel does not belong to this chain, and an earlier draft of this cluster wrongly
said it did. *Frankenstein* opens on a ship bound for the pole, and it was published
the same year the expeditions sailed; but Mary had written that frame before
Scoresby's report reached London. Same year, no cause. On the timeline that is a facet
edge, and it is enough.
