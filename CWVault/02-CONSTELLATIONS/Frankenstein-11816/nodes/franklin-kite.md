## Panel

In 1752 Benjamin Franklin flew a silk kite under a storm cloud with a key tied to the
string, and drew a spark from the key. The point was not the stunt. He had proposed
that lightning was ordinary electricity on a large scale, and this was the test. It was
in fact done first in France, that May, by Dalibard with an iron rod, following
Franklin's published instructions. The lightning rod followed within the year.

## More

Franklin's electrical work was a decade long and mostly indoors: Leyden jars, the idea
of positive and negative charge, the observation that pointed conductors draw charge
off quietly. The kite is remembered because it is a picture. What it settled was that
the sky's electricity and the laboratory's were one thing, which is the step that makes
Galvani's frog and Aldini's corpse and Faraday's coil part of the same subject.

It is worth saying how dangerous it was. A Russian physicist, Richmann, was killed the
next year doing a version of the experiment. Franklin's kite string was wet only near
the bottom, he stood under cover, and the storm was not overhead; he was careful and
also lucky.

The novel's lightning is the 1931 film's, not Mary Shelley's — but she does give Victor
a boyhood memory of watching lightning destroy an oak, and it is the moment he turns to
electricity. So the kite belongs, one hop back.
