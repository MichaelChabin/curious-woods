## Panel

In June 1816 Mary Godwin was eighteen, unmarried, and staying near Lake Geneva with the
poet Shelley when Byron proposed that everyone write a ghost story. She could not think
of one for days. Then, after a night of talk about whether a corpse might be reanimated,
she saw it: a student kneeling beside the thing he had put together. She began the next
morning. The book came out on New Year's Day 1818 with no name on it.

## More

The novel's creature is not made with lightning; that arrived with the 1931 film. Mary is
deliberately vague about the "spark of being," which is what makes it science fiction
rather than magic: the method is withheld, not supernatural. What she had heard, and says
so in the 1831 preface, was talk of *galvanism* — the thirty-year argument, running since
her infancy, about whether electricity was the stuff of life. Giovanni Aldini's public
demonstrations in London (1803) had happened when she was five, in the city she grew up
in, and were the talk of it.

The book's frame is a letter from an Arctic expedition, which is one of several ways the
novel is about reaching too far. It sold modestly, was assumed to be by Shelley, and was
turned into a stage play within five years. Mary revised it heavily in 1831; the 1818
text is the stranger and better one, and it is the one to quote.
