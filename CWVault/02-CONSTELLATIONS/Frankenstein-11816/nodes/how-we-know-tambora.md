## Panel

Snow falls on the middle of Greenland and never melts; it packs into ice, one layer a
year, like tree rings. Drill a core a mile deep and count down: about two hundred
layers below the surface there is a thin band full of sulphur that fell out of the sky
in 1815 and 1816. Antarctic cores show the same band. That is how a mountain in
Indonesia was finally convicted of a Vermont snowstorm.

## More

The idea that volcanoes cool the planet was proposed seriously in 1913 by an American
meteorologist, W. J. Humphreys, who noticed that cold years followed big eruptions. It
stayed a suggestion for decades because there was no way to measure the past. Ice cores
changed that in the 1970s and 80s: the layers can be counted like pages, the sulphur
in each can be weighed, and a spike in 1815–16 turns up in both hemispheres — which
rules out any local volcano and points to something enormous near the equator. Tree
rings add a second witness: 1816 shows frost damage in trees from Quebec to Germany.

This is the platform's own method applied to history. Nobody remembers 1816; the
evidence is counted, layer by layer, until the answer converges (i11). The date is not
looked up, it is *arrived at*, and the arriving is the story.

It is also where the speed-of-information thread ends: the news that took months to
cross the world in 1815 took a hundred and seventy years to be understood.
