## Panel

In 1805 Jane Marcet published *Conversations on Chemistry* — real chemistry, current to
the year, written as a running conversation between the patient Mrs. B and two girls,
Emily and Caroline, who interrupt, object, and get things wrong on the way to getting
them right. She wrote it, she said, because public lectures had lost her until a friend
talked them through with her afterward. Her name did not appear on it for decades. A
bookbinder's apprentice named Michael Faraday read it while binding a copy.

## More

Faraday said so ever after: Marcet's *Conversations* and the *Encyclopaedia Britannica*
article on electricity were his education, and when he was famous they became friends
and she updated her later editions from his discoveries — the student, by then, teaching
the teacher. The book went through sixteen London editions and sold enormously in
America, mostly in pirated printings that also left her name off.

She chose the dialogue form on purpose. Caroline is the skeptic who asks the reader's
own questions; Emily is the careful one; Mrs. B never lectures for long before someone
interrupts. It is teaching that refuses to be a lecture — which makes her the closest
thing this platform has to an ancestor, and the reason this node is here. A child who
asks why the stories talk *with* her can be shown a book from 1805 doing the same
thing, written by a woman who could not put her name on it.

Tennyson recalled her too — "When I was a lad, a mere lad, you know, I was given a
book called Conversations on Physical Science…" (quoted in the current Tennyson
biography; exact citation to confirm — the title he remembers is close to her
*Natural Philosophy*, which is its own small lesson in how books live in memory).
Charles Lyell is also reported among her readers. Faraday remains the documented
anchor; these two are marked for verification before they reach a child.

Her *Conversations on Political Economy* appeared in 1816, the year of this
constellation; *Natural Philosophy* followed in 1819. She was Geneva-Swiss by family,
and her London house was a salon where the scientists her books explained actually met.
