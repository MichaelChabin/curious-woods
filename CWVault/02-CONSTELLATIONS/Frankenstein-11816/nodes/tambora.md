## Panel

On 10 April 1815 a mountain on the island of Sumbawa blew off its top third. The sound
carried to Sumatra, sixteen hundred miles away, where soldiers turned out expecting an
attack. Within a few hundred miles the sky was dark for two days. Tens of thousands died
nearby, most of them afterward, from hunger. It is the largest eruption anyone has
written down. Almost no one in Europe heard about it for months.

## More

Tambora had been a mountain of about 4,300 metres; afterward it was 2,850, with a crater
six kilometres across. It threw roughly a hundred cubic kilometres of rock and ash into
the air — and, more important for the rest of the world, tens of millions of tonnes of
sulphur high into the stratosphere, where it spread into a haze that circled the planet
and stayed for more than a year. That haze is the link to everything else in this
cluster: it dimmed the sun a little, everywhere, for long enough to ruin a harvest.

The nearest Europeans, Stamford Raffles's staff in Java, collected accounts from
survivors; those reports are the reason we know what happened. Raffles himself sent a
ship to investigate because he thought the noise was a naval battle.

The scale is worth sitting with. The distance the sound travelled is about the distance
from London to Moscow. The volume of rock is a cube about four and a half kilometres on
a side.
