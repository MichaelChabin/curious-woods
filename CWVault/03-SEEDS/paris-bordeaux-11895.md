# Paris–Bordeaux–Paris, 1895

June 1895. Émile Levassor drives the round trip — roughly 1,200 km — in something close to
forty-eight hours, alone, in an open car, at an average pace a fit cyclist could nearly
match on a good day. He is disqualified on a technicality about seating. It does not matter.

The number to check before this becomes a story: the exact elapsed time and the average
speed. The average is the point, and it is startlingly low — which is exactly why it works
for a child. The revolution was not that it was fast. It was that it did not stop.

## What it opens onto

**Order of magnitude, and the difference between speed and endurance.** A horse is faster
than that car over an hour and useless over two days. This is a genuinely good
approximation problem: how far can a horse go in a day, a train, a car, a person walking?

**Distance as a felt thing that changed within one lifetime.** Pairs directly with
`montparnasse-11895` — four months apart, same country, the two faces of the same
machinery. One shows what happens when the new speed fails. The other shows what it bought.
