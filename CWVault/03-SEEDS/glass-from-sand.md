# Glass from sand — and why it took so long

The origin story of the Glass lab. Michael's note: Glass Geometry grew out of this, and
these stories are still live even though the lab has travelled a long way from them.

Sand does not want to be glass. Silica melts somewhere around 1700 °C, which is hotter than
a wood fire and hotter than most of human history could reach. The trick — soda ash, later
lime — drops the working temperature by hundreds of degrees, and that chemical accident is
the whole reason glass exists as a common material rather than a curiosity.

Then colour: metal oxides in the melt. Cobalt for blue, gold for red, manganese to take the
green out. The medieval glassmaker knew the recipe and not the reason, which is a good place
for a child to stand.

## The facet that does the work here

`scale: temperature`. The melting point of silica is a fixed number on a beam that also
carries the temperature of a candle flame, an iron forge, the surface of the sun, and the
inside of a kiln. A child who learns where glass sits on that beam has learned something
that stays true and connects outward forever. This is the argument for building the
Reference Atlas temperature scale — it earns its keep the moment a second seed hangs on it.

The palettes in `art/palettes.json` already carry `recipe` fields. The chemistry is in the
tool already; the story explaining it is not.

## What it opens onto

Why civilisations that had sand and fire still did not have windows. What a furnace is,
and why getting hotter is an engineering problem rather than a matter of more wood.
Whether the child can find, on the temperature beam, the gap that had to be crossed.
