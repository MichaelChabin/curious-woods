# Poetry tricks

A poem sometimes carries a trick worth pointing out: a thing a writer does on purpose that she could do tomorrow in her own writing. The puzzle mentions it once she has finished the poem, in one short note.

Michael, 28 Sept: these are "not something to memorize, they are tricks that are fun to use." Lists of them are easy to find, and a list is exactly what can kill poetry for a child. So this one is short, and it stays short.

## The rules

- **A trick earns a place** only if she can hear or see it in the poem at once, and could use it herself.
- **One note per poem, never more,** under the finished poem, like the swapped-lines note. Never before she has finished.
- **The first time** she meets a trick, she gets the full note. **After that,** the short one. Her device remembers which tricks she has met, the way it remembers the introduction, and nowhere else.
- **No classroom names** (alliteration, pun, meter). If a name is ever worth giving, it comes the Story-Voice way: explained in the sentence it arrives in, then "you don't need to remember the name."
- **Never tell her it's funny, clever or interesting.** End on the fact.
- **Nothing waits on a trick.** Limericks come before other forms because she hears the beat in them, not because she has "covered" anything. Nothing is gated.

## The six

**1. Made-up words.** Poems: the pelican.
First time: "Nobody had ever written *bellican* or *helican* before this poem. The writer made them up because nothing rhymes with pelican, and he trusted you to know what they meant the moment you read them."
Again: "Another word made up for the rhyme."

**2. Bending how a word is said.** Poems: Niger.
Here the note is the background line, before the poem, since she needs it to read the poem at all: "…people there say its name the French way: nee-ZHAIR. To make the limerick work, it was changed to rhyme with tiger."
Again: "Here a word is bent a little to make the rhyme."

**3. One word, two meanings.** Poems: Hall, the canner.
First time (Hall): "Two words in this poem each mean two things. A spring is a season, and it is also water coming up out of the ground. A fall is a season, and it is also what Hall did."
First time (canner): "*Can* does three jobs here. It means *is able to*, it means *to seal food in a tin*, and it means the tin."
Again: "Another word doing two jobs at once."

**4. The same sound, over and over.** Poems: the flue, the tutor.
First time (flue): "Nearly every word that matters in this poem starts with *f* or *fl*. Said slowly it sounds like a tongue twister. Said fast, it is one."
First time (tutor): "*Tutor, tooted, two, tooters, toot*: the whole poem is built out of one sound."
Again: "Another poem built out of one sound."

**5. The surprise saved for the last line.** Poems: Lynn, Crewe, Peru, Niger.
First time: "Everything in this poem is there to set up the last line. Edward Lear usually ended a limerick by saying his first line again. The writers who came after him saved the last line for the surprise."
Again: "Again, the last line is where the surprise was waiting."
(Niger already carries a note; this one goes on the others.)

**6. Breaking the pattern on purpose.** Poems: Japan.
First time: "A limerick has a beat, da-da-DUM, da-da-DUM, and the first four lines of this one keep it. The last line forgets the beat completely. That is the joke."
Again: none yet; Japan is the only one.

Poems with no trick yet: Bright, the bush. That's fine. Not every poem needs a note.

## Notes for us

- Check before adding a seventh: would a bright ten-year-old use it in her own writing this week? If not, it stays out.
- Where tricks cross into other forms (the beat in Dickinson, the turn in a haiku), write the new note for the new form; don't stretch an old one.
- Pillar: Stories. Intuition: comfort with dense unfamiliar text, the habit of reading closely and trusting that re-reading works.
