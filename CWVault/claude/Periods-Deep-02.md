## 1. Shells

**Label:** Shells
**Kind:** period
**Span:** 539 to 487 Ma · both edges dated boundaries at points in the rock: 538.8 ± 0.6 (Fortune Head, Newfoundland) and 486.85 ± 1.5 (Green Point, Newfoundland); 487 replaces 485 (Michael, 10 Oct), because 485 lay outside the chart's error

**Summary**

**More**

```
From about 539 to 487 million years ago.
```

In the Flinders Ranges of South Australia, near an old mine called Ajax, there is grey limestone that was once a reef in a warm, shallow sea, about 520 million years ago. The rock is full of small cups and cones, most of them a few centimetres across, each with a double wall full of holes. They were archaeocyaths ("AR-kee-oh-SIGH-aths"), animals that were probably sponges. Growing together with microbes that also made lime, they built mounds of rock on the sea floor: some of the first big reefs built partly by animals.

Around reefs like these, and on sea floors everywhere, lived animals with hard parts. That is what is new: shells, spines and armour, and eyes too, not in a few kinds of animal but in thousands. A few animals had made shells before (*Bites and shells*). Now hard parts were everywhere, and because hard parts last, the rocks fill with fossils (*Animals, kept*).

Within about twenty million years, fossils turn up of most of the great branches of the animal family alive today: animals with jointed legs, animals with shells in two halves, snails, worms, and the branch with a stiff rod along the back, the one fish belong to. In a few places mud buried animals so fast that their soft parts were kept as well (*Chengjiang*, *Burgess Shale*).

Most of the land lay south of the equator, much of it joined in one great continent, Gondwana (*The map clears*). The sea was warm, and it rose through the stretch. The air held less oxygen than it does now.

Fifty-two million years is nearly four fifths as long as the whole Mammals line.

Geologists call this stretch the Cambrian ("KAM-bree-un"), from Cambria, the name for Wales in medieval Latin, where Adam Sedgwick, an English geologist, studied its rocks. He gave it the name in 1835.

At its end the sea was full of animals. On land, as far as anyone can tell, nothing grew but films of microbes.

- John Paterson, Gregory Edgecombe and Michael Lee, "Trilobite evolutionary rates constrain the duration of the Cambrian explosion," *PNAS*, 2019. The oldest trilobites.
- Stephen Jay Gould, *Wonderful Life* (W. W. Norton, 1989). For a parent.

## 2. Crowded seas

**Label:** Crowded seas
**Kind:** period
**Span:** 487 to 444 Ma · both edges dated boundaries at points in the rock: 486.85 ± 1.5 (Green Point, Newfoundland) and 443.1 ± 0.9 (Dob's Linn, Scotland)
**Length:** 43 million years
**Official name:** Ordovician, after the Ordovices, a people of north Wales in Roman times; named by Charles Lapworth in 1879
**Known from:** shells by the billion in limestone, laid down in warm, shallow seas that covered much of the continents
**Opens to:** nothing yet
**Inside it:** events *Plants ashore*, *Short, sharp ice* (at the end edge). Plots: *Sea level*, *Oxygen*.
**Index:** none
**Pictures wanted:** a slab of Cincinnati limestone packed with shells (Cincinnati Museum Center or Commons; licence to check); *Isotelus*, Ohio's state fossil (Cincinnati Museum Center; licence to ask).

**Summary**
Shallow seas spread over the continents and filled with life, until there were three or four times as many kinds of sea animal as at the start. On land, the oldest signs of plants. It ended in ice. *More*

**More**

```
From about 487 to 444 million years ago.
```

In and around Cincinnati, in Ohio, the hillsides and creek beds are made of thin grey layers of limestone and mud, and much of the limestone is nothing but shells. About 450 million years ago this was the floor of a warm, shallow sea, south of the equator, in a belt of storms. The storms heaped the shells into beds.

Pick up a slab and there are shells in two halves, from fingernail to coin size; little stony twigs and crusts that were colonies of tiny animals; stems of sea lilies, animals related to starfish that stood on stalks; and trilobites. One trilobite, *Isotelus* ("eye-so-TEE-lus"), grew longer than a hand. Hunting over the sea floor were animals with long, straight, pointed shells, relatives of the nautilus, the octopus and the squid. The biggest piece of such a shell ever found is three metres long, and it is only part of a shell.

That is what is new: how many kinds of animal there were. In about twenty-five million years the number of kinds of sea animal grew three or four times over. Trilobites came in more shapes than ever.

The seas themselves spread. Sea level stood higher than at almost any other time we know of, perhaps a hundred and fifty metres above today's, and by some estimates more than two hundred, and shallow seas covered much of the continents.

On land, about 470 million years ago, plants left spores in rock in what is now Argentina, the oldest sign of plants on land that most specialists accept (*Plants ashore*). The plants themselves have never been found.

Forty-three million years is about two thirds as long as the Mammals line.

Geologists call this stretch the Ordovician ("or-duh-VISH-un"), after the Ordovices, a people who lived in north Wales when the Romans came. Charles Lapworth, an English geologist, gave it the name in 1879.

At its end, ice spread over what is now North Africa, which then lay near the South Pole, and the seas grew cold. Most kinds of sea animal died out (*Short, sharp ice*).

**References**
- David Harper, "The Ordovician biodiversification: setting an agenda for marine life," *Palaeogeography, Palaeoclimatology, Palaeoecology*, 2006. Three to four times as many families.
- Thomas Servais and others, "Understanding the Great Ordovician Biodiversification Event (GOBE): influences of paleogeography, paleoclimate, or paleoecology?," *GSA Today*, 2009.
- Cole Edwards, Matthew Saltzman, Dana Royer and David Fike, "Oxygenation as a driver of the Great Ordovician Biodiversification Event," *Nature Geoscience*, 2017.
- David Meyer and Richard Davis, *A Sea Without Fish: Life in the Ordovician Sea of the Cincinnati Region* (Indiana University Press, 2009). For a parent.

**Notes for us.** The scene is the Cincinnatian (Katian, about 450 Ma): limestone made of shells, mudstone between; a sea 10 to 100 m deep, about 20° S, in the hurricane belt (Ordovician Atlas, Meyer and Davis). *Isotelus*, Ohio's state fossil since 1985; a complete specimen at the Cincinnati Museum Center is 40 cm, so "longer than a hand" is safe. *Isotelus rex* (72 cm) is from Manitoba, not Ohio. Nautiloids: the 9 m *Cameroceras* is a second-hand report of a shell never kept (Flower 1955; "doubtful", Klug 2025); the largest real piece is the 3 m *Endoceras* fragment at Harvard's Museum of Comparative Zoology, broken at both ends. GOBE: families three to four times (Harper 2006), genera about four times (Edwards 2017), "roughly tripled" (Servais 2009), over about 25 million years, about 485 to 460; "three or four times over". Causes: oxygen (Edwards 2017), cooling (Trotter 2008). Sea level "more than 200 m" (Med): must agree with the *Sea level* plot. Lapworth settled the Sedgwick–Murchison dispute; Ordovices glossed as "hammer-fighters" by Etymonline, a guess, left out. 43 of 66 is 0.65. Agreement: *Plants ashore* (470, Jujuy, "oldest widely accepted sign"); *Short, sharp ice* ("ice sheets spread over North Africa, which then lay near the South Pole"; "most kinds of sea animal"). Optional fact cut for length: the meteorite shower after an asteroid broke up, 465.76 ± 0.30 Ma (Liao 2020), fossil meteorites in Swedish limestone. For the checkers: "shell the size of a fingernail" for brachiopods; "lacy crusts" for bryozoans; "or-duh-VISH-un"; "English geologist" for Lapworth.

## 3. Green land

**Label:** Green land
**Kind:** period
**Span:** 444 to 419 Ma · both edges dated boundaries at points in the rock: 443.1 ± 0.9 (Dob's Linn, Scotland) and 419.62 ± 1.36 (Klonk, Czech Republic)
**Length:** 25 million years
**Official name:** Silurian, after the Silures, a people of south-east Wales in Roman times; named by Roderick Murchison in 1835
**Known from:** small plants pressed flat in river and shore mud, spores, charcoal, and fossils in sea rock
**Opens to:** nothing yet
**Inside it:** events *Short, sharp ice* (at the start edge), *Jaws*, *Animals ashore*. Plots: *Sea level*, *Oxygen*.
**Index:** none
**Pictures wanted:** a *Cooksonia* fossil (Natural History Museum, London, or Commons; licence to check); a drawing of *Cooksonia* at life size (to make).

**Summary**
After the ice, plants stood up. Along rivers and shores grew green stems a few centimetres tall, with no leaves. Small animals came onto the land. In the sea, fish with jaws spread. *More*

**More**

```
From about 444 to 419 million years ago.
```

At Perton, near Stoke Edith, in Herefordshire, close to the border with Wales, there is rock from near the end of this stretch, laid down where rivers ran into the sea. A plant from it is called *Cooksonia* ("cook-SOH-nee-a"), after Isabel Cookson, the Australian botanist who collected it.

*Cooksonia* was a few centimetres tall. It had no leaves and no flowers: only thin green stems that forked, and forked again, with a small round case at the tip of each branch, full of spores. A bank covered in it would have been green, and none of it taller than a finger.

That is what is new: plants that stood up. Plants had been on land for some forty million years already (*Plants ashore*), but as far as anyone knows they lay low and flat, like liverworts. To stand up, a plant needs stiff stems, with pipes inside to carry water up from the ground. Some fossils of *Cooksonia* show such pipes.

The stretch began just after the great ice had shrunk, when most kinds of sea animal had died (*Short, sharp ice*). The seas grew warm again and stood high. Fish with jaws spread through them (*Jaws*), and sea scorpions, some of them about two metres long, hunted in the shallows.

Small animals came onto the land. The oldest one known to have breathed air was like a little millipede, from Stonehaven, in Scotland, though its age is argued (*Animals ashore*).

The tallest living thing on land was not a plant but *Prototaxites* ("pro-toe-tax-EYE-teez"), most likely a fungus: a trunk with no branches. The biggest found, a little younger than this stretch, stood about eight metres tall.

Twenty-five million years is a little over a third as long as the Mammals line.

Geologists call it the Silurian ("sigh-LOOR-ee-un"), after the Silures, a people who lived in south-east Wales when the Romans came. Roderick Murchison, a Scottish geologist, gave it the name in 1835.

**References**

- Milan Libertín and others, "Sporophytes of polysporangiate land plants from the early Silurian period may have been photosynthetically autonomous," *Nature Plants*, 2018. The oldest *Cooksonia*, about 432 million.
- Ian Glasspool and Robert Gastaldo, "Silurian wildfire proxies and atmospheric oxygen," *Geology*, 2022. The oldest charcoal.
- Thomas Halliday, *Otherlands* (Allen Lane, 2022). For a parent.

## 4. Fish

**Label:** Fish
**Kind:** period
**Span:** 419 to 359 Ma · both edges dated boundaries at points in the rock: 419.62 ± 1.36 (Klonk, Czech Republic) and 358.86 ± 0.19 (La Serre, France, a point now under review)
**Length:** 60 million years
**Official name:** Devonian, after Devon, in England; named by Adam Sedgwick and Roderick Murchison in 1839
**Known from:** whole fish in limestone and red sandstone, fossil forests, footprints, and the daily growth lines of corals
**Opens to:** nothing yet
**Inside it:** events *Forests*, *Tiktaalik*. Plots: *Sea level*, *Oxygen*, *Day length* (about 22 hours at 390, from Wells's corals).
**Index:** none
**Pictures wanted:** a Gogo fish freed from its nodule (Western Australian Museum or Museums Victoria; licence to ask); *Materpiscis* with its embryo (Museums Victoria; licence to ask).

**Summary**
The seas held fish of every shape: fish in armour of bone, sharks, fish with fleshy fins built on bones. On land, plants grew into trees, and forests spread. In a river lived a fish with fins nearly like legs. *More*

**More**

```
From about 419 to 359 million years ago.
```

In the Kimberley, in the far north of Western Australia, a line of limestone hills was once a reef in a warm sea, about 380 million years ago. Beside the reef the water was deep and still, with little oxygen. Dead fish sank into it and were sealed in lumps of lime before they could rot. Today, when collectors soak those lumps in weak acid, the same acid that is in vinegar, the fish come out whole, every bone in place.

Many of the Gogo fish were placoderms ("PLAK-o-derms"), fish whose heads and chests were covered in plates of bone, with sharp-edged plates of bone in their jaws. One, named *Materpiscis*, "mother fish", has a young one inside her, still joined by its cord: one of the oldest known animals with backbones to give birth to live young.

That is what is new: fish of every shape. Armoured fish, sharks, fish with thin rays in their fins like most fish today, and fish with fleshy fins built on bones. The biggest of the armoured fish, *Dunkleosteus*, was about three or four metres long.

Fish were not new. Fish with jaws are known from about 439 million years ago (*Jaws*), and fish without jaws from long before. The name marks the time when fish were the most noticed animals, not when they began.

The oldest forest yet found grew where Somerset is now, about 390 million years ago (*Forests*), and by the end of the stretch there were trees with wood and leaves in many places. In rivers lived fish with necks and bony fins (*Tiktaalik*).

A year then had about four hundred days, as fossil corals count them.

Sixty million years is nearly as long as the whole Mammals line.

Geologists call this stretch the Devonian ("di-VOH-nee-un"), after Devon, in England. Adam Sedgwick and Roderick Murchison gave it the name in 1839.

It ended in a long run of dyings. Reefs like the one at Gogo were among the worst hit, and by the end every armoured fish was gone.

**References**

- Russell Engelman, "A Devonian fish tale: a new method of body length estimation suggests much smaller sizes for *Dunkleosteus terrelli* (Placodermi: Arthrodira)," *Diversity*, 2023.
- Lauren Sallan and Michael Coates, "End-Devonian extinction and a bottleneck in the early evolution of modern jawed vertebrates," *PNAS*, 2010.
- Neil Shubin, *Your Inner Fish* (Pantheon, 2008). For a parent.

**Notes for us.** The scene is Gogo (Frasnian, about 380 Ma): a reef built mainly by microbes and sponge-like stromatoporoids, not corals, so the More says only "a reef"; fish sealed in limestone nodules in deep, poorly oxygenated water; freed in dilute acetic acid; more than 50 species; Curt Teichert recognised the reef in 1940. *Materpiscis attenboroughi* (Long 2008): the oldest live birth among vertebrates, not among all animals. Gogo also gave the oldest 3D vertebrate heart (Trinajstic 2022), cut for length. *Dunkleosteus*: Engelman 2023, about 3.4 m typical, about 4.1 m largest; the old 6 to 10 m is wrong. Fish older: *Haikouichthys*, Chengjiang, about 518; *Qianodus*, 439. Day length: Wells 1963, about 400 ridges a year in Middle Devonian corals, about 21.9 hours at 390 Ma (the *Day length* plot); "about four hundred days… about twenty-two hours". End: Kellwasser (372.15 ± 0.46) and Hangenberg (about 359); reefs hardest hit; the last placoderms gone at the Hangenberg (Sallan and Coates 2010); severity argued. Sedgwick and Murchison: proposed 1839, printed 1840. 60 of 66 is 0.91. Agreement: *Forests* (390, Somerset, "the oldest forest yet found"); *Tiktaalik* (375, "a fish with a neck and fins with bones like an arm's"; "footprints in Poland suggest animals with legs were older still", both copied); *Jaws* (439). For the checkers: "the acid in vinegar" (acetic acid, dilute); "blades of bone where teeth would be"; "di-VOH-nee-un"; "PLAK-o-derms"; "trees with wood and leaves in many places" by 359 (*Archaeopteris*).

## 5. Coal forests

**Label:** Coal forests
**Kind:** period
**Span:** 359 to 299 Ma · both edges dated boundaries at points in the rock: 358.86 ± 0.19 (La Serre, France, under review) and 298.9 ± 0.15 (Aidaralash, Kazakhstan)
**Length:** 60 million years
**Official name:** Carboniferous, "coal-bearing", from the Latin *carbo*, coal; named by William Conybeare and William Phillips in 1822. In North America it is split into the Mississippian and the Pennsylvanian.
**Known from:** coal and the plants pressed in it, fossil tree stumps, insects in mudstone
**Opens to:** nothing yet
**Inside it:** events *Land egg*, *Coal*. Plots: *Oxygen* (the high near 305), *Sea level*, *Day length*.
**Index:** none
**Pictures wanted:** the *Meganeura* fossil from Commentry (Muséum national d'Histoire naturelle, Paris; licence to ask); a scale-tree trunk with its diamond pattern (Commons; licence to check).

**Summary**
Swamp forests grew along the warm middle of the world, and their dead plants became coal. The air held more oxygen than it does now. Some insects had wings seventy centimetres across, and a millipede grew longer than a person. *More*

**More**

```
From about 359 to 299 million years ago.
```

Stand in a coal swamp near the end of this stretch and the trees are like nothing alive. The tallest are scale trees: bare poles thirty metres high or more, a metre or more thick at the base, with a crown of branches only at the very top. Their bark is covered in diamond-shaped scars where leaves once grew. Their nearest relatives alive today are small plants called quillworts.

Over the water flies *Meganeura* ("meg-a-NEW-ra"), its wings about seventy centimetres across. Its fossils come from coal mines at Commentry, in France. It looked like a dragonfly, but it belonged to a group of insects that is now gone, sometimes called griffinflies. On the forest floor lived *Arthropleura*, a millipede that grew to more than two and a half metres long.

That is what is new: forests so wide and so wet that their dead plants piled up faster than they could rot, and were buried, and became coal (*Coal*). The swamps grew in a belt along the equator, where the continents were coming together into one.

The air held more oxygen than it does now: at least a quarter as much again, and perhaps more (*Oxygen*).

Near the South Pole, ice sheets grew and melted many times. Each time the sea fell and rose, so layers of swamp and layers of sea lie stacked one on another.

Animals with backbones walked the land. Clawed footprints about 355 million years old, in Victoria, Australia, may have been made by animals whose eggs could be laid on dry land. By about 315 million years ago, small animals like lizards were trapped in hollow tree stumps in Nova Scotia (*Land egg*).

About 305 million years ago the climate grew drier, and the great swamp forests broke apart into patches.

Sixty million years is nearly as long as the whole Mammals line.

Geologists call this stretch the Carboniferous ("kar-buh-NIF-er-us"), "coal-bearing". William Conybeare and William Phillips, two English geologists, gave it the name in 1822.

In the ranch country around Seymour, in north Texas, the ground is red: old river mud and old soil, stained by rusted iron. About 280 million years ago rivers wound through it to ponds, with ferns by the water and conifers on the drier ground. In the ponds lived a fresh-water shark and *Diplocaulus*, an amphibian with a head shaped like a boomerang. Along the banks walked *Dimetrodon*, three metres long, with a tall sail on its back (*Dimetrodon*).

*Dimetrodon* was not a dinosaur. It died out about forty million years before the oldest dinosaurs yet found. It was not a reptile either. It belonged to the same great branch of animals as mammals.

What is new is one continent. Through the stretch before, the continents had been coming together, and now nearly all the land was joined, from near the South Pole to far into the north, in one great continent, Pangaea ("pan-JEE-a"), Greek for "all the Earth". Its middle was far from any sea. Much of it was dry, with deserts in many places and great rains in their season. The ice near the South Pole melted away early in the stretch.

On land, most of the largest animals were on the mammals' branch: first sail-backs like *Dimetrodon*, and later animals with sabre teeth, and plant-eaters with tusks and beaks. In the south grew forests of a tree called *Glossopteris*. Its fossil leaves, found in Africa, India, South America, Australia and Antarctica, helped show, long afterwards, that those lands had once been joined.

Forty-seven million years is about seven tenths as long as the Mammals line.

Geologists call this stretch the Permian ("PUR-mee-un"), after Perm, an old province of Russia west of the Ural Mountains, where Roderick Murchison studied its rocks. He gave it the name in 1841.

It ended in the Great Dying, when four in five kinds of sea animal died out, probably because of volcanoes in Siberia (*The Great Dying*).

At Ghost Ranch, in northern New Mexico, there is a quarry where in 1947 a museum team found a bed of dinosaur bones, skeleton lying on skeleton: hundreds of them, perhaps more than a thousand animals. All are *Coelophysis* ("see-lo-FY-sis"), a slender hunter up to three metres long, most of it neck and tail, about as heavy as a middle-sized dog. How so many died together is argued; a flood is the leading guess. They lived late in this stretch, perhaps 205 million years ago.

They were late. For nearly the first twenty million years of this stretch there were no dinosaurs at all (*Dinosaurs*). Even after the dinosaurs appeared, they were few. The crocodile side of the reptile family was commoner and came in more shapes: long-snouted fish-eaters, armoured plant-eaters, big hunters that walked upright.

The stretch began in an emptied world, just after the Great Dying, when one plant-eater, *Lystrosaurus*, was by far the commonest animal in many places (*Lystrosaurus*). All the land was one continent, Pangaea. Its middle was hot and dry, and there was no ice at either pole. About 233 million years ago, rain fell hard, on and off, for about two million years.

Fifty-one million years is more than a quarter of the Dinosaurs line, and about three quarters as long as the Mammals line.

At Dinosaur National Monument, in Utah, a hall has been built against a wall of tilted sandstone. In the wall are about fifteen hundred dinosaur bones, left where they lie: backbones, ribs, leg bones, a skull here and there. About 150 million years ago this was a river. The White Cliffs of Dover stand more than a hundred metres over the sea between England and France. They are chalk, and chalk under a microscope is mostly tiny plates of lime, each a few thousandths of a millimetre across. The plates were the armour of single-celled algae called coccolithophores ("cock-o-LITH-o-fores"). When the algae died, their plates sank, and settled on the sea floor, about a centimetre every thousand years, until the chalk was hundreds of metres thick. The cliffs east of Dover are about 94 to 86 million years old.

For the sea floor to be where Dover is, the sea had to be high. At their highest, shallow seas covered about a third of today's land, and one cut North America in two. Between about 90 and 80 million years ago, guesses for the height of the sea run from about fifty metres above today's to more than two hundred (*Sea level*). The world was warm, with no large ice sheets for most of the stretch.

What is new is flowers. Pollen of flowering plants goes back about 135 million years. Among the oldest whole flowering plants yet found are water plants with no petals, about 125 million years old (*Oldest flowers*). By the end of the stretch, flowering plants were the most varied plants in much of the world, though conifers and ferns still made up much of many forests.

The dinosaurs went on, and some grew larger than ever. *Argentinosaurus*, from about 95 million years ago, was perhaps thirty-five metres long, among the largest animals ever to walk. At the very end lived *Tyrannosaurus* and *Triceratops*.

Seventy-seven million years is longer than the whole Mammals line.

Geologists call this stretch the Cretaceous ("kri-TAY-shus"), "chalky", from *creta*, the Latin word for chalk. Jean-Baptiste d'Omalius d'Halloy, a Belgian geologist, gave it the name in 1822, for the chalk around Paris.

It ended about 66 million years ago, when an asteroid about ten kilometres across hit what is now Yucatán, in Mexico (*The asteroid*).

## Big footprints

**Label:** Big footprints
**Age:** 200.95 Ma · ± 0.2 Ma · from the layers: the tracks at Rocky Hill lie in the East Berlin Formation, between two lavas matched to lavas in the Newark Basin dated about 201.27 and 200.92 Ma (Blackburn 2013); the first big tracks, in the Newark Basin, are less than 10,000 years after the extinction (Olsen 2002), about 201.55
**Tail:** none — Rocky Hill is a moment; the first big tracks, a few hundred thousand years older, are told in the More
**Known from:** footprints in lake-shore mud turned to stone
**Place now:** Dinosaur State Park, Rocky Hill, Connecticut, United States (41.65 N, 72.66 W)
**Kind:** crop or animal (a living thing; the list has no better word)
**Replaces:** none; new

**Summary**
About 201 million years ago. Just after the dying that ended the Triassic, big meat-eating dinosaurs left thousands of footprints on lake shores in what is now Connecticut. Before the dying, meat-eaters' footprints in that part of the world were smaller. *More*

On 23 August 1966, in Rocky Hill, Connecticut, Edward McCarthy was digging with a bulldozer for a new state building when he turned over a slab of sandstone. On it were footprints, each with three long toes, some longer than a grown-up's foot. More digging found about two thousand of them. The building went somewhere else. Over hundreds of the tracks the state put a dome, and around it a park.

The rock was mud at the edge of a shallow lake, in a valley where the land was pulling apart as Pangaea cracked (*Pangaea cracks*). The mud has cracks where it dried in the sun, ripples from the water, and the dents of raindrops.

Most of the tracks are called *Eubrontes* ("you-BRON-teez"), "true thunder". The name belongs to the footprints themselves, because nobody knows exactly which animal made them. It was a dinosaur that walked on two legs and ate meat, perhaps five or six metres long, something like *Dilophosaurus*.

In the 1830s Edward Hitchcock, a geologist at Amherst College, studied tracks like these up and down the Connecticut Valley, and thought giant birds had made them. Birds are dinosaurs, so he was nearer right than anyone then could know.

The tracks also tell when the big meat-eaters arrived. In 2002 Paul Olsen and his team compared thousands of footprints, from more than seventy places in eastern North America, laid down before and after the dying that ended the Triassic (*Triassic ends*). Before it, the biggest meat-eaters' tracks were smaller. Less than ten thousand years after it, the big *Eubrontes* tracks appear, about a fifth longer than the biggest before. Olsen's team argued that with their rivals gone, the meat-eating dinosaurs grew bigger, fast.

The tracks at Rocky Hill are a little younger, a few hundred thousand years after the dying, between two flows of lava. By then the big footprints were everywhere in the valley.

## Dimetrodon

**Label:** Dimetrodon
**Age:** 280 Ma · ± 5 Ma · from the place of the Clear Fork red beds of Texas in the layers (Kungurian, about 283 to 274 Ma), not a radiometric date
**Tail:** 295 Ma — the oldest *Dimetrodon*; the genus lived from about 295 to 272 Ma
**Known from:** skeletons and teeth in red river mud in Texas and Oklahoma, and a few in other states, in Canada and in Germany
**Place now:** near Seymour, Baylor County, Texas, United States (33.59 N, 99.26 W, the town; the quarries are on private ranches)
**Kind:** crop or animal (a living thing; the list has no better word)
**Replaces:** none; new

**Summary**
About 280 million years ago. Along rivers in what is now Texas lived *Dimetrodon*, a hunter about three metres long with a tall sail on its back. It was not a dinosaur. It was on the mammals' branch. *More*

**More**


In the 1870s, collectors working in the red badlands of north Texas sent crates of bones to Edward Drinker Cope, a fossil hunter in Philadelphia. Among them were bones of a hunter, some with very long spines on its back. In 1878 Cope named it *Dimetrodon* ("dye-MET-ro-don"), "two-measure teeth", because its mouth held teeth of two sizes: big stabbing ones, and smaller ones.

*Dimetrodon* walked on four legs that sprawled out to the sides. The common Texas kinds were about three metres long. Along its back stood long spines of bone, with skin stretched between them: a sail. Healed breaks in some of the spines suggest that skin was there.

What the sail was for is argued. For a long time people thought it warmed the animal in the morning sun, or let heat out. But some small kinds had big sails, and the spines have no channels for the blood that warming would need. Many now think the sail was for show, to rivals or to mates, as a deer's antlers are.

It was the biggest hunter of its world, and one of the commonest large animals in the Texas rocks. It ate fish and amphibians. Near Seymour, its shed teeth have been found among the bones of young *Diplocaulus*, an amphibian with a head shaped like a boomerang, as if *Dimetrodon* had dug them out of their burrows.

Its later kinds had teeth with tiny saw edges, like a steak knife: the oldest such teeth known in an animal that lived on land.

*Dimetrodon* is in many boxes of toy dinosaurs, but it was not a dinosaur. It died out about forty million years before the oldest dinosaurs yet found. It was not a reptile either. It belonged to the same great branch of animals as mammals, on a side branch of its own.

Texas then lay near the equator, near the western edge of the one great continent, Pangaea, not far from the sea (*One continent*).
