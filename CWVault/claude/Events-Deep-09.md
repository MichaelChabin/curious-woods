## 1. Acanthostega

**Label:** Acanthostega
**Age:** 365 Ma · ± 5 Ma · from spores and fossils in the layers (Famennian, about 372 to 359 Ma); no date on the rock itself
**Tail:** none
**Known from:** about two hundred specimens, most from one bed of young animals that died together
**Place now:** Stensiö Bjerg, Gauss Halvø, East Greenland (Kalaallit Nunaat), about 800 metres up; the site's position is not published
**Kind:** crop or animal
**Drawing:** acanthostega.svg

**Summary (40 words)**
About 365 million years ago. *Acanthostega*, an animal with four legs and eight fingers on each hand, lived in rivers in what is now East Greenland. It breathed with gills, and its legs could not carry it on land. *More*

**More (336 words)**

```
About 365 million years ago.
```

In the summer of 1987, the palaeontologist Jenny Clack and her team were climbing a bare hillside in East Greenland, looking for one layer of rock. Their clue was a notebook. In 1970 a student on an expedition from the University of Cambridge, in England, had found fossils on this slope and written "skull roof bones common." The notebook put the spot at about 820 metres. The team searched lower down and worked upward, and about ten days after they arrived they found it, at about 800 metres. In six weeks they collected about a tonne of rock, and about two thirds of it held bones.

Back in Cambridge, freeing the bones from the rock took years. One block held a whole front leg with its fingers still in place. The person freeing it uncovered five fingers and stopped. Then three more turned up. All eight belonged to the same hand.

The animal is *Acanthostega* ("a-KAN-tho-STEG-a"). It was about sixty centimetres long, with a broad, flat head and a tail with a fin on it, like a fish's.

Fingers sound like a land animal. *Acanthostega*'s bones say otherwise. Its front legs could not bend forward at the elbow, so they could not have held up its weight out of water. Behind its head are bones that, in a fish, hold the gills, with grooves where the gills' blood vessels ran. It breathed with gills, as a fish does.

So legs with fingers came first, in the water, where *Acanthostega* probably used them to paddle and to hold on in weedy shallows. Five fingers, the number on a human hand, came later.

In 2016 a team led by Sophie Sanchez, with Per Ahlberg, who had been on the 1987 expedition, scanned the arm bones. Every animal they looked at was young, though at least six years old, with arm bones still partly gristle. The animals seem to have died together when the water they lived in dried up.

By 2026 nobody had found a fully grown *Acanthostega*.

## 2. Meganeura

**Label:** Meganeura
**Age:** 300 Ma · ± 3 Ma · from fossils and plants in the layers (Gzhelian, about 304 to 299 Ma); no date on the rock itself
**Tail:** none
**Known from:** wings and bodies pressed flat in the shale of coal mines
**Place now:** Commentry, Allier, France (about 46.29 N, 2.74 E, the town; the mines are closed)
**Kind:** crop or animal
**Drawing:** meganeura.svg

**Summary (36 words)**
About 300 million years ago. *Meganeura*, an insect like a dragonfly with wings about seventy centimetres across, hunted over the swamp forests of what is now central France. Why it grew so big is argued. *More*

**More (327 words)**

```
About 300 million years ago.
```

At the Muséum national d'Histoire naturelle in Paris is a fossil from the coal mines of Commentry, in central France. Pressed into the rock is an insect. Its wings are crossed with fine veins, like a dragonfly's, and from tip to tip they measured about seventy centimetres.

The fossil came out of the mines in 1880. In 1885 Charles Brongniart, who studied fossil insects, named it *Meganeura* ("meg-a-NEW-ra"), "big veins."

It was not a dragonfly. It belonged to a group of insects called griffinflies, which are now gone. Its close relatives had big eyes, strong jaws and spiny legs, good for snatching other insects out of the air.

Why was it so big?

For a long time the favourite answer was the air. In *Meganeura*'s time the air held more oxygen than it does now. An insect has no lungs. Air reaches its muscles through branching tubes, and the idea was that in a big insect the tubes could not carry enough oxygen, unless the air held more of it.

In 2026 a team led by Edward Snelling tested this. They looked at the flight muscles of forty-four kinds of flying insect, small and large, under an electron microscope, and measured how much room the finest air tubes took up. At every size it was about one part in a hundred, or less. In the heart muscle of birds and mammals, the finest blood vessels take up about ten times as much room. So an insect has plenty of room for more tubes, and the tubes in its flight muscles were not what kept insects small. Oxygen may still matter somewhere else in the body; that is not yet tested.

That leaves other answers. One is that nothing yet flew that could eat *Meganeura*: there were no birds, no bats and no flying reptiles. Another is that big kinds simply died out more easily. Nobody knows.

The biggest dragonflies alive today have wings about sixteen centimetres across.

## 3. Sinosauropteryx

**Label:** Sinosauropteryx
**Age:** 125 Ma · ± 1 Ma · uranium-lead dating of zircon crystals in volcanic ash in the same beds (125.46 ± 0.05 Ma, Zhong 2021)
**Tail:** none
**Known from:** a few skeletons pressed flat in the mud of old lakes, with a dark fringe along the back and tail
**Place now:** Sihetun, near Beipiao, Liaoning, China (about 41.59 N, 120.79 E, the dated section)
**Kind:** crop or animal
**Drawing:** sinosauropteryx.svg

**Summary (39 words)**
About 125 million years ago. *Sinosauropteryx*, a small meat-eating dinosaur, lived beside lakes in what is now Liaoning, China. Found in 1996, it was the first dinosaur, apart from birds, known to have a fringe of fine threads. *More*

**More (319 words)**

```
About 125 million years ago.
```

In August 1996, at the village of Sihetun, in Liaoning, in northeastern China, a farmer named Li Yumin split open a slab of fine grey rock and found a skeleton inside. The slab split into two halves, each holding one face of the skeleton, and the halves went to a museum in Beijing and an institute in Nanjing.

It was a small dinosaur, about the size of a turkey, that walked on two legs and had short arms with a big claw on each thumb. More than half its length was tail. All along its neck, back and tail ran a dark fringe of fine threads.

That October the palaeontologist Philip Currie, who was visiting China, saw it. "You can't come to any conclusion other than that they're feathers," he said.

They were not feathers like a bird's, with a stiff stem down the middle and a flat blade on each side. They were simple threads, one by one or in little tufts. Most people who study them think they are early feathers, from before feathers had blades, and long before anything flew with them.

The animal was named *Sinosauropteryx* ("sine-oh-sore-OP-ter-iks"), "Chinese lizard wing." One skeleton has a lizard inside its ribs, its last meal, and two eggs, so it was probably a female.

Did it have colours? In 2017 a team led by Fiann Smithwick mapped where dark pigment was kept on three skeletons (one has since been given a name of its own). They read a dark back and a pale belly, a dark band across the eyes like a mask, and stripes on the tail. Not everyone agrees. Mary Schweitzer, who studies what is left of soft tissue in fossils, said the evidence was not strong enough, and that dark patches could have shifted as the body rotted.

The two halves of the first slab are still in China, one in Beijing and one in Nanjing.

## 4. Repenomamus

**Label:** Repenomamus
**Age:** 125.7 Ma · ± 0.1 Ma · uranium-lead dating of zircon crystals in volcanic ash at the bottom and top of the beds (Zhong 2021)
**Tail:** none
**Known from:** whole skeletons buried by volcanic flows, one with its last meal inside
**Place now:** Lujiatun, near Beipiao, Liaoning, China (about 41.61 N, 120.91 E, the 2012 find, as published)
**Kind:** crop or animal
**Drawing:** repenomamus.svg

**Summary (38 words)**
About 125.7 million years ago. *Repenomamus*, a meat-eating mammal, lived among dinosaurs in what is now Liaoning, China. The biggest kind was the size of a badger, and one skeleton has a young dinosaur in its stomach. *More*

**More (299 words)**

```
About 125.7 million years ago.
```

Near the village of Lujiatun, in Liaoning, in northeastern China, there are beds of volcanic rock where animals were buried whole. Flows of volcanic ash and water swept over them so quickly that many skeletons are whole, and some lie curled up as if asleep.

One of these skeletons is a mammal about as big as a large house cat, with a long body, short legs and sharp front teeth. Inside its ribs, where its stomach was, is a small heap of bones. Some of them are still joined together. They belong to a young *Psittacosaurus* ("sit-AK-o-SORE-us"), a plant-eating dinosaur with a beak like a parrot's. It had been torn into pieces and swallowed.

The mammal is *Repenomamus* ("rep-EN-o-MAY-mus"). In 2005 a team led by Hu Yaoming described what was in the stomach, and also a second, bigger kind, about a metre long and as heavy as a badger. The bigger kind was the largest mammal then known from the time of the dinosaurs.

Did *Repenomamus* hunt the young dinosaur, or find it dead? Nobody can tell from the bones.

In 2023 a team led by Han Gang described another fossil from the same beds. A *Repenomamus* lies wrapped around a *Psittacosaurus* about three times its weight. One of the mammal's paws grips the dinosaur's jaw, a back foot grips the dinosaur's leg, and the mammal's teeth are in the dinosaur's ribs. The team argued that the mammal was attacking when the mudflow buried them both. Others think the dinosaur may already have been dead, and the two tumbled together in the flow.

Either way, mammals in the time of dinosaurs were not all small and hiding. Some ate dinosaurs.

*Repenomamus* belonged to a branch of the mammals that left no descendants. None of its kind is alive today.

**Pictures wanted:** the drawing (repenomamus.svg); the 2023 fossil (Han 2023, *Scientific Reports*, open access, probably CC BY 4.0; licence to check).

## 5. Pakicetus

**Label:** Pakicetus
**Age:** 49 Ma · ± 3 Ma · from the fossils in the layers (Kuldana Formation, early to middle Eocene); no date on the rock itself
**Tail:** none
**Known from:** a skull, and the jumbled bones of many animals from an old stream bed
**Place now:** Chorlakki, Kohat District, Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan (about 33.62 N, 71.92 E, the fossil site)
**Kind:** crop or animal
**Drawing:** pakicetus.svg

**Summary (34 words)**
About 50 million years ago. *Pakicetus*, a long-tailed animal the size of a wolf, lived beside streams in what is now Pakistan. It walked on four legs, but its ears were a whale's. *More*

**More (306 words)**

```
About 50 million years ago.
```

In 1978, in the dry hills of Kohat, in what is now Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, in Pakistan, a team led by the palaeontologist Philip Gingerich, of the University of Michigan, found part of a skull. There were no legs and no body, only the back of the head and some teeth. But the skull was enough.

Inside the ear was a bone with a thick, curled lip. Every whale and dolphin alive has an ear bone with this lip, and almost nothing else does. In 1981 Gingerich and Donald Russell named the animal *Pakicetus* ("PAK-i-SEE-tus"), "whale from Pakistan."

So was it a whale?

Twenty years later, in the Kala Chitta Hills, also in northern Pakistan, a team led by Hans Thewissen dug skeletons from the bed of an old stream. The bones were jumbled together, many animals at once. Put back together, *Pakicetus* looked something like a wolf with a long snout and a long tail. Its legs were long and thin. It had no flippers.

Its ankle bone had grooves on both ends, like a pulley with two wheels. That ankle is found in deer, pigs, cows, camels and hippos, the animals with an even number of toes on each foot. So whales belong with them.

How did *Pakicetus* live? Its eyes sat high on its head, close together, as a hippo's do. Its leg bones were dense and heavy, which helps an animal stay down in water. And the chemistry of its teeth says it drank fresh water. It probably waded in shallow streams that dried into pools for much of the year, catching fish and whatever else it could.

Whales that came after it went back into the sea. Within about ten million years, whales had flippers, and back legs too small to walk on.

Today the closest living relatives of whales are hippos.
