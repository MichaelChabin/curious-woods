## 1. Dufuna canoe

**Label:** Dufuna canoe
**Year:** −6200 · precision: century (two radiocarbon dates, between about 6500 and 6000 BCE) · kind: object
**Place:** Dufuna, on the Komadugu Gana river, Fune, Yobe State, Nigeria (about 12.3 N, 11.1 E; approximate)

**Summary (39 words)**
About 6200 BCE (about 8,200 years ago). Someone hollowed a mahogany trunk into a canoe more than eight metres long, in what is now north-east Nigeria. A herder digging a well found it in 1987. It is the oldest boat found in Africa. *More*

**More (346 words)**

```
About 6200 BCE (about 8,200 years ago)
```

In May 1987 a Fulani cattle herder, Mallam Ya'u, was digging a well at Dufuna, a village beside the Komadugu Gana river in Yobe State, in the dry north-east of Nigeria. Four and a half metres down he struck something hard. It was wood, and he reported it.

Archaeologists from the University of Maiduguri took chips of the wood to be dated, and in 1994 they dug with the people of Dufuna and with Peter Breunig, an archaeologist from Frankfurt, until the whole thing lay open. It was a canoe cut from a single trunk of African mahogany, 8.4 metres long and only half a metre wide, with sides about five centimetres thick. Both ends had been worked to a point, and the canoe was almost whole.

Buried wood usually rots. This wood lay under clay and sand, so deep that the ground around it was always soaked, and the water had kept it. Two laboratories dated it by radiocarbon, a way of telling the age of anything once alive from a kind of carbon in it that slowly fades away, and their answers fall between about 6500 and 6000 BCE.

For several thousand years rain fell much farther north in Africa than it does now, the Sahara was green, and Lake Chad, which is small today, was about as big as the Caspian Sea, the largest lake there is. Its shore came within about fifty kilometres of Dufuna, and the canoe lay on the flood plain of the river, where the people who made it probably fished.

Nothing was found in the canoe or beside it. The archaeologists think its makers worked with axes of chipped stone, and they point out that a boat this well made was probably not anyone's first try, so people there had probably been building boats long before.

The canoe was buried again until there was a way to keep it, and in 1998 it was lifted on a stretcher of tree trunks and palm-leaf rope and driven to Damaturu, the state capital, where it was still kept in 2026.

## 2. Budj Bim

**Label:** Budj Bim
**Year:** -4600 · precision: century · kind: craft or invention
**Place:** Tae Rak (Lake Condah), Gunditjmara country, south-western Victoria, Australia (38.06 S, 141.83 E)

**Summary (37 words)**
About 4600 BCE (about 6,600 years ago). On Gunditjmara country in what is now south-eastern Australia, people cut a channel through hard volcanic rock to steer water and catch eels. It is one of the oldest fish traps anyone has dated. *More*

**More (335 words)**

```
About 4600 BCE (about 6,600 years ago)
```

Budj Bim is a volcano in the south-west of Victoria, in Australia. Geologists date its eruption to about thirty-seven thousand years ago. The lava ran some fifty kilometres to the sea and left a floor of black rock full of hollows that water collects in. One is a shallow lake the Gunditjmara call Tae Rak.

Short-finned eels, which the Gunditjmara call kooyang, come in from the sea up the creeks and grow in those wetlands for years before swimming out. Gunditjmara people cut channels through the rock to send water where they wanted it, and the eels went with it. They built low stone walls across the channels and set woven baskets in the gaps, so an eel swimming down a channel swam into a basket. Some channels run two hundred metres or more. Eels were kept alive in ponds and taken later.

The Gunditjmara's own organisation, Gunditj Mirring, worked with archaeologists from Monash University at a buried part of the system called Muldoons, and is named as an author of their report. They dug out one channel, cut into the solid rock and filled with flood mud. In the mud were bits of charcoal, which can be dated. The charcoal said the channel was already there about six thousand six hundred years ago.

That is the youngest the channel can be; it could be older. Stone blocks were added later to raise the walls, within the last six to eight hundred years, so the trap was being rebuilt thousands of years after its channel was cut.

Around the lake stand dozens of rings of stone, which archaeologists read as the bases of houses.

In 2019 the Gunditjmara had Budj Bim put on the World Heritage list, the United Nations' list of places that matter to the whole world. It was the first place in Australia listed for Aboriginal culture alone. They first asked in 1989. As of 2026 they look after the land themselves, some of it bought back a piece at a time.

No picture of a burial or a sacred place, and nothing used without the Traditional Owners' say.

## 3. Belitung wreck

**Label:** Belitung wreck
**Year:** 830 · precision: decade · kind: object
**Place:** off Tanjung Pandan, Belitung, Indonesia (2.75 S, 107.61 E)

**Summary (33 words)**
About 830 (about 1,200 years ago). A ship whose planks were sewn together with cord sank on a reef off Belitung Island, in Indonesia, carrying about seventy thousand pieces of Chinese pottery. Divers found it in 1998. *More*

**More (349 words)**

```
About 830 (about 1,200 years ago)
```

In 1998 men diving for sea cucumbers off Belitung ("buh-LEE-toong"), an island in Indonesia, found a mound on the sea floor seventeen metres down. It was pottery. Under it lay a ship that had struck a reef of black rock called Batu Hitam.

The ship was not built the way Chinese ships were. Its planks had no iron nails. They were stitched edge to edge with cord, through holes drilled five or six centimetres apart. The hull was about eighteen metres long, its timbers mostly a hardwood called *Afzelia*, with cross-beams of teak. Michael Flecker, the archaeologist who wrote the report on the wreck, takes the *Afzelia* for African kinds and argues the ship was built in Arabia, perhaps Oman or Yemen. Stephen Haw, a historian of China, reads the same wood differently, since *Afzelia* grows in South-East Asia too, and puts the building nearer to where the ship sank.

Divers brought up about sixty thousand pieces of pottery, most of them bowls from the kilns at Tongguan, near Changsha, where potters turned them out in quantity for sale abroad, many painted with a few quick brushstrokes. Many others had broken, so the ship probably loaded something like seventy thousand. The bowls were stacked in spirals inside big jars, packed so tightly that most came up whole.

One bowl carries a date: the sixteenth day of the seventh month of the second year of Baoli, as years were then named in China, which is 826. That is when the bowl was made. The ship sank later, probably before 850.

There was ten tonnes of lead in bars, twenty-nine bronze mirrors, silver flasks and boxes, gold dishes, an eight-sided gold cup, and jars packed with star anise, a spice shaped like a star.

Nobody knows where the ship was bound, or whose the cargo was, or who was aboard. A salvage company raised the cargo, not a museum, and archaeologists have argued about that ever since: a slower dig might have answered those questions. As of 2026 most of what came up is in the Asian Civilisations Museum in Singapore.

## 4. Jikji

**Label:** Jikji
**Year:** 1377 · precision: year (the seventh lunar month of 1377; the day is blank) · kind: text
**Place:** the site of Heungdeok temple, Uncheon-dong, Cheongju, South Korea (36.64 N, 127.47 E)

**Summary (39 words)**
1377 (649 years ago). At Heungdeok temple, outside Cheongju in Korea, a book of Buddhist teachings was printed from single characters cast in metal. One volume survives, in Paris. It is the oldest book printed from movable metal type that we have. *More*

**More (350 words)**

```
1377 (649 years ago)
```

On the last page of a thin paper book a line of Chinese characters says that the book was printed from cast type at Heungdeok temple ("HUNG-duk"), outside Cheongju ("CHUNG-joo"), in the seventh month of 1377. The space for the day is blank.

The book is called *Jikji* ("JIK-jee"). A Buddhist monk named Baegun gathered it in 1372 from the sayings of earlier teachers, and after he died two of his pupils, Seokchan and Daljam, had it printed, with money given by a nun named Myodeok.

Books in Korea were written in Chinese characters then, and most were printed from wooden blocks, a whole page carved on each block. This book was made another way, from single characters, each cast by pouring melted metal into a mould, which were set side by side, inked, printed and taken apart to be used again. The pages show it. Here and there a character is upside down, one leans, and one that was left out has been written in by hand.

Korean writings mention earlier books printed from metal type, in the 1230s, long before Johannes Gutenberg printed his Bible from metal type in Germany, but no copy that everyone accepts has survived. *Jikji* itself had two volumes, and the first has never been found.

The second volume left Korea with Victor Collin de Plancy, a French diplomat in Seoul, who bought it; nobody recorded where or when. Henri Vever, a jeweller, bought it at an auction in 1911 for 180 francs and left it to France's national library. At the library Park Byeongseon, a Korean librarian, picked it out from Korean books nobody had catalogued, and in 1972 the book was put on show.

In 1985, people preparing ground for building in Cheongju uncovered the remains of a temple, and with them a bronze gong engraved with the name Heungdeok. A museum of early printing was built on the spot.

As of 2026 the book is still in Paris. Many people in Korea have asked for it to come home, and the library has answered that it is safest where it is.

On 1 April 2008 Kapaandu Shatika, who worked for the diamond-mining company Namdeb, came on some lumps of metal shaped like half a ball, and some metal pipes, in the floor of a pit near Oranjemund ("oh-RAHN-yuh-moont"), in the far south of Namibia. The pit was sea floor. Namdeb mines diamonds from that shore by pushing up a great wall of sand to hold back the Atlantic and then digging out the ground behind the wall, seven or eight metres below the level of the sea.

The half-balls were copper and the pipes were cannon. Under the sand lay the timbers of a ship and more than forty tonnes of its cargo: 1,845 copper ingots, more than a hundred elephant tusks, sword blades, and more than two thousand gold coins.

Some of the coins are of a kind made in Portugal only between 1525 and 1538, and Portuguese records list a ship called the *Bom Jesus* that left Lisbon for India in March 1533 and was lost near the southern end of Africa. So this is probably that ship, though nothing found carries its name.

The copper bears the trident mark of the Fuggers ("FOOG-ers"), a family of merchants in Augsburg, in Germany, who mined copper in what is now Slovakia. In 2020 a team of geneticists and archaeologists read the DNA in forty-four of the tusks. All came from forest elephants of West Africa, from at least seventeen herds, and only four of the seventeen family lines have been found in elephants alive today.

The archaeologists had to hurry, because the sea was eating the sand wall. Of the crew they found only some toe bones inside a shoe, and very few belongings, so they think that many of the men got ashore. Nobody knows what became of those men.

Portugal, whose king had owned the ship, left everything to Namibia. In 2024 the museum in Oranjemund opened a room about the ship, with a few of the finds. In 2026 a museum for the whole find was still only a plan.

## 6. Sequoyah

**Label:** Sequoyah
**Year:** 1821 · precision: year · kind: text

**Summary (37 words)**
About 1821 (about 210 years ago). Sequoyah, a Cherokee silversmith who had never learned to read, finished a set of 86 signs, one for most of the syllables in Cherokee, so that Cherokee could be written in signs of its own. *More*

**More (350 words)**

Sequoyah ("se-KWOY-uh") was a Cherokee silversmith and blacksmith who lived at Willstown, in what is now north-east Alabama. He had watched soldiers and traders read letters, which later accounts say he called talking leaves. He could not read a word of English, and Cherokee had never been written in signs of its own.

He began about 1809 by drawing a picture for every word. There were too many words. Cherokee speech is built out of syllables, short pieces of sound like *ga*, *ne* and *si*, and there are not many different ones. Give nearly every syllable a sign, and any Cherokee word can be written in a row of signs.

He worked at it for years while neighbours thought he was wasting his time; his wife is said to have burned his early drawings.

By about 1821 he had 86 signs, and one was dropped later, leaving the 85 still in use. Some look like letters from English, Greek and Hebrew books, though they stand for nothing like the same sounds; a sign shaped like the English S means *du*. Scholars argue how much he took from those books and how much he drew.

Later accounts tell how he taught the signs to his young daughter Ayokeh, and how the two showed the Cherokee leaders what the signs could do: one would leave the room while the other wrote down what was said, then come back and read it. The leaders gave him a medal in 1824, and the Cherokee Nation took the signs up the year after.

A Cherokee speaker has no spellings to learn: the signs give a word's syllables, and knowing the language does the rest. Within a few years people were sending each other letters, and in 1828 the Nation began printing a newspaper, the *Cherokee Phoenix*, in Cherokee and English, from type cast for his signs in Boston.

Nobody knows exactly when he finished the set, or where; the accounts disagree.

As of 2026 Cherokee is still written in his signs, and the Cherokee Nation's immersion school in Tahlequah, Oklahoma, teaches every lesson in Cherokee.

## 7. Sobral eclipse

**Year:** 1919 · precision: exact (29 May 1919) · kind: sky
**Place:** the Jockey Club racecourse, Sobral, Ceará, Brazil (3.69 S, 40.35 W)

**Summary (39 words)**
29 May 1919 (107 years ago). At Sobral, in Brazil, two astronomers from England photographed the stars around an eclipsed Sun. In their photographs the stars had shifted by about as much as Albert Einstein said the Sun would bend their light. *More*

**More (350 words)**

On the morning of 29 May 1919 the sky over Sobral ("so-BROW"), a town in Ceará, in north-east Brazil, was cloudier than usual. On the racecourse of the Jockey Club two telescopes lay on their sides, each fed by a mirror that turned to follow the Sun. About a minute before the Moon covered the Sun, a gap opened in the cloud.

The telescopes were worked by Andrew Crommelin and Charles Davidson, astronomers from the Royal Observatory at Greenwich, who had reached Sobral by steamer and train a month before. Vicente Saboya, a politician elected for Sobral, lent them his house. In the square in front of the Patrocínio church, Henrique Morize and astronomers from Brazil's Observatório Nacional had set up to study the corona, the Sun's pale outer glow.

Crommelin and Davidson wanted the stars. In 1915 Albert Einstein, a physicist in Berlin, had published a theory of gravity which said that light passing close to the Sun is pulled off its straight path, so that a star seen near the Sun's edge would seem to sit a tiny distance from its usual place. An older way of reckoning gave half as much bending. Stars near the Sun can be photographed only during a total eclipse, and this one lasted about five minutes, in front of a bright cluster of stars called the Hyades.

The larger telescope's photographs were blurred, probably because the Sun's heat had warped its mirror, but the smaller telescope, with a lens four inches across, gave seven sharp photographs on glass plates, with seven stars on each.

In July Crommelin and Davidson photographed the same stars from Sobral at night, with the Sun out of the way. At Greenwich the two sets of plates were measured and compared, and the stars had moved by a few hundredths of a millimetre at most. That was close to what Einstein had said and about twice the older figure.

The results were announced in London on 6 November 1919. In the late 1970s the small telescope's plates were measured again, by machine, and gave nearly the same answer.

## 8. Coelacanth

On 22 December 1938 the trawler *Nerine* came into the harbour at East London, on the east coast of South Africa, with a heap of fish nobody would buy. Her skipper, Hendrik Goosen, kept anything odd for Marjorie Courtenay-Latimer, who ran the town's small museum. His crew telephoned her, and she came down to the docks, partly to wish them a happy Christmas.

Near the bottom of the heap was a fish about a metre and a half long, pale blue with faint white flecks, its scales hard and its fins stiff, more like short legs. No book at her museum showed it.

The town mortuary would not take it, nor the cold store, so a taxidermist named Robert Center wrapped it in newspaper soaked in formalin, which stops flesh rotting. It was rotting anyway in the summer heat — December is summer in the Eastern Cape — and after five days she let Center skin it.

The day after the catch she had sent a letter and a sketch to J. L. B. Smith, a chemistry lecturer at Rhodes University College, a hundred and seventy kilometres away, who was the country's authority on fish. He was away for the holidays. He read the letter on 3 January, and when he reached East London in February and saw the mounted skin he had no doubt. It was a coelacanth ("SEE-luh-kanth"), a kind of fish known only from fossils, none of them younger than about sixty-six million years.

Smith announced it in the journal *Nature* in March 1939 and named it *Latimeria chalumnae*, after her and after the river off whose mouth the trawler had worked, the Chalumna, or Tyolomnqa.

Fourteen years passed before Smith saw a second, caught off Anjouan in the Comoro Islands. Fishermen there called it gombessa and brought in two or three most years.

Coelacanths rest by day in caves in steep rock, mostly between a hundred and fifty and three hundred metres down, and hunt at night. As of 2026 two living kinds are known: this one and a second found in Indonesia in 1997.

## 9. Monarch winter

Cerro Pelón is a mountain in the State of Mexico, west of Mexico City, and its upper slopes carry oyamel firs, a tree of high cold ground. On 2 January 1975 Catalina Aguado, who had grown up in Michoacán, climbed it with her husband Ken Brugger and a man from a village below, hired to carry water and gear. She reached the top first. The trunks and branches there were orange and black. Monarchs were packed on them in their millions, hanging still in the cold.

Monarchs spend the summer across the eastern United States and southern Canada, and every autumn they fly south. Nobody in those countries knew where they went. Fred Urquhart, a zoologist in Toronto, and his wife Norah, who ran the part of the work that depended on strangers, had spent years sticking small numbered tags on monarchs' wings and asking whoever found one to post it back. The tags showed the butterflies heading south-west and crossing Texas, and there the trail stopped. Norah Urquhart had a notice put in a Mexico City newspaper asking for help, and Brugger answered it in the winter of 1972 to 1973.

He and Aguado searched two winters, driving the mountain roads in a Winnebago and asking what people had seen.

What they found that January was news in Toronto, not on the mountain. People living on those slopes had watched the butterflies arrive every autumn for as long as anyone could remember; the monarchs reach that part of Mexico in early November, when families keep the Day of the Dead.

Aguado and Brugger telephoned Toronto a week later. The Urquharts came the following winter, and Fred Urquhart wrote the story for *National Geographic* in August 1976, with Aguado on the cover, standing among the butterflies. He did not print the name of the mountain; he said secrecy would protect the butterflies.

As of 2026 the woods where the monarchs winter are a reserve of about 56,000 hectares. Most of that land belongs to the villages and Indigenous communities who live there, and the government manages it with them.

## 10. Ozone hole

**Label:** Ozone hole
**Year:** 1985 · precision: exact (16 May 1985) · kind: earth
**Place:** Halley research station, Brunt Ice Shelf, Antarctica (75.58 S, 26.22 W)

**Summary (37 words)**
16 May 1985 (41 years ago). Joe Farman, Brian Gardiner and Jonathan Shanklin reported that each spring the ozone high above Halley station, in Antarctica, was thinning, and that about a third had gone. Ozone absorbs the Sun's ultraviolet light. *More*

**More (350 words)**

```
16 May 1985 (41 years ago)
```

At Halley, a research station on a floating shelf of ice at the edge of Antarctica, instruments called Dobson spectrophotometers had been pointed at the Sun since 1956. Each compares two kinds of ultraviolet light, which the eye cannot see: one kind that ozone soaks up and one that ozone mostly lets through. From the difference it tells how much ozone lies overhead.

Ozone is a form of oxygen with three atoms instead of two. It is thickest between about seventeen and twenty-five kilometres up, and there is little of it: brought down to the ground it would make a layer about three millimetres thick. That layer absorbs ultraviolet light that would burn skin and damage living cells.

In 1983 Jonathan Shanklin, of the British Antarctic Survey, was putting years of Halley's readings into a computer. He meant to draw a graph for visitors showing that the ozone was the same as twenty years before. But the readings for spring, which south of the equator begins in September, looked lower. He went through the record with Brian Gardiner, who checked the figures, and Joe Farman, the physicist who led their group. Since the 1960s about a third of the October ozone had gone.

They published on 16 May 1985. The next year, maps made from an American satellite showed the thin patch reaching across Antarctica, and people called it the ozone hole, though no part of the sky is empty of ozone.

The cause was chlorine from CFCs, gases made for spray cans and refrigerators. In the dark Antarctic winter, clouds of ice form, and on the ice the chlorine changes into a form that the returning sunlight sets loose. One atom of chlorine can destroy thousands of ozone molecules.

In 1987, at Montreal in Canada, countries agreed to cut the gases, and later to stop making them, and in time every country in the United Nations joined. The hole still opens every spring, and in 2026 it was the largest in ten years. Atmospheric chemists expect the ozone over Antarctica to be back to its 1980 amount around 2066.
