## Colour (agreed; mock-up liked, 19 Oct)

- **The drained sea floor in neutral gray**, shaded relief, no hue. This is the common convention in research maps of the floor: gray is the stage and the data (age, stripes) goes on top in colour. Middle gray on the shelves, darkening to near-black in the trenches, never light enough to read as ice. Replaces the dark blue, which just looked like a map of the ocean.
- **The glassy water stays**, drawn over the gray where water remains, but thinner than in the mock-up, which read as slate.
- **High land never goes white.** The top of the land ramp is a darker, warm bare rock; the mock-up's first "pale rock" still read as snow on Tibet.
- **Ice is its own thing:** white with a faint blue and a fine texture, unmistakable, and on over Greenland and Antarctica.
- **Later, a layer of its own:** *What it's made of.* Fresh basalt is black at the ridges; older floor is buried under paler sediment, thicker with age (published sediment-thickness grid).
- On the bench, as toggles: gray floor, material floor, the current blue.
- Mock-up: drain-the-sea-colour-mock.png (sent in chat, 19 Oct): today, 2,500 m lower, drained.

## The spreading ridges: on from the start (Michael, 19 Oct)

The network of spreading ridges is one of the most important ideas in geophysics, and the child must see it. So it is **on by default**, not something she has to find.

- **A thin orange line along every spreading ridge**, drawn as a soft glow rather than a hard stroke. Map-makers already draw spreading ridges in red or orange, so this follows convention.
- **Honest meaning:** molten rock sits a kilometre or two under the ridge all along it, and new floor freezes there; eruptions on the floor come and go. The line means *new floor is being made here now*, not a river of lava. That sentence is available if she asks (probe readout or *More*), never pressed on her.
- **Arrows for plate motion**, from the plate model (measured by GPS, a few centimetres a year).
- **Both can be turned off** with words in the left column, *Ridges* and *Arrows*.
- Source for both: the plate-boundary model already planned for the map.

## Curiosity, for an "if" story

If the sea came up to the top of Everest (8,849 m) and was then drained, the water would make a ball about 2,200 km across, close to the size of Pluto (about 2,380 km). That's about four times all the water there is today. Rough: shell of 8.85 km over the whole Earth, minus the land above today's sea level, plus today's ocean. Four times the water, but only about 1.6 times as wide.
