## 1. Stonehenge

**Label:** Stonehenge
**Year:** about −2500 · precision: century · kind: object
**Place:** Stonehenge, Wiltshire, England (51.18 N, 1.83 W)
**Replaces:** `stonehenge`

**Summary (36 words)**
About 2500 BCE. On a plain in southern England, people raised a ring of giant stones. The stones came from three places: a wood nearby, hills in Wales, and probably the far northeast of Scotland. *More*

**More (343 words)**

```
About 7,500 years after the ice
      or about 2500 BCE
            or about 4,500 years ago.
```

On a grassy plain in the south of England, a ring of huge grey stones stands against the sky. The tallest are higher than a two-storey house, and some pairs carry a third stone laid across their tops. This is Stonehenge. Its biggest stones were raised about 4,500 years ago.

Those big stones are called sarsens ("SAR-senz"). A typical one weighs about twenty tonnes, as much as three big elephants. In 2020 a team led by the geographer David Nash compared the chemistry of the stones with sarsen boulders from all over southern England, and found that most of the big stones match a single place, West Woods, about 25 kilometres to the north.

The smaller stones inside the ring, called bluestones, came much farther. They were brought from the Preseli Hills in Wales, about 230 kilometres away.

Then, in 2024, came a surprise. Deep inside the ring lies a flat stone of about six tonnes, called the Altar Stone. When scientists dated tiny mineral grains in chips of the stone, the grains matched rock in the far northeast of Scotland, more than 750 kilometres away. Some scientists have since questioned that, and the argument is not over.

How do you move a six-tonne stone 750 kilometres? Nobody knows. The people who built Stonehenge had no writing and left no record. Some think the Altar Stone came around the coast by boat; others think it was dragged over land on wooden sledges. Nobody knows, either, why anyone would bring stones so far when there was stone close at hand.

The builders did leave one clue to what they had in mind, because the stones are lined up with the Sun. On the longest day of the year, the Sun rises just to the left of a big stone outside the ring, called the Heel Stone. On the shortest day, the Sun once set between the two uprights of the tallest pair.

The Altar Stone from Scotland still lies in the middle of the ring, under two stones that fell from the tallest pair.

Research pass: sarsens average about 20–25 t (Nash 2020; English Heritage gives 25 tons); 50 of 52 match West Woods, about 25 km north. Bluestones from Carn Goedog and Craig Rhos-y-felin, about 230 km (Parker Pearson 2019; English Heritage says 240). Altar Stone about 6 t, from the Orcadian Basin, at least 750 km (Clarke 2024). ## 2. Enheduanna

**Label:** Enheduanna
**Year:** about −2300 · precision: century · kind: person
**Place:** Ur (Tell el-Muqayyar), southern Iraq (30.96 N, 46.10 E)
**Replaces:** `enhed`

**Summary (40 words)**
About 2300 BCE. At Ur, in what is now Iraq, a king's daughter named Enheduanna was high priestess of the moon god. Poems copied centuries later carry her name. She may be the first author whose name we know. *More*

**More (293 words)**

```
About 7,700 years after the ice
      or about 2300 BCE
            or about 4,300 years ago.
```

A round stone about as wide as a dinner plate was dug up in pieces at Ur, in what is now southern Iraq. On one side, a row of people are carved making an offering. On the other side is writing that names Enheduanna ("en-hed-oo-AH-na"), daughter of King Sargon, and scholars think she is one of the people in the row.

Sargon had conquered all of southern Iraq and more, and he made his daughter high priestess of the moon god at Ur. She is remembered, though, for something else.

A collection of forty-two short hymns, one for each of the great temples of the land, ends with these lines:

> The compiler of the tablets was Enheduanna. My king, something has been created that no one has created before.

A longer poem is spoken in her own voice. In it, a man named Lugal-Ane has driven her out of her temple. "He made me fly like a swallow from the window," she says. By the end of the poem, the goddess Inanna is on her side again.

Did she write these poems? Nobody can be sure. Every copy we have was made about five hundred years after her lifetime, most of them by students practising their writing. Some scholars, such as William Hallo, take her name on the poems as a sign that she wrote them. Others think later writers put a famous name on the poems to make them grander.

If she did write them, she is the first author anywhere whose name we know. Older poems exist, but none of them carries an author's name.

The round stone has been put back together, and it is in a museum in Philadelphia, in the United States. Her name is still on it.

The old blurb's "the oldest writing anywhere that says: I made this" is an overclaim and is gone; the colophon is in the third person ("the compiler… was En-hedu-ana"). Sargon reigned about 2334–2279 BCE (middle chronology). 42 Temple Hymns; colophon lines 543–544 (ETCSL). Lugal-Ane and the swallow line (ETCSL). Religion: high priestess and moon god stated as facts. ## 3. First alphabet

**Label:** First alphabet
**Year:** about −1850 · precision: century · kind: text
**Place:** Serabit el-Khadim, Sinai, Egypt (29.04 N, 33.46 E)
**Replaces:** `alphabet`

**Summary (38 words)**
About 1850 BCE. At turquoise mines in Egypt's Sinai desert, people who did not write Egyptian borrowed a few Egyptian pictures and used each one for a single sound. Most alphabets used today come from their idea. *More*

**More (348 words)**

```
About 8,150 years after the ice
      or about 1850 BCE
            or about 3,900 years ago.
```

A little stone sphinx, a bit longer than a pencil, is kept at the British Museum in London. On its right shoulder is a line of Egyptian writing, made of tiny pictures, saying that the sphinx is "beloved of Hathor, mistress of turquoise." Hathor was an Egyptian goddess. On its left shoulder and its base are other marks. Some of them look like Egyptian pictures, but they are not Egyptian writing.

The sphinx was found at Serabit el-Khadim ("seh-RAH-beet el-KHAH-dim"), in the Sinai desert, where the kings of Egypt sent workers to dig turquoise, a blue-green stone.

In 1916 an Egyptologist named Alan Gardiner studied the strange marks and found a word in them. It was written with four signs, the first of them a house, and when he read the signs as sounds, they spelled the name of a goddess, Baalat, "the Lady."

How had pictures turned into sounds?

Egyptian writing used hundreds of signs. Some stood for whole words and some for sounds, and it took years to learn them all. The new writing kept only a couple of dozen pictures and used each one for the first sound of its name in the writers' own language. Their word for house began with the sound b, so a house stood for b. Their word for water began with m, so wavy water stood for m. With so few signs to learn, almost anyone could learn to write.

Who had the idea? The Egyptologist Orly Goldwasser thinks it was the workers at the mines, who could not read Egyptian. Others think the writers were people from Canaan, the land to the northeast, who worked alongside the Egyptians and knew something of their writing. Marks of the same kind, found beside a desert road in Egypt called Wadi el-Hol, may be just as old, and nobody knows for sure where it began.

Over many hundreds of years the signs changed shape again and again, as traders passed them along from one people to another. The house became the letter B on this page, and the water became M.

## 4. Oracle bones

**Label:** Oracle bones
**Year:** about −1200 · precision: century · kind: text
**Place:** Yinxu, Anyang, Henan, China (36.13 N, 114.31 E)
**Replaces:** `oracle`

**Summary (38 words)**
About 1200 BCE. At Anyang, in China, the kings of the Shang carved their questions to their ancestors on bones and turtle shells. The writing is already fully grown. Today's Chinese characters come straight down from it. *More*

**More (350 words)**

```
About 8,800 years after the ice
      or about 1200 BCE
            or about 3,200 years ago.
```

On the inside of a turtle's flat undershell, someone has cut neat rows of small hollows and scorched each one. On the outside, a crack runs from each scorched spot, and next to the cracks someone has carved a question: Will it rain? Will the harvest be good? Is one of the king's ancestors causing his toothache?

This is how the kings of the Shang asked their dead ancestors for advice, about 3,200 years ago, at Anyang ("AHN-yahng") in northern China. The king's diviners pressed something red-hot into the hollows until the shell or bone cracked, and read the answer in the cracks. Then the question was carved beside the cracks. The Chinese character for this kind of divining, 卜, still looks like one of those cracks.

Well over a hundred thousand pieces of these bones and shells have been found. Their writing uses four to five thousand different characters, of which scholars can read about a quarter with certainty. The characters used in China today come straight down from the ones on the bones.

Was this the beginning of Chinese writing? Almost certainly not. The writing on the bones is a full writing system, with thousands of signs used with ease, and writing like that takes a long time to grow.

So where is the earlier writing? One of the characters on the bones gives a clue. The character for a book, 册 ("tsuh"), is a picture of strips of bamboo or wood tied together with cord, and people wrote on such strips with a brush. In Iraq, people pressed their writing into clay, which lasts for thousands of years. Bamboo rots, so if the Shang wrote a great deal on it, we may never know.

Specialists agree that the Chinese invented their writing themselves. It was not borrowed from anywhere else.

For years, farmers near Anyang dug up the bones and sold them as "dragon bones," to be ground into medicine. In 1899 a scholar named Wang Yirong recognised the marks on them as ancient Chinese writing. How many questions were ground into medicine before then, nobody knows.

## 5. Iron

**Label:** Iron
**Year:** about −900 · precision: century · kind: craft or invention
**Place:** Tell Hammeh, Jordan Valley, Jordan (32.2 N, 35.6 E, approximate), where early iron-making waste has been dated
**Replaces:** `iron`

**Summary (36 words)**
About 900 BCE. Around the eastern Mediterranean, making iron became common. Copper and tin, the metals of bronze, are rare. Iron is in rock almost everywhere. Metal no longer had to come from far away. *More*

**More (344 words)**

```
About 9,100 years after the ice
      or about 900 BCE
            or about 2,900 years ago.
```

In the tomb of Tutankhamun ("toot-ahnk-AH-mun"), a young king of Egypt who died about 3,350 years ago, lay a dagger with a gold handle and a blade of iron. The iron came from the sky. In 2016 scientists found nickel and cobalt in the blade, mixed with the iron the way they are mixed in meteorites, which are pieces of rock or metal from space that have reached the ground. In the young king's time iron was a treasure, and kings sent gifts of it to other kings.

About four hundred years later, at a place called Tell Hammeh in the Jordan Valley, people were making iron so often that they left heaps of waste behind. What had changed?

Not the iron. Iron is one of the most common things in the ground. About one twentieth of the Earth's rocky crust is iron, and it is what makes many rocks and soils red or brown. Copper is about a thousand times rarer, and tin more than twenty thousand times rarer.

That mattered, because for many centuries the best tools had been made of bronze, which is copper with a little tin. Tin is found in only a few places, so bronze-makers depended on traders who brought it from far away.

What changed was that people learned how to get iron out of rock. They heated the rock with charcoal in a small clay furnace. The iron did not melt. Instead it gathered into a glowing, spongy lump full of glassy waste, and the smith beat the lump with a hammer, again and again, to drive the waste out. Once people knew how to do it, a village could make its own metal from rock nearby, without waiting for tin from the far end of a trade route.

Was iron better than bronze? Not at first. Plain iron is no harder than good bronze, and smiths had to learn to turn it into steel and harden it before it was.

Tutankhamun's dagger is in the Grand Egyptian Museum, near Cairo. Its blade has hardly rusted.

**References**

- Daniela Comelli and others, "The meteoritic origin of Tutankhamun's iron dagger blade," *Meteoritics & Planetary Science*, 2016. Nickel and cobalt in the blade.
- Harald Alexander Veldhuijzen and Thilo Rehren, "Slags and the city: early iron production at Tell Hammeh, Jordan, and Tel Beth-Shemesh, Israel," in Susan La Niece, Duncan Hook and Paul Craddock, eds., *Metals and Mines: Studies in Archaeometallurgy* (Archetype and the British Museum, 2007), pp. 189–201. The waste at Tell Hammeh and its date.
- Historical Metallurgy Society, "Bloomery" (historicalmetallurgy.org). How the furnace worked.

Left out: the tin-shortage theory (Snodgrass) and its critics (Muhly): a second argument. For the checkers: "kings sent iron gifts to other kings" (the Amarna letters: Tushratta of Mitanni to Amenhotep III); Tutankhamun's dagger "wrapped with his body" (found on his right thigh, within the wrappings); "hardly rusted"; which museum holds it now; Tell Hammeh's coordinates and the *Metals and Mines* citation; Veldhuijzen's full name.

## 6. Olmec heads

**Label:** Olmec heads
**Year:** about −1100 · precision: century · kind: object
**Place:** San Lorenzo, Veracruz, Mexico (17.75 N, 94.76 W, approximate)
**Replaces:** `olmec`

**Summary (34 words)**
Before 900 BCE. Near the Gulf coast of Mexico, a people we call the Olmec carved giant stone heads, each a different face in a different helmet. Nobody knows whose faces they are. *More*

**More (300 words)**

```
About 8,900 years after the ice
      or about 1100 BCE
            or about 3,100 years ago.
```

A stone face almost three metres tall looks out from a museum in Xalapa ("hah-LAH-pah"), in Mexico. It weighs about twenty-five tonnes. It has full cheeks, a broad, flat nose and thick lips, and it wears a close-fitting helmet.

Sixteen more heads like it have been found, seventeen in all, at four places near the Gulf of Mexico. Each has a different face, and each wears a different helmet with its own pattern. The smallest weighs about six tonnes and the biggest about forty.

They were made by a people we call the Olmec ("OHL-mek"), though that is not their own name. "Olmec" is a word from Nahuatl, the Aztecs' language, meaning "rubber people," which the Aztecs, more than two thousand years later, used for the people living near that coast in their own time. Nobody knows what the people who carved the heads called themselves.

The heads at San Lorenzo, the oldest of the four places, had been buried by about 900 BCE, so they were carved before then.

How were they moved? Most of the heads were carved from single boulders of basalt, a hard, dark volcanic rock, and the boulders came from the Tuxtla Mountains, a long way from where the heads were found. The Olmec had no horses or oxen to pull loads, and no wagons. Many archaeologists think they dragged the boulders to the rivers and floated them on rafts, but nobody knows for sure.

Whose faces are they? Most archaeologists think they are rulers. Around the shoulder of a wine jug found in a graveyard in Athens, someone has scratched a line of writing. It runs from right to left, the letters face backwards, and it says:

> Whoever of all these dancers now dances most gracefully…

Then it breaks off. The jug was probably a prize. It was made about 2,760 years ago, and its line is one of the oldest pieces of writing in the Greek alphabet.

The Greeks got their letters from the Phoenicians ("fuh-NEE-shunz"), traders and sailors from the coast of what is now Lebanon, whose letters had come down from the alphabet of the turquoise mines in Egypt. Greek writers, such as the historian Herodotus, called their letters "Phoenician letters."

But the Greeks had a problem. Phoenician letters were all consonants, sounds like b, d, k and m, with no letters for vowels like a, e and o. A Phoenician reader filled in the vowels from knowing the language, and a Greek reader could not do that so easily. Phoenician, though, also had some sounds that Greek did not use, so the Greeks had letters to spare, and they used the spare letters for vowels.

The first Phoenician letter, aleph, stood for a catch in the throat, like the stop in the middle of "uh-oh." The Greeks used it for the sound a and called it alpha, and in the same way other spare letters became e, i, o and u.

Who did this, and where? Nobody knows exactly. Many scholars think it happened on the island of Euboea ("yoo-BEE-uh"), at towns like Eretria ("eh-RET-ree-uh"), where some of the oldest Greek letters have been found. Some scholars think the vowels were invented only once, and copied everywhere from there.

Within fifty years, Greeks were scratching their names, boasts and jokes onto cups.

Alpha and beta, the first two Greek letters, gave us the word alphabet, and alpha became the A on this page.

A clay tablet holds a list of years. In Assyria, a year did not have a number; it had a name. Each year was named after an important official, and scribes kept long lists of the names, in order, with a few words about what happened in each year. One line reads:

> Bur-Sagale, governor of Guzana: revolt in the city of Ashur. In the month of Simanu, an eclipse of the Sun.

Bur-Sagale ("boor-sah-GAH-lay") was the official. Ashur was a city of Assyria, in what is now northern Iraq, and Simanu was a month in late spring and early summer.

An eclipse of the Sun happens when the Moon moves in front of it, and the Moon and the Earth move so regularly that astronomers can work out every eclipse there has ever been, and where its shadow fell. In 1867 a British scholar named Henry Rawlinson, who had helped work out how to read Assyrian writing, looked for an eclipse that would fit, and found one. On 15 June 763 BCE, late in the morning, the shadow of the Moon crossed northern Assyria, and along its path the day went dark.

Why does one line matter so much? Because the list is a chain, and once one link is fixed, the rest follow. If Bur-Sagale's year was 763 BCE, then the next year on the list was 762 BCE, the one after that 761, and so on, name after name, forwards and backwards. The list covers about three hundred years, and the eclipse gives a date to almost every one of them. Without it, the names would still be in order, but nobody could say when any of them was.

There is a check. A completely different list, of the kings of Babylon, kept much later by a Greek astronomer named Ptolemy, gives the same years for the kings who appear on both lists.

The eclipse that stopped a battle beside the Halys River, 178 years later, was dated the same way, by working backwards from the sky.

In a valley in the hills of northern Iraq, a long, broken wall of cut stone runs across the fields. It is about 280 metres long and 22 metres wide, and where it crossed a stream it once stood about nine metres high, with the stream running under it through pointed arches. On top of the wall ran water.

It is a bridge built to carry a canal, at a place called Jerwan ("jer-WAHN"). A channel built to carry water a long way is called an aqueduct. Most of this one ran along the ground, but at Jerwan, where it had to cross a valley, it ran on top of the bridge.

Who built it? His name is on it. Some of the blocks are carved with his words: "I had an aqueduct of white stone blocks made." The king was Sennacherib ("seh-NAK-er-ib"), who ruled Assyria about 2,700 years ago from his capital, Nineveh, a city beside the Tigris River. He wanted more water for the fields and meadows around Nineveh, so he had canals dug from the hills to the north to bring the water of mountain streams to the city. One of them ran about 95 kilometres, from a gorge called Khinis to Nineveh, and the bridge at Jerwan carried it across the valley.

How much stone did it take? In 1933 two archaeologists working for the Oriental Institute in Chicago, Thorkild Jacobsen and Seton Lloyd, dug the bridge out and guessed that it was built of more than two million blocks. In 2014 two other scholars worked it out again, more carefully, and arrived at about 440,000. Each block weighed about as much as three grown men, and someone had to cut every one of them, carry it, and set it in place.

It is one of the oldest bridges for water known anywhere. Parts of it still stand, and some of the blocks still carry the king's words.

A lump of pale yellow metal, about as big as a lentil, has a lion's head stamped on one side, with its mouth open. On the other side there is only a plain dent, where a punch struck it. It is a coin, one of the oldest anyone has found. It was dug up, with ninety-two others, at the ruins of the temple of Artemis at Ephesus, on the west coast of Turkey, where the coins had been buried more than 2,600 years ago.

Many of the coins, including the lion coins, were made in Lydia ("LID-ee-uh"), a kingdom next door, from electrum, a mixture of gold and silver found in nature in the rivers of Lydia. A few of them carry a word in the Lydian language, walwet, which many scholars think is the name of a king, Alyattes ("al-ee-AT-eez"). He was the king who made peace after an eclipse stopped a battle in 585 BCE.

Before coins, people paid with metal by weighing it, every time. The lion changed that. It said that the piece came from the king and was worth a fixed amount, so nobody had to weigh it. The coins came in fixed sizes, the smallest about as big as a peppercorn.

The stamp did something else as well. When the coins were tested, they turned out to hold less gold than the natural metal from the rivers. The coins were worth what the stamp said, not what was in them, and the king kept the difference.

Later a Lydian king, Croesus ("KREE-sus"), began to make coins of pure gold and of pure silver, and the Greek historian Herodotus wrote that the Lydians were the first people to make coins of gold and silver.

At about the same time, people in China were making coins of their own, in bronze, shaped like little spades. There is no sign that either people knew about the other.

We think Pythagoras ("pih-THAG-or-us") was born on the Greek island of Samos about 570 BCE, and that when he was about forty he moved to Croton, a Greek city on the coast of southern Italy. There he gathered followers and gave them rules: don't eat beans, never walk on the public roads. He taught that after death, a person's soul is born again in another person or an animal.

Things like these, not mathematics, made him famous in his own time.

He wrote no books that we know of. The fullest accounts of his life were written about eight hundred years after he died, and by then he had become a legend.

One old story tells that he was walking past a blacksmith's shop when he heard the hammers ringing out musical notes. He went in to look, and found that the notes depended on how heavy the hammers were. Hammers don't actually work that way, so the story can't be quite right. But the idea behind it is true, and it is easiest to hear with strings.

If you stretch a string tight and pluck it, it shakes the air around it, and you hear a note. A second string, the same in every way but half as long, shakes twice as fast. Its note is an octave higher than the first, and the two blend perfectly. A string two thirds as long also blends well with the first string, and so does one three quarters as long. Pluck the first string, the half string and the two-thirds string together, and you hear a chord. A great deal of music is built on those simple fractions.

Pythagoras's followers wrote these fractions down. The first one known to have done it was Philolaus ("fil-oh-LAY-us"), who lived about a hundred years after Pythagoras.

Did Pythagoras find these fractions himself? Nobody knows for sure. He became a legend in Greece, and the trouble with legends is that you never know when to believe them.

For two days, a king of Sparta and his soldiers were trapped on the Acropolis ("uh-KROP-uh-lis"), the rocky hill in the middle of Athens, surrounded by the ordinary people of the city. On the third day the king gave up and was allowed to leave.

How had it come to this? Two years earlier, with help from Sparta, the Athenians had thrown out Hippias, the man who ruled them alone. Then two powerful Athenians fought over what should come next. One of them, Isagoras, called in the king of Sparta to help him take charge. The other, Cleisthenes ("KLYS-thuh-neez"), had promised the ordinary people a share in running the city. The people would not have Isagoras. They trapped him on the hill with the king, and they brought Cleisthenes back.

Then Athens changed. Every citizen was put into one of ten new groups, called tribes, and each tribe mixed men from the city, the coast and the countryside. Each tribe sent fifty men to a council of five hundred, which ran the city's business. The big decisions were made by an assembly, where any citizen could come, speak and vote. Before long it met on a hillside called the Pnyx ("pnicks").

Who was a citizen? Only free men. Most came from Athenian families, though Cleisthenes added some newcomers. Women could not vote, enslaved people could not, and neither could foreigners who lived and worked in Athens. Most of the people of Athens had no vote at all. But inside the assembly, a poor farmer's vote counted for exactly as much as a rich man's.

One of the new rules let the citizens vote to send a powerful man away from Athens for ten years, though it was first used about twenty years later. Each voter scratched a name on a broken piece of pottery. In an old well below the Acropolis, archaeologists found 191 of these pieces, almost all bearing the same name, Themistocles, and written in only about fourteen different hands. It looks as though someone had prepared them in advance.

In the town of Qufu ("choo-foo"), in eastern China, there is a graveyard with more than a hundred thousand graves. Almost everyone buried there belongs to one family, the Kong family, the descendants of one man.

His name was Kong Qiu ("kong chyoh"). He came to be called Kongzi or Kong Fuzi, Master Kong, and long afterwards European missionaries turned Kong Fuzi into Latin as Confucius.

He was born about 551 BCE in or near Lu, one of many states in a China that was divided and often at war. He wanted to help a ruler govern well. He held a few posts in Lu, then travelled from state to state looking for a ruler who would take his advice, and when he did not find one, he came home and taught.

He taught how to behave well toward your parents, your friends and the people you rule, and he taught how to study. Two of the things he is remembered as saying are these:

> When I walk with two others, I'm bound to find my teacher there.

> To know when you know something, and to know when you don't know, that's knowledge.

He wrote no book. After he died, his students, and then their students, wrote down what they remembered of him: short sayings, answers to questions, little scenes. Over two or three hundred years these were gathered into a book called the Analects, and nobody knows how many of the sayings are really his. For two thousand years after that, the Analects was one of the most widely read books in China, and later in Korea, Japan and Vietnam.

His family has kept track of itself for centuries. An edition of the Kong family tree printed in 2009 fills eighty books and names more than two million people across eighty-three generations. It is said to be the longest family tree in history.

In Athens, people sit on wooden benches, looking down on a flat floor where the chorus will dance. It is spring, and the city is holding its festival for Dionysus, a god of wine. For several days the city watches new plays, and judges choose the best.

In 499 BCE a young man of about twenty-five named Aeschylus ("ESS-kuh-lus") entered the contest for the first time. He did not win; his first win came fifteen years later.

A play then had a chorus, a group of men who sang and danced together, and a single actor, who wore a mask and could play several characters by changing masks. The actor spoke, and the chorus answered. But the actor had nobody else to talk to.

Aeschylus added a second actor. Now two characters could stand face to face, argue, lie to each other, or change each other's minds. We know this from the philosopher Aristotle, who wrote about plays some 160 years later. He says that the next great playwright, Sophocles, added a third.

Aeschylus wrote between seventy and ninety plays, and seven have survived.

He was also a soldier. In 490 BCE he fought at Marathon, where the Athenians defeated a Persian army that had landed in Greece, and where his brother was killed. The Greek historian Herodotus says that 192 Athenians and about 6,400 Persians died there.

Aeschylus died in Sicily as an old man. The words on his grave said who he was and where he came from, and that the grove of Marathon could tell of his courage. They said nothing about his plays. One ancient writer says Aeschylus wrote those words himself; another says the people of the town where he died wrote them. Nobody knows which is right.
