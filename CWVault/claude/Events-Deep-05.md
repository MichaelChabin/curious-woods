## 1. Archaeopteryx

**More (348 words)**

Around the town of Solnhofen ("ZOLN-hoe-fen"), in Bavaria, in southern Germany, the hills are full of quarries, and the stone in them is cream-coloured limestone that splits into thin, flat slabs, like the pages of a book. For more than a hundred years the best of it was used for printing pictures.

In 1861, in a quarry at Solnhofen, a slab split open on a single feather, about seven centimetres long, a dark film on the pale stone. Hermann von Meyer described it that summer, and that autumn named the animal it came from *Archaeopteryx* ("ar-kee-OP-ter-ix"), "old wing."

The same year, near the village of Langenaltheim, another slab split open on the skeleton of an animal about the size of a crow. Around its arms were the marks of feathers, laid out as in a wing. But it also had teeth in its jaws, claws on its fingers, and a long tail of bone.

Was the feather from the same kind of animal? Nobody can prove it. In 2019 Thomas Kaye and his team shone a laser on the slab and found the outline of the feather's lost quill, and argued that its shape matched none of the long flight feathers of the wing or tail of *Archaeopteryx*. In 2020 Ryan Carney and his team argued that it was one of the smaller feathers that lie over the bases of the long wing feathers.

The limestone was laid down in quiet, salty lagoons between islands. Many geologists think the water at the bottom was too salty, or had too little oxygen, for anything to live there, so whatever sank to the bottom was not eaten, and was slowly covered by fine mud.

From the 1990s, farmers and scientists in Liaoning ("lee-ow-NING"), in northeastern China, began to find small dinosaurs with feathers. One of them, *Anchiornis* ("an-kee-OR-nis"), is about 160 million years old, and it had long feathers on its arms and on its legs. Feathers are older than birds.

About a dozen skeletons have been found, all in the limestones of southern Bavaria, and only the one feather.

## 2. Flowers

High in the Montsec ("mun-SEK") mountains, in Catalonia, in the northeast of Spain, is an old quarry called La Pedrera de Meià. A little more than a hundred years ago its fine, pale limestone was cut into slabs for printing, and sent to printers in Barcelona. Some slabs, split open, held fossils: fish, insects, and the stems and leaves of plants.

One of the plants was named after Lluís Marià Vidal, a Catalan mining engineer and geologist who studied the Montsec. It was named *Montsechia* ("mun-SEK-ee-a"), but for many years nobody was sure what it was: a liverwort, a horsetail, even a kind of pine.

In 2015 Bernard Gomez, David Dilcher and their team looked again, at more than a thousand specimens, and argued that it was a flowering plant, one of the oldest known. It lived under the water of a lake, like the hornwort that grows in ponds today, and it had no petals.

What makes a flowering plant is not the petals. It is that its seeds grow wrapped inside a closed case, which becomes the fruit. Each little case of *Montsechia* held a single seed.

In Liaoning, in northeastern China, another very early flowering plant, *Archaefructus* ("AR-kee-FRUK-tus"), "old fruit," grew in lakes about 125 million years ago. It had no petals either.

*Montsechia*'s rock is dated by the fossils in it, and in 2023 geologists moved the dates of that stretch of time, so the two plants now look about the same age.

Pollen is older. Flowering plants make pollen with a wall of a particular pattern, and grains like that are found in rock about 135 million years old, in Morocco, in Israel and in England. Nobody has found the plants that made them.

In 1879 Charles Darwin wrote to a friend that the rapid rise of the flowering plants, as far as anyone could judge from the rocks, was "an abominable mystery." Today the gene clocks, which count the differences in the genes of living plants, put their beginning much earlier, perhaps more than 200 million years ago. Many fossil experts doubt it.

## 3. The asteroid

**Label:** The asteroid
**Line:** earth
**Age:** 66.04 Ma · ± 0.04 Ma · radiometric (argon) on ash at the boundary and on glass beads from the impact, 66.043 ± 0.043 Ma (Renne 2013); a uranium–lead date in Colorado gives 66.021 ± 0.024 (Clyde 2016)

**Place now:** Bottaccione Gorge, Gubbio, Umbria, Italy (43.365 N, 12.583 E)

**Replaces:** sketch *The asteroid*

**Summary (37 words)**
About 66 million years ago. An asteroid about ten kilometres across hit the earth where Yucatán, in Mexico, is now. About three in four kinds of living thing died out, among them every dinosaur except the birds. *More*

**More (349 words)**

```
About 66 million years ago.
```

Outside the town of Gubbio ("GOOB-bee-oh"), in Umbria, in the middle of Italy, a road runs up a narrow gorge, and beside the road is a wall of pink limestone in thin layers. The limestone was laid down on the floor of a sea, and the layers are full of the shells of tiny animals. Partway up, the shells change. Below, there are many kinds, and big ones. Above, there are only a few kinds, and small. Between the two is a layer of clay about a centimetre thick.

In the 1970s Walter Alvarez, a geologist, wanted to know how long that clay had taken to settle. His father, Luis Alvarez, a physicist, suggested measuring iridium ("ih-RID-ee-um"), a metal that is very rare in the earth's crust but falls to earth all the time in the dust of space. A little iridium would mean the clay settled fast; a lot would mean slowly.

With Frank Asaro and Helen Michel they measured it, and there was about thirty times more than they expected. They found the same in clay from the same moment in Denmark and New Zealand. In 1980 they argued that an asteroid about ten kilometres across had hit the earth, and that the dust and its effects had killed the dinosaurs.

Where was the crater? In 1978 Glen Penfield, working for Mexico's oil company with Antonio Camargo, had found a buried ring under Yucatán, and in 1981 they described it. In 1991 Alan Hildebrand, with Penfield, Camargo and others, argued that the ring, about 180 kilometres across, is the crater. It is named after a village near its middle, Chicxulub ("cheek-shoo-LOOB").

In North Dakota, in 2019, a team led by Robert DePalma, then a graduate student, described a site called Tanis. Fish there were buried with tiny beads of glass from the impact in their gills, in a layer that the team argued was laid down by a surge of water within an hour of the strike, three thousand kilometres away. Some geologists doubt parts of that reading. If it is right, Tanis records the first hour.

## 4. Hot spike

**Label:** Hot spike
**Age:** 56.01 Ma · ± 0.05 Ma · astronomical: the layers' rhythm in seafloor mud, tuned to the earth's orbit, not a radiometric date (Zeebe and Lourens 2019); it lasted about 170,000 years
**Tail:** none — a dated moment
**Known from:** a sudden change in the carbon of seafloor mud and soils, and the teeth of small horses
**Place now:** Cabin Fork, Bighorn Basin, Wyoming, United States (43.91 N, 107.69 W)
**Kind:** earth event
**Replaces:** sketch *A hot spike*

**Summary (35 words)**
About 56 million years ago. A great deal of carbon reached the air and the sea over a few thousand years, and the world warmed by about five or six degrees. Some animals grew smaller. *More*

**More (350 words)**

```
About 56 million years ago.
```

In the Bighorn Basin, in northern Wyoming, the bare hills are striped red, purple and grey. Each stripe is an old soil, laid down on the flood plain of rivers and buried, one on top of another. Teeth and jaws of small mammals weather out of the hillsides.

Some of the teeth belong to *Sifrhippus* ("SIF-er-HIP-us"), one of the oldest horses. It had several toes on each foot, each with a little hoof of its own, and it lived among trees.

In 2012 Ross Secord and his team measured the teeth layer by layer at Cabin Fork. The size of a tooth tells you the size of the animal. Before a certain layer the horses weighed about five and a half kilograms. Over the next 130,000 years or so, they shrank to about four, the size of a small house cat. Then they grew again, to about seven.

What happened at that layer happened all over the world. The carbon in soils and in seafloor mud changes suddenly, in a way that means a huge amount of carbon reached the air and the sea over a few thousand years. The earth warmed by about five or six degrees on average. The warm spell, from its start until the earth began to cool again, lasted about 170,000 years.

Why the horses shrank is argued. Many think warm-blooded animals tend to be smaller where it is hotter; some think the plants had become poorer food.

The carbon's source is argued too. Many now think most of it came from volcanoes, at the time the North Atlantic was opening. Others argue for gas from the sea floor, or thawing frozen soils.

The warming was found first in the sea. In 1991 James Kennett and Lowell Stott studied a core of mud drilled from the seafloor off Antarctica, and found that many kinds of tiny animal living on the deep sea floor had died out at once, while those near the surface lived on.

The carbon came fast, but at least ten times more slowly than people are putting carbon into the air today.

In 2001 a drilling ship in the Pacific, north of the equator, lowered a pipe through nearly five kilometres of water and drilled into the mud of the sea floor. The mud came up in long tubes, called cores, and in it were the shells of tiny creatures, each a single cell, that had lived on the floor of the deep sea. Each shell was about the size of a grain of fine sand.

The shells hold a record of ice on another continent.

Oxygen comes in kinds that weigh slightly different amounts, and seawater holds both. When water evaporates from the sea, the lighter oxygen goes up more easily than the heavy. If that water falls as snow on land and stays there as ice, the sea is left with a little more of the heavy oxygen. The tiny creatures build their shells from the sea, so their shells keep its mix. Cold water also adds heavy oxygen to a shell, so the shells record cold and ice together, and geologists use other clues to tell them apart.

In 2005 Helen Coxall and her colleagues, working on that core, showed that the heavy oxygen jumped in two steps, a few hundred thousand years apart, about 34 million years ago. The first came mostly from the deep sea cooling. The second, bigger one marks an ice sheet growing across Antarctica, most likely East Antarctica. As water was locked up in the ice, the sea fell by about seventy metres.

Why then? For a long time the favourite answer was that Antarctica drifted away from Australia and South America, and that a current began to run round it, keeping warm water away. Many now think the bigger cause was that the air held less and less carbon dioxide, until it crossed a line where snow on Antarctica no longer melted in summer.

West Antarctica, the smaller part, seems to have stayed free of ice for a while, at least near its Pacific coast, with forests of southern beech on it.

There has been ice on Antarctica ever since.

Run a finger along the edge of some blades of grass, the wrong way, and they can cut you. Grass leaves are stiff with tiny pieces of glass, made from silica that the roots draw up from the soil. The pieces are called phytoliths ("FY-toe-liths"), "plant stones," and each kind of grass makes them in its own shapes: some like dumbbells, some like saddles, some like little crosses.

When a leaf rots, its phytoliths stay behind in the soil. So an old soil, buried and turned to rock, can say what grew on it, long after every leaf is gone.

Caroline Strömberg spent years dissolving away samples of old soils from the Great Plains of North America, in Nebraska, Wyoming and the states around them, to free the phytoliths, and counting them under a microscope. Grasses of open, sunny country were already there by about 34 million years ago, as many kinds, but they were only a small part of the plants. Trees still ruled. Places where grass outnumbered everything else came later, by about 22 million years ago.

The horses of the Great Plains grew tall teeth, which wear down slowly, at least four million years after that. Grass is hard on teeth, and so is the grit that comes with it.

In South America it went the other way round. Animals in Patagonia had tall teeth long before grass took over there, and some argue the cause was volcanic ash on the leaves. In East Africa, a team found in 2023 that grasses of a hot-country kind were common in places about twenty million years ago, earlier than had been thought.

Grass itself is older than any of this. In 2005 Vandana Prasad and her team found phytoliths of grass in the fossil dung of dinosaurs in central India, about 66 million years old.

Grasslands of a new kind, the ones that grow best where it is hot and dry, spread over the warmer parts of the world later still, between about eight and six million years ago.

West of Lake Turkana, in northern Kenya, the land is hot and dry and cut by gullies. On the morning of 9 July 2011, Sonia Harmand and Jason Lewis, of a team working in Kenya, took a wrong turn on their way to a fossil site. By the afternoon Sammy Lokorodi, a Turkana man working with the team, had spotted pieces of struck stone lying on the ground.

They named the place Lomekwi 3 ("lo-MEK-wee"), and they came back to dig.

A tool of this kind is made by hitting one stone with another, so that a sharp flake breaks off. The flake can cut. The stone it came off, called a core, keeps the scars of every blow. The team found cores, flakes, stones used as hammers, and stones that may have been used as anvils, about a hundred and fifty pieces in all. Some were big and heavy, struck while resting on another stone.

The tools could not be dated. The layers could. Above and below the tools were layers of volcanic ash, and the rock between them had been laid down when the earth's magnetism pointed one way. In 2015 the team put the tools at about 3.3 million years old, seven hundred thousand years older than any tools known before.

Who made them is a mystery. No toolmakers' bones were found with them. A skull of an ape-like human relative, *Kenyanthropus* ("ken-YAN-thro-pus"), of about the same age, had been found about a kilometre away, but that proves nothing.

Some archaeologists doubt the date. In the middle of the Isthmus of Panama, the Panama Canal runs through a deep cut in the hills, called Corte Culebra. Ships pass through it every day, from the Atlantic side to the Pacific. From 2007 to 2016 the canal was enlarged, with new locks and a new channel on the Pacific side, and the digging opened fresh slopes of rock, up to fifty metres high, along some twenty kilometres.

Geologists have searched the rock of the cut ever since it was first dug. In it they found the bones of land animals, among them horses, between about fifteen and nineteen million years old, of kinds known from North America. So Panama was already land, joined to North America, long ago. The question was when the last gap of sea between Panama and South America closed.

Before it closed, sea water ran between the Pacific and the Caribbean, and sea animals lived on both sides as one. When it closed, each was cut in two. Nancy Knowlton studied snapping shrimp, which live on both coasts in pairs that look nearly alike. Put together in a dish, a shrimp from one side and its partner from the other mostly snapped at each other, and almost never bred.

In 2016 a large team led by Aaron O'Dea brought the evidence together: the sea animals, the chemistry of the two seas, which grew apart, and the land animals that crossed. Their answer was that the last passage closed about 2.8 million years ago.

Not everyone agrees. In 2015 Camilo Montes and his team argued, from tiny crystals carried by rivers into Colombia, that the gap had closed between 13 and 15 million years ago. Others think the land closed and opened again more than once.

When the way was open, animals walked it. Armadillos, opossums and porcupines went north; cats, bears, horses and camels went south. A study published this year argues that North American animals had gathered in Mexico for millions of years before, waiting.

Today the Canal joins the two oceans again, with locks that lift ships over the land.

On the side of Monte San Nicola, a hill about ten kilometres inland from the town of Gela ("JEH-la"), near the south coast of Sicily, the ground is striped. Pale bands of limestone and limy clay alternate with thin dark ones.

The rock was laid down on the deep floor of the Mediterranean. The dark bands formed at times when the deep water ran short of oxygen and more food fell from above than could be eaten, so it piled up on the bottom. That happened at a rhythm, set by the slow wobble of the earth as it moves round the sun, and by counting the bands geologists can tell their age.

Geologists had already marked a point in this hillside, at the foot of one pale band. In 2009 they agreed that it marks the start of the stretch of time we live in, called the Quaternary ("KWA-ter-nary"). Its age is about 2.58 million years.

They chose that time because of ice. In mud drilled from the floor of the North Pacific, small stones turn up from about 2.75 million years ago, far more than before. Only ice can carry stones that far out to sea. From about then, ice sheets grew over the far north of Europe and Asia, then Alaska, then the rest of North America.

And then melt. Then grow again. The shells of tiny single-celled creatures in seafloor mud keep the record, in their heavy and light oxygen, the same record that shows Antarctica freezing. For more than a million years the ice came and went about every 41,000 years. In the last million years it has come and gone about every 100,000 years, and the ice grew bigger than it had before.

In 1976 James Hays, John Imbrie and Nicholas Shackleton found those rhythms in mud from the southern Indian Ocean, and matched them to slow changes in the earth's tilt and orbit, which Milutin Milanković, a Serbian engineer and mathematician, had worked out decades before.

Since then there have been about fifty cold spells. The last ended about twelve thousand years ago.

Jebel Irhoud ("jbel ee-GHOOD," the *gh* soft, like a French *r*) is a hill in Morocco, about fifty kilometres inland, southeast of the port of Safi. In 1961 miners digging there for barite, a heavy white mineral, broke into a cave that had long ago filled up with earth and stones. In the earth that filled it was a human skull.

Over the next few years more bones were found. The people they came from were at first thought to be a kind of Neanderthal, and perhaps 40,000 years old. Later dating made them about 160,000.

In 2004 a team from Morocco and Germany, led by Abdelouahed Ben-Ncer and Jean-Jacques Hublin, began to dig again in what was left of the cave. They found more bones: pieces of skulls, a jaw, teeth. They also found flints that had been dropped in fires.

Heat makes a flint into a clock. A stone soaks up energy, very slowly, from the faint radioactivity of the ground around it and of the stone itself, and stores it. A fire empties the store. In a laboratory, heating the flint again makes it glow, a little, by the amount stored since the fire. In 2017 Daniel Richter and his team dated the burnt flints to about 315,000 years ago, give or take 34,000.

Before then, the oldest bones of our species were from Omo, in Ethiopia, thought to be about 195,000 years old. In 2022 the ash above them was traced to the volcano it came from, Shala, and those bones are now at least about 230,000.

The faces of the Irhoud people were much like ours. Their skulls were not. Ours are round at the back, and theirs were long and low. Some say that makes them early members of our species, with the round skull coming later; some say they were close cousins, not quite us.

Hublin's team has argued that our species did not begin in one place. People across much of Africa may all have been part of it. Irhoud is where the oldest bones happen to have survived.

Twenty-four thousand years ago, the place where Chicago stands was under ice more than half a mile thick, more than eight hundred metres. The top of it was higher than the tallest building in Chicago is now.

The ice had come from the north. It grew over Canada until it was as much as three kilometres thick, and it spread south and pushed down the basin where Lake Michigan is now, like a great tongue. It reached northeastern Illinois about 30,000 years ago. By about 24,000 years ago it had reached farthest south, into the middle of Illinois, and it covered all of the Chicago region.

Ice was spread over much of the north of the world at once: over almost all of Canada, much of Britain and Ireland, and Scandinavia. Nearly all of these ice sheets were at their largest between about 26,500 and 19,000 years ago. Geologists call that time the Last Glacial Maximum.

So much water was locked up in the ice that the sea was about 120 to 130 metres lower than it is now. Coral shows it. The kind called elkhorn coral grows only just under the surface of the sea. Richard Fairbanks and his team drilled into the sea floor off Barbados, and in 1989 reported elkhorn coral from the time of the ice about 120 metres down, where it could never have grown.

Where the sea had been shallow, it was now dry land. Siberia and Alaska were joined by a broad plain, where the Bering Strait is now.

The world as a whole was about five or six degrees colder than it is today. Some scientists put it closer to four and a half.

Then the ice began to melt back. As it shrank, it left long, low ridges of rubble around the south end of Lake Michigan. Chicago was free of ice by about 17,000 years ago. The melting ice filled the basin with a lake higher than Lake Michigan is now, called Lake Chicago, and much of the city stands on that lake's flat old floor.
