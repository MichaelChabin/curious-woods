# The Brain Lab — map

## Why one lab

Michael, 13 Nov: there are too many separate goals, and labs are a way to focus them. When stories need many common tools they must share a theme, and a lab keeps the tools alike. The Time Lab is doing this for time and maps. Geometry is doing it for the plane. This does it for the brain.

The theme is old. *A Book of Numbers… and your brain* (Michael, about 2024) already paired numbers on a plane with the child's brain, with practice joining them. The first half became Glass Geometry. This is the second half.

The Labs Ledger already has a lab called *The Brain*. This map widens it. It does not start a new one.

## Four views of one thing

**The cell.** The neuron: one cell, one pulse, the gap between two cells, a net. New. Two benches exist in `experiments/`: `two-eyes.html` and `pond.html`.

**The brain.** The painting, two views, one set of names. Exists: *About Your Brain*, hung.

**You.** The benches where she tests herself: Flash, Creep, Trace, and `js/necker.js`. Exists in part. The stories are the 24 in `Brain-Series-Map.md`, plus the new ones below. The Perception Lab seed (colour, pitch, rhythm) is this view under another name.

**Practice.** Her list, the tapping, the schedule. Michael, 13 Nov: "the underlying mechanism of practice is the brain and becoming more effective at practice involves understanding one's particular brain better." Practice becomes an experiment she can tune, for the things she wants to be automatic.

## The spine of the cell view

Seven steps, each adding one part. They are for us. A story asks the lab for its step by name, as a geometry story asks for `circles`. The child never sees the steps, and stories are not prerequisites of each other.

1. `signal` — a signal inside one cell
2. `neuron` — a cell that only carries signals
3. `gap` — the junction between two cells
4. `net` — many cells, no centre
5. `centre` — a brain
6. `change` — a connection that grows stronger or weaker with use
7. `silicon` — the same thing made by people

Proposed, not designed: one drawn neuron and one amber pulse, the same in every story and every view. The two benches already use an amber pulse and a blue one, one per eye.

## Stories: the cell view

Every fact below is from memory and unchecked unless it says otherwise. Checking belongs to each story's research.

### C1. The Cell That Backs Up — step `signal`
*Beat.* A paramecium has no nerves. When it bumps into something, a pulse of electricity runs over it and its oars reverse.
*She does.* Puts a wall in its way and watches the pulse and the reversal, slowed down.
*Science.* Jennings 1906 on the avoiding reaction; Eckert 1972 on the calcium pulse.
*Honesty.* It is a cell, not a neuron. The parts are older than the neuron, and that is the point.

### C2. Two Eyes, Two Oars — step `signal` — bench built
*Beat.* What a creature does depends on how it is wired.
*She does.* Drags the light; taps *Uncross the wires*; the same creature now flees.
*Science.* Braitenberg, *Vehicles*, 1984.
*Honesty.* The creature is invented. The real cell, Chlamydomonas, has one eyespot and rolls so that the eyespot sweeps past the light.

### C3. The Pond — step `signal` — bench built
*Beat.* Nobody designs the light seeker. It takes over because it eats.
*She does.* Moves the light; makes the light burn; watches the pond crash and recover from a few that were losing.
*Ruled 12 Nov (Michael).* About a minute to take over is right, because a story sets it up.
*Honesty.* A model, not a record. The light has to wander, or wireless creatures born beside it do well sitting still. A few strangers drift in if the pond nearly empties.
*Open.* Whether natural selection belongs here or in a later lab of its own.

### C4. The Sponge That Sneezes — step `neuron`
*Beat.* A sponge has no neurons, and its sneeze takes minutes. What neurons bought was speed.
*She does.* Not found yet.
*Science.* Sally Leys's lab on sponge contractions.

### C5. The Animal With No Head — step `net`
*Beat.* A jellyfish runs on a net with no centre.
*She does.* Touches the net anywhere; the pulse spreads every way.

### C6. Arms That Think — step `centre`
*Beat.* Most of an octopus's neurons are in its arms.
*She does.* Not found yet.

### C7. The Dragonfly's Catch — step `net`
*Beat.* A handful of neurons steer a dragonfly to where the fly will be, not where it is.
*She does.* Tries to tap where a crossing fly will be; then watches the dragonfly's few neurons do it.
*Science.* Gonzalez-Bellido and others 2013, *PNAS*; Olberg.

### C8. A Crow's Packed Brain — step `centre`
*Beat.* A bird's neurons are small and crowded close, so a small brain holds a great many.
*Science.* Olkowicz and others 2016, *PNAS*.
*Honesty.* Say "packed", and that the counts compare with a monkey's. Do not say bird brains are faster, or solve problems mammals cannot.

### C9. The Same Few Chemicals — step `gap`
*Beat.* A worm and a child cross the gap with the same few chemicals.
*Honesty.* Nearly all animals. Comb jellies seem to be the exception, and nobody knows whether neurons evolved once or twice.

### C10. 302 — step `net`
*Beat.* One small worm has 302 neurons, and every one has been named and mapped. Then a fly. Then a piece of human brain half the size of a rice grain.
*She does.* Taps any of the 302 and sees what it touches.
*Science.* White and others 1986. The human piece is checked: Shapson-Coe and others, *Science*, 10 May 2024; one cubic millimetre, about 16,000 neurons, 150 million synapses, 1.4 petabytes (Google Research). It is not 10 cm.

### C11. The Pretend Neuron — step `silicon`
*Beat.* In 1958 Frank Rosenblatt built a machine out of pretend neurons, and it learned.
*She does.* Trains a tiny net by nudging the strength of its connections.
*Honesty.* A pretend neuron is far simpler than a real one.

### Y1. Counting the Limerick — the opening of story 6
*Source.* Michael, *A Book of Numbers*, page 16. Already on the wanted list under this name (the notes of `Story-The-Cube-That-Wont-Hold-Still.md`, 20 Sept; in `99-ARCHIVE/` since 23 Nov).
*She does.* Counts the words of the young lady of Niger as she reads it. She gets 33. Was it funny? Then she counts the words backwards, without reading, and finds it faster and surer.
*Tool.* A timer. Wordplay already holds the limerick.

### Y2. The Actor's Trick
*Beat.* An actor learns lines by knowing where she stands, where the others are, and what she is doing. A line tied to a place has more roads leading to it than a line repeated ten times.
*She does.* Learns a short poem by staging it.
*Already ours.* `00-FOUNDATION/The-Practices.md` (April) has this as a form of practice called *Inhabiting*, with Emily Dickinson's "I'm Nobody! Who are you?" as its example.
*With it.* 明: the sun beside the moon means bright. Li Bai's *Quiet Night Thoughts*. (Michael, *A Book of Numbers*, pages 19–20.)
*Science.* To find. Noice and Noice on actors; Maguire and others 2003 on memory champions and routes through places.
*Links.* Practice; step `change`.

### Y3. The Curve of Forgetting
*Beat.* In the 1880s Hermann Ebbinghaus memorized lists of nonsense syllables and tested himself for months, to see how fast they faded.
*She does.* Measures her own curve. Needs the come-back-tomorrow hook that stories 8 and 9 also need.
*Links.* It is the engine under Practice. The science is written up in `The-Practices.md`, Appendix I.

### Y4. The Cell That Liked Edges
*Beat.* In 1959 a neuron in a cat's brain fired at the edge of a glass slide, and at nothing else.
*She does.* An illusion that shows her own edge cells at work.
*Science.* Hubel and Wiesel 1959.
*Honesty.* Choose the illusion with care; the usual explanation of the Hermann grid is disputed.

### Y5. The Slug That Learned — step `change`
*Beat.* Eric Kandel found learning in a sea slug: a gap between two cells that grew stronger.

### Y6. As Old as You Are
*Beat.* Bomb tests in the 1950s left a mark in every cell made since. It let us date neurons, and most are as old as their owner.
*Science.* Spalding and others 2005, *Cell*.
*Honesty.* "Among the longest lived", not "never replaced". One part of the brain seems to make new ones, and that is argued about.

- **The how belongs to the lab; the what comes from everywhere.** The lab owns the way she practices. What she practices arrives through *I want to Remember this*: elements, characters, the structure of stars. Variants stay separate rooms, added one at a time (Michael, Sept).
- **The ensō stays where it is** and opens straight onto today's sitting. It becomes a second door into the same room.
- **The schedule is invisible.** Her list offers what is ready today. No dates and no counts, per the September ruling.
- **Few controls.** The design record sorts them into calibrations she finds each time (spread, tempo, which room) and costumes that stick (n², the look). Choices come only before the first tap and after the last. Hold to that list.
- **The tool says what it cannot tell.** A few sittings cannot show whether a setting helped.
- **Settling.** The two or three quiet minutes before practice (from the book's ritual) are where calm focus could become a habit. The stories let her feel the difference; this is where she does it daily.
- **To amend if ruled:** `00-WHAT-CW-IS.md` lists Practice as its own part.

## Three corrections to carry

- **Guessing.** The book says never guess. Story 13 says surprise is what makes things stick, and a wrong guess corrected at once is a surprise. What harms is practicing a mistake nobody corrects. Wants Michael's ruling, and a check of the evidence.
- **Pleasure.** "Essential to learning" claims more than the dopamine work shows. Keep to "surprise makes things stick."
- **The neuron picture.** Most of the branches are dendrites, which gather. A neuron has one axon, which sends.

## Ruled, 13 Nov 2026

Michael, on the eight points put to him with Claude's advice: "None" to change. So, as advised:

1. **Four views.** The lab has four views: cell, brain, you, practice.
2. **Practice is the fourth view.** The ensō stays where it is in the gallery and opens straight onto today's sitting. `00-WHAT-CW-IS.md` is to be amended to match.
3. **The seven steps** are our order now. Each becomes a real level of the lab only when it is built. Some (`centre`, `silicon`) may prove to be subjects more than powers.
4. **One neuron, one amber pulse, everywhere,** as a principle. The look gets a bench before it is settled, and the pulse must not be mistaken for the timeline's copper.
5. **The pond** stays in the cell view for now. Natural selection gets a home of its own only when a second story needs it (the sun-and-temperature world would be that story). The first neurons go on the deep timeline as an age nobody knows exactly.
6. **Guessing: two memories, two rules.** For what she is making automatic, never rehearse a wrong answer; the tapping practice already works this way. For understanding, a guess followed at once by the answer is a surprise, and surprise helps. Michael: "Guessing what 7x8 is practices errors but an explorer who can't guess never moves." The evidence for both halves is still to be checked.
7. **The Brain Series Map is not merged yet.** Both files stand, this one pointing at that one, until those stories are next revised.
8. **The name is *The Brain*,** as the Labs Ledger already has it.

And a note for Practice, Michael the same day: no multiple-choice question measures what a child has accomplished, however hard it is made. The three choices in the tapping practice are not a test. They are there to be easy.
