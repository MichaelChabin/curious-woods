# The God Who Would Not Move

Rome is one of the two marks on the map below. The other is a stone in a field, six miles down the road toward a town called Laurentum. Once a year the Roman state sent a man out to that stone to burn the innards of a sheep.

The stone was the edge of Rome. Not the edge of the city — the edge of everything Rome had. Six miles.

By the time anyone wrote the ceremony down, Rome ran from Scotland to the Sahara, and the sacrifice at the sixth milestone was a habit nobody had thought to stop.

## What two neighbours did

Everything below comes from a poet called Ovid, who wrote out the Roman year month by month around the year 8. This is the twenty-third of February.

Two farmers whose fields met at a stone each came to it from his own side. Each brought a garland and a cake. They built a rough altar at the stone. The farmer's wife carried coals from her own fire in a broken pot; the old man laid the wood and coaxed the flame up out of dry bark; a boy stood holding a wide basket. Grain went into the fire three times. The small daughter dropped in sliced honeycomb. Wine was poured, some of it into the flames. Everyone wore white and nobody spoke.

Then they cut a lamb's throat and splashed the stone with the blood. Ovid says the stone does not complain if it gets a suckling pig instead.

Then the two families ate together and sang to it.

Ovid calls the stone *communis Terminus* — the shared Terminus. A *terminus* is a boundary marker, a stone set in the ground to say where one man's land stops and the next man's starts. Terminus was also a god. The stone and the god had the same name because they were not two things.

## What happened if you moved one

Rome's oldest law on this is reported by a Greek historian named Dionysius, who lived in Rome and wrote about its beginnings. A man who dug up a boundary stone or shifted it became *sacer*. Say SAK-er. It is the root of our word sacred, and it did not mean what sacred means to us. It meant handed over to the god, and out from under the law's protection. Anyone who wanted to could kill him, and it was not murder.

Later Rome got less drastic about it. A Republican statute sets the penalty at five thousand sesterces — a fine.

## How can you tell a boundary stone from a stone?

You dig beside it and look underneath, and this works because of what was done when the stone went in.

Setting one was its own small ceremony. A hole was dug. Blood was poured into it, by people with their heads covered. Then incense, grain, honeycomb, wine. All of it was burned in the hole, and the stone was set down on the ashes while they were still hot.

So the Roman surveyors' own manuals tell them: if you are not certain a stone is a boundary marker, dig beside it and look for the layer of ash. If the ash is there, it is.

They wrote that down for each other. It is the sort of instruction you only need if people are lying to you about stones.

## The hill

Tarquin, the last king of Rome, wanted to build a temple to Jupiter on the Capitoline hill. The hill already had small shrines all over it. You could not simply move a god off his own ground; you had to ask him, and the men who asked were augurs — priests who read the answer in the flight and the feeding of birds. Say AW-gur.

The augurs went round the hill asking one god at a time. Every god agreed to move.

One did not.

So they built the temple around him. Terminus stayed exactly where he was, inside the new walls, and Ovid says a small opening was left in the roof above him, so that he would have nothing over his head but the sky.

The sources do not agree about who else refused. Livy says Terminus alone in one book of his history and Terminus with Juventas, the goddess of young manhood, in another. Dionysius says both of them. Augustine, writing four hundred years later, says three, adding Mars. Nobody writing any of this was there; the earliest of them is telling a story already five hundred years old. What all four agree on is Terminus.

## What the Romans took it to mean

Dionysius gives the reading the augurs put on it. The god of boundaries had refused to be moved, so nothing would ever move the boundaries of Rome, or wear down her strength.

It is worth stopping on how neat that is. A boundary stone is only as good as whoever is willing to enforce it, and the Romans had put the enforcement inside the stone. You did not argue with a boundary. You argued with a god, and lost.

## Four hundred years later

Augustine was a bishop in North Africa. He wrote a long book against the Roman gods, and when he got to this story he went straight for the omen.

The emperor Hadrian, he points out, gave up three provinces east of the Euphrates — handed them back, on purpose, because holding them was not worth what it cost. So much for the boundaries that could not move.

Terminus, Augustine says, seems to have been more afraid of Hadrian, a king of men, than of the king of the gods.

*Tap **east of the Euphrates** to see the land Hadrian gave back.*

## More

The stone in the field and the edge of the empire share a word, and the field had it first. In the surveyors' manuals, a *limes* is the strip of untilled ground between two men's land — a baulk, wide enough to walk along. Centuries later the same word is the name for the outer edge of the whole empire. Historians used to take that to mean a fortified line, walls and ditches all the way round; the word does not carry that, and Benjamin Isaac took the idea apart in 1988 by following what *limes* actually meant at each date. First a military road. Then a province's land border, with no implication of anything built on it. Then a frontier district with a commander. Never a wall.

The twenty-third of February was the last day of the old Roman year, before the extra days were slipped in to keep the calendar level with the sun. The festival of the boundary stone sat on the boundary of the year.

When Ovid speaks to the stone directly he says: *you set the limits of peoples and cities and vast kingdoms.* He is talking to the thing in the ground between two wheat fields.

Nobody knows which stone was the sixth milestone, or where exactly the road ran. The mark on the map is approximate, which is why it has no dot.

## Notes, for us

**The beat.** Rome put a god inside its boundary stones so that nobody could move them, and the boundaries moved anyway. Everything in the story carries that or goes.

**What to cut first.** The *More* paragraph about the calendar, then the Isaac paragraph — that one is really the seed of a different story and could move to *The Straight Lines Are Still There*. After that, the five-thousand-sesterces sentence. Do not cut the ash.

**What changed and why.** The story was going to end on the hole in the roof, which is the prettier image. Augustine is better because he is a fact rather than a feeling, and because he is the best ancient evidence that Romans really did read the story as a promise about frontiers — he is attacking that reading directly, which means it was there to attack. It also keeps the story from admiring itself.

The section "How can you tell a boundary stone from a stone?" is a question, so its first sentence answers it, per the voice page.

Nothing anywhere tells her what to conclude. The line about arguing with a god and losing is the closest the story comes, and it is a statement about Romans, not about her.

**The tool this story calls.**

*In the flow, once.* A map of central Italy: Rome as a place, Laurentum as a place, and the sixth milestone as a `note` — words with no dot, because we do not know where it was. Caption: *The twenty-third of February.* This is the one map in the story and it runs across, so the text says "the map below."

*In a window, once.* The words **east of the Euphrates** open a window map of the eastern provinces with the three Hadrian gave up washed in violet. One wash, no path, no caption — a window map holds less. The instruction in the text names the gesture and the exact words on the screen, per the rulings.

Neither map needs anything `map.js` does not already have after the second pass, except the `italy-central` and `near-east` base pictures, which are four numbers each.

**Honesty checks, one per claim that could be challenged.**

*The sixth milestone was the edge of Roman territory.* This is the traditional Roman explanation, reported in Smith's dictionary from the ancient sources, not a modern archaeological finding. The story says "was the edge of Rome" flatly and I think that is defensible as reporting what Romans believed about their own festival, but if Michael wants it hedged, "which Romans said had once been the edge of everything Rome had" does the job and costs a clause.

*The rite, in detail.* All from Ovid, *Fasti* 2.639–684, verified against the Latin. Ovid is describing his own time, not the archaic period.

*Blood on the stone.* Genuinely contested and the story quietly takes a side. Ovid describes a lamb and a pig. Dionysius (2.74.4) says explicitly that it was never lawful to stain the stones with blood. Plutarch (*Numa* 16.1) reconciles them: bloodless anciently, animals in his own day. The story gives Ovid's version because Ovid is the eyewitness of the three. Worth a sentence if Michael wants the disagreement visible — it would fit the story's temperament.

*Sacer, and killing with impunity.* Dionysius 2.74.3, attributed there to Numa. Treat as Rome's account of its own archaic law rather than as a documented statute.

*Five thousand sesterces.* The Lex Mamilia Roscia Peducaea Alliena Fabia, transmitted inside the Corpus Agrimensorum.

*The ash under the stone.* Siculus Flaccus, *De condicionibus agrorum* 11. **This one needs checking before build.** The substance is standard and uncontroversial, but the research reached it through a secondhand quotation and could not open the Latin. Campbell 2000 has the text and translation; somebody should look at the actual page before this goes on the site, because it is the best detail in the story and the one a reader is most likely to look up.

*The hole in the roof.* Ovid alone, *Fasti* 2.667–72 (line numbers vary by edition). There is no archaeological evidence for it, and none for the shrine of Terminus inside the temple either. The story says "Ovid says," which is the honest form.

*Which gods refused.* Set out in the story itself, with all four sources named. Livy 1.55, Livy 5.54.7, Dionysius 3.69.5, Augustine 4.23.

*Which Tarquin.* Livy says Superbus, Dionysius says Priscus. The story says "Tarquin, the last king," which follows Livy. The whole Tarquin narrative is legendary and the frontmatter year says so.

*Hadrian gave up three provinces.* Augustine, 4.29. Augustine is a hostile witness about a Roman god but a perfectly ordinary one about a well-known imperial decision; Hadrian's withdrawal from Trajan's eastern conquests is not in dispute.

*Augustine's line.* Quoted close but not verbatim; it is a translation from the Latin either way. Fine as reported speech, and the reference points at the chapter.

**Open.** Whether to show the disagreement about blood in the story rather than only in these notes. I lean yes — the story already does this once, with the gods on the hill, and doing it twice would make it a habit she notices rather than a one-off.
