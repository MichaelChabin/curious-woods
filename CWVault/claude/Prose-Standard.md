# How Curious Woods prose reads

Amended 23 Nov 2026 by Ruling-Voice-01.md. The test is whether it is read. A told-aloud voice is wanted, word counts are guides, and the voice is never finished. Where this page and that one differ, that one wins.

## The test

She reads it to the end because she wants to, and afterwards she tells someone one thing from it.

Everything below serves that test. A piece that obeys every rule and fails the test has failed. A piece that breaks a rule and passes is worth a look, and probably shows a rule that needs changing.

## Who is reading

A bright ten-year-old you like a lot. She reads adult books when they interest her. She knows only what Curious Woods has already given her. She can tell in one sentence when she is being taught at, and she leaves. E. B. White is the model: short words, plain sentences, one idea at a time, and no sentence that admires itself.

## Read the models first

Rules wear down into a checklist. Examples don't. Before writing anything, read these whole, and write until yours sounds like them:

- *Pythagoras*, Michael's own rewrite (`Events-Batch-04.md`, event 12). Sentences that run; a mechanism she can try in her head; an honest "nobody knows" for the ending.
- *Pakicetus* (`Life-Trial-01.md`). A scene, a puzzle ("So was it a whale?"), and the answer arriving bone by bone.
- *Girl with a Pearl Earring* (`Story-Vermeer-Girl-with-a-Pearl-Earring.md`). A full story.
- *Iron* and *Sulfur*, as reflowed on 25 Oct (`Elements-Batch-01.md` once the desk's version is approved). A reference entry that still reads well.
- Carbon and Oxygen, told aloud (`Elements-Told-Aloud-02.md`). A reference entry that opens on its strangest true thing and comes back to it at the end.

## What makes it fun to read

These are what the models have in common. A piece needs most of them, not all.

**Something worth repeating, early.** Find the strangest true thing in the material and put it within the first few sentences, not at the end of a list. "There is a great deal of carbon in you" beats "There is not much carbon in the Earth's rock." Most subjects have one. If you can't find it, you haven't finished the research.

**People doing things.** A named person in a named place, with their hands busy: burning diamonds with a great lens, boiling down urine, digging jumbled skeletons from a dry stream bed. An action carries a fact better than a statement does.

**A turn.** One moment where the story changes direction: a question the next paragraph answers ("So was it a whale?"), or a short line that sums things up ("But nobody knew that charcoal, the black in a pencil and a diamond were one thing."). One or two per piece. They are seasoning, not the meal.

**Size she can feel.** A number turned into something she can hold: a 10 cm cube, about as fast as fingernails grow, the weight of the iron in her own blood.

**A mechanism she can run in her head**, when it can be given honestly. Half the string, twice as fast. A half-explanation is worse than none; if it can't be done honestly in the space, leave it out.

**An honest edge.** "Nobody knows" or "the date is argued", said plainly, is an invitation. It is never a weakness.

## How it looks on the page

**One idea per paragraph.** A paragraph holds one idea and is about 40 to 90 words long. Over about 100, it is two paragraphs. A block of text is a wall, and she won't climb it, however good the facts inside.

**Sentences that run, not choppy ones.** Join related facts with *and*, *but*, *so*, *because*, *which*, *when*. A string of five-word sentences reads like a list read aloud. A sentence over about 35 words is usually two.

**The hedge goes first, and once.** "We think Pythagoras was born on Samos about 570 BCE, and that when he was about forty he moved to Croton."

**When a piece has a fixed order** (an element entry's three questions: how much, where from, who first held it), each part of the order is its own paragraph, so she knows where to look.

## Clarity, which comes before all of it

- She understands every sentence the first time.
- Every *it*, *they*, *this*, *that*, *one*, *he* points at exactly one thing. Where it might not, say the noun again.
- Assume nothing. Every name and idea the piece leans on is explained where it first appears ("Anatolia, the land that is now Turkey"). Nothing else is explained.
- Pronunciation in brackets after a hard name: Pakicetus ("PAK-i-SEE-tus").
- Numbers rounded to what she can hold. The exact figures live in the notes or the fields.
- Plain years: 1871. "Someone", not "somebody". "Experiment", never "test".

## What we never do

Never tell her something is interesting, amazing, important or fun. Never "did you know", never an exclamation mark, never "the building block of". Never turn from the subject to face her with advice. Never end on a lesson, a moral or a takeaway: end on a fact, an image, her own result, or what is still unknown. Never "grown-ups". If a sentence would look at home in a lesson plan or on a poster, cut it. (The full list of refusals is in the cw-story skill and Story-Voice.md.)

## Before you hand it over: two reads

**Read it once as her**, the writer's own pass. Mark every sentence you had to read twice and rewrite it. Mark every pronoun that could point at two things. Mark every paragraph over about 100 words and split it.

**Then a fresh reader**, who did not write it, reads it as her and answers four questions in a line each:

1. Where would she stop reading?
2. Which sentence did you have to read twice?
3. What is the one thing she would tell someone afterwards?
4. Does it sound like the models, or like an encyclopedia?

If question 3 has no answer, the piece goes back to be rewritten, not polished. In a batch chat the fresh reader is a separate agent; without agents, it is a separate pass done after a break, and the batch file says so.

## Keeping it from wearing down

A standard drifts when it is copied into briefs piece by piece, and when a long batch slides into get-it-done. So:

- **This page is named, never copied.** A brief says "read Prose-Standard.md and the models it lists" and adds only what is special to its kind. When Michael's edits teach something new, it goes here, once.
- **Every batch file carries the fresh reader's four answers** for each piece, so a tired batch shows.
- **Now and then, a voice check.** Take three recent pieces from different kinds, read them against the models, and say plainly which one has drifted and how.
