# A 3D Earth: an audit before the design

Written for a designer. Where a word from the code is unavoidable it is in `this type` and explained the first time. Everything here was read in the code and the vault, and two things were measured by running the Time Machine on the Mac in the app's browser pane: how long the plate file takes to arrive, and how long the motion model's run takes. Nothing was tried on the iPad.

Two things Michael settled on 26 Oct frame it: the 3D Earth lives on a bench beside the Time Machine until it matches the 2D globe, and only then replaces it; and she reaches the inside with a word, *Cut open*.

One thing found on disk that the prompt did not know: the standard's 3D shelf was built today, in another session, while this one read. `js/3d/` holds six of the seven pieces (`scene.js`, `cutaway.js`, `noise.js`, `glow.js`, `honest.js`, `still.js`; not `lens.js`), three.js r186 sits in `js/vendor/three-r186/`, and `experiments/sun-3d.html` is the Sun rebuilt on them. It was written between 12:09 and 12:28 and committed at 12:37 (`ac41b13`, not pushed; its report is `claude/Report-3D-Shelf-Oct26.md`). Two things to know about it: it was built to Michael's prompt and the Ideas Ledger, because the standard and the 25 Oct worknote were not on disk when it was built (they are now, untracked), so the shelf and the standard may differ in places its report lists; and the commit says the ten-minute heat check passed on the iPad and the Mac, while the board's checked list says the session itself never saw WebGPU in Safari. Section 8 says what it already gives the Earth.

**The short answer.** The plan is right in its aim and wrong in its method, in one place that matters and four that follow from it. The 2D globe asks, for every pixel, *which plate is here*, because a 2D canvas cannot paint a polygon with a rotated picture. A graphics card can: it paints polygons. So the "which plate" picture, which the plan makes the centre of the shader, should not exist at all. The pieces of continent become shapes on the ball, each carrying today's picture and turned by its plate, and the card finds the plate under every pixel by drawing. That one change makes the rest smaller: the "small table of turns" becomes a few hundred numbers a frame, the half-million-year raster goes, and the thing that made play step on Safari goes with it. The other places the plan is wrong are about what a sphere cannot do (become the map by zooming), what the standard forbids (phones), what the plan forgot (the flat deep map is the same engine), and what does not line up by itself (the inside's ages and the outside's). They are in section 0, before the how.

## 0. Where the plan is wrong

**1. "Which plate is here" is a 2D-canvas question.** Today the globe builds an *id raster*: an offscreen picture of the whole world, equirectangular, 1,024 by 512, with every living piece filled in its plate's number, rebuilt every half million years. Then for each pixel of the disc it reads the raster, turns the point back to today by that plate's rotation, and reads today's picture there. That is a workaround for a canvas that can only fill flat shapes with flat colours. On the card the natural thing is the opposite: each piece is a small mesh on the sphere, its corners in today's coordinates, its texture coordinates pointing into today's picture, and its plate's rotation applied as the mesh's own turn. The card then draws each piece where it is, and the question "which plate is here" is never asked. The plan's three ingredients (today's picture, a which-plate picture, a table of turns) become two (today's picture, the pieces as meshes) plus one rotation per plate per frame. Section 3 gives the numbers and the two ways the plan named, and why the third is right.

**2. A sphere does not flatten into the map by zooming.** A perspective view of a ball, close in, is locally flat, but it is never the equirectangular or Mercator frame that `map.js` draws in, and the map's marks, labels, contours, probe and gestures all live in that flat pixel frame. "Far out a globe, close in the map's detail" is two different things joined: the tiles can be draped on the ball (they are equirectangular, which is exactly how a sphere is parameterised), but the *map* (the frame, the bend, the mark layer) needs either a handover or a morph. Section 6 says the morph is the thing that makes "zoomed until it flattens" true, and what it costs.

**3. The standard bans 3D on phones, and the Time Machine serves phones today.** The page has been checked at 375 px again and again since 9 Oct, and the globe runs there. The standard says a 3D interactive on a short side under 600 points shows a still and one line. So replacing `globe.js` means one of three things, and it is Michael's to choose before the bench: the 2D engine stays for phones (two approaches to one problem, which he hates); the Earth is an exception to the phone rule (its shaders are light; the ban was written for the Sun and the gas); or the Time Machine on a phone shows the flat map only and a still past the seam. Nothing in the plan or the standard decides this.

**4. The flat deep map is the same engine.** `globe.js` draws two views: the disc, and the same world flat, painted into the map's own frame (centre, pixels per degree, the Mercator bend) so that the seam at 2.6 million years is one fade between two paintings of one view. The Time Machine opens with *Map* as its default view, not *Globe*, so the flat view is what most visits see past the seam. The plan speaks only of the globe. If the 2D engine is to go, the 3D engine must also draw the flat deep map, and the only clean way is the morph of section 6 (the ball flattened into the map's frame, seen by a flat camera). Otherwise `globe.js` stays for the flat view and the plan has two engines again.

**5. The inside's ages and the outside's do not line up by themselves.** The Cut Open sketch runs from 4.0 to 2.5 billion years (drips of crust sinking, melt rising, rafts growing). The globe's molten sequence runs from 4.567 to 4.4 billion (melt, black crust, the Moon, melt again, the sea). Between them, 4.4 to 4.0, the globe shows invented blobs; past 4.0 it shows the real cratons guessed. The sketch's 26 rafts are its own random; the globe's 5 to 9 blobs are seeded from the crust curve. One Earth object means one state for both faces: section 8 says what has to be decided (which inside goes with which age, and that the rafts at the cut's rim are the blobs that cross the cut).

**6. "One Earth object for the story maps' globe" costs every story page three.js.** Eleven pages load `map.js` today and none loads 3D. three.js r186 is 3.6 MB on disk (about 700 KB compressed, a guess). The aim is right (the globe at a story's end, stories choosing), but it has to be an opt-in that loads the engine only on the page that asks, not a change to `map.js` that every story carries.

**7. Smaller, and worth saying now.** The shelf's `scene.js` turns the ball as a free trackball about the camera's axes, with inertia. The deep-time ruling 3 of 6 Oct says the globe has a standard view: north up at rest, the poles the limits of the tilt, a drag to the right moves the surface east whatever the tilt. The two are not the same gesture; a trackball lets her turn Antarctica to the top. The Earth needs its own turn on the shelf's scene, or the standard needs a line saying a globe on a stand turns like a globe on a stand. And `scene.js` offers an idle turn (`turn`, radians a second); the standard says nothing spins on its own when the page opens. The Earth passes zero.

Everything else in the plan stands: the shaders in TSL on the WebGPU renderer, the motion model in JavaScript, one rotation per plate per frame, Blue Marble as the measured top grade, the cut-away on the same object, the bench first.

## 1. Everything `globe.js` does

`globe.js` is 1,216 lines. Its header is the best description of it and was read, not inferred. Below, every job, and for each one: **card** (moves to the graphics card), **JS** (stays in JavaScript), **gone** (a real sphere makes it unnecessary), or **neither** (fits none of the three).

**The two projections.**
- *The disc.* Orthographic: a ball seen from infinitely far, capped at 560 px. **Gone**: a sphere and a camera replace it. (The cap goes with it; the ball is as big as its box.)
- *The flat view.* The same rings and skin, equirectangular on a 2:1 canvas, with `fill` and `setView` making it a painting of a page's map view: centre, pixels per degree, and the map's blend from plate carrée toward Mercator with the 85° cap. **Neither.** A 3D engine imitating a 2D map's frame is the morph of section 6: the ball's vertices moved onto the map's frame and seen by a flat camera. If that is not built, this job stays in `globe.js`.
- *Three copies a world apart* (`traceFlat` draws a ring across the dateline three times). **Gone** on a sphere; on the flattened morph, the world wraps the same way the map's `worldCopies` wraps.

**The view rules.** North up at rest; the tilt stops when a pole faces her; a drag right moves the surface east whatever the tilt (`viewMatrix`, the drag handlers). **JS**, and it must be written into the shelf's scene as the Earth's own gesture, because the shelf's trackball does not keep them (section 0, point 7).

**The grades and their seam.** `gradeFor(age)`: ground this side of 2.6 million years; measured to 200; inferred to 540; fitted to the model's reach at 1,000; guessed to the oldest rock at 4,000; invented to 4,400; none above. With a sentence for each. **JS**, a few numbers; the card gets the grade as a number that changes the look (solid or painted, section 5).

**The skin** (`paintSkin`, about 90 lines, the heart of the file). For every pixel inside the ball: the plate from the id raster, the point turned back to today, the picture read between its four nearest texels. Then the look by age: bare rock from the grain (grey to tan, rusty where the cooling map runs high, lit by the grain) until plants at 450 million years, the picture blending in by 300; near-black crust blending in from 2,300 back to 2,500; today's sea in the picture under a piece painted as the translucent shelf wash (the sea mask). **Card**, whole: this is a fragment shader on the pieces' meshes, reading the picture, the grain and the cooling map, with the age as a uniform. The sea mask (built once from today's picture, blue leads red by a fifth) is a texture made once in JS or a test in the shader.

**The id raster** (`idRaster`, `eachBody`, `indexOf`). **Gone**: the pieces as meshes make it unnecessary (section 3). If anything ever needs it, the card draws it in one pass.

**The island, the shelf and the drowned band** (`shrunk`, `shelfRim`, `drownedBand`): the part of an old piece above the sea as its outline shrunk toward its centre; a wide translucent stroke round the union of a body's pieces; the band between outline and island washed even-odd. **Card** for the drawing, **JS** for the geometry: each is a second mesh per piece (the island ring, an offset band), built once at load; the island's share by age is a scale toward the piece's centre, which a vertex shader can take as one number per piece.

**The dissolving pieces** (`dissolvePieces`): a piece fades into the sea over the eight million years before its birth. **Card**: per-mesh opacity; **JS** decides the number.

**The ice of the cold ages** (`paintIce`, the `ICEHOUSES` table): per pixel, the latitude under the pixel at this age against the cold age's reach, feathered over sixteen degrees, roughened by the grain, north and south by age, a snowball white all over. **Card**: a fragment shader on the sea sphere and the pieces, latitude from the surface point. **JS**: the table and the strength by age.

**The ground grade this side of the seam** (`setGround`, `setFloor`, `iceMask`): the sea lowered from the floor's depth (the pyramid's level-1 floor tiles, 1,024 by 512), newly dry floor painted as land by its height above the new shore; the ICE-6G sheets from outline masks filled per age, blended between two ages, never below the sea. **Card** for the painting (the floor as a depth texture, the sea level and the ice strength as uniforms); the ice outlines become masks the card can fill (polygons again) or the JS fills as now into a texture.

**The molten sequence** (`moltenState`, `paintMolten`): above 4,400 million years, crust wherever the cooling map is below a threshold rising with the age, the cracks glowing and dimming, a faint sea, the white flash of the Moon at 4,510, melt again, the maps turned 137° and flipped for a different second crust, the sea's blue by 4,400. **Card**, whole: a fragment shader on the full sphere reading the three maps, with the state's five numbers as uniforms. **JS**: `moltenState`, the phase names, `onMolten`.

**The motion model** (`runSim`, `cratonSim`, `blobSet`, `makeBlobs`, `guessedMatrix`, `blobMatrix`, about 230 lines). **JS**, entire. Section 4.

**Another guess** (`reguess`, `seed`, `setSeed`). **JS**.

**Today's coast and shelf lines** this side of five million years (`prepareCoast`, the fade). **Card**: two line meshes on the sphere, or dropped where the map's own contours are drawn over the ball at close zoom. Today the map hides its contours over the deep view (21 Oct), so the globe's own lines are the only coast on the disc.

**The sea** (a radial gradient, magma to black to blue through the molten ages). **Card**: the sphere's own material.

**The limb shading and the haze** (a darker rim, a halo blue once there is a sea and grey steam before). **Card**: a rim term in the sphere's shader, or a thin shell. Not `glow.js`, which is a light drawn *behind* a ball; the haze sits at the edge, over.

**The flat fills without textures** (`fillLand`, `landPath`, `traceGlobe`, the dashed and dotted edges): what the globe draws before the textures arrive or when `textures: false`. **Gone**: a mesh with a plain colour is the fallback, and the pictures are small.

**The per-pixel machinery** (`pixelToWorld`, `viewPixels` with its cache, `texIndex`, `skinTarget`, `iceTarget`, the half-resolution `skinScale`, `skinMs`). **Gone**. The tier's pixel ratio replaces the half-resolution skin; the frame timer in `scene.js` replaces `skinMs`.

**The hooks a page uses.** `setTime`, `time`, `view`, `turn`, `project`, `placeAt`, `setView`, `setGround`, `setFloor`, `ready`, `grade`, `moltenPhase`, `reguess`, `seed`, `setSeed`, `simMs`, `setProjection`, `projection`, `destroy`; the options `onTurn`, `onGrade`, `onMolten`, `onReady`, `onDraw`. **JS**, and they should keep their names and meanings, because `map.js` is written against them and the swap in section 11 is then a flag. Two change underneath: `project` becomes the camera's projection (three.js does it), and `placeAt`'s `plateAt` (point-in-polygon over the pieces in today's coordinates) can become a pick against the meshes, which also answers "which plate is under her finger at *this* age", a thing the 2D globe cannot.

**What fits none of the three**, beyond the flat view: the id raster's trick of switching the engine into texture space to draw itself (`idRaster` sets `proj = 'flat'` for a moment); the gap-closing hairline strokes in the raster and the land fills, which exist because filled paths leave seams between pieces. The meshes will leave the same seams, and need their own answer (section 3).

## 3. The "which plate" picture

The numbers first, read from `stories/plates/continents.json` (963 KB; CC BY 4.0, Merdith and others 2021 through EarthByte):

| | |
|---|---|
| Plates | 416 |
| Pieces of continent | 795 (797 rings, 5,058 points; 4 pieces have an end age) |
| Age samples | 155, every 5 million years from 0 to 1,000 (the model's reach) |
| Rotation samples in all | 34,624 (each plate from its own first age) |
| Pieces older than the reach | 185, on 46 plates (the cratons the motion model moves) |
| The cratons' run | 1,500 steps of 2 million years, 1,000 to 4,000; 46 bodies |
| The blobs' run | 5 to 9 blobs, 200 steps, 4,400 to 4,000 |
| The id raster today | 1,024 by 512, rebuilt every half million years (8,000 possible ages, one kept) |
| The skin's pictures | 1,024 by 512: the ground (world.webp with its vegetation), grain, cooling order, cracks |

**The two ways the plan named.**

*Keep building the id raster in JavaScript and hand it to the card when the age moves.* It works, and it is the smallest change: the Path2D fill of all living pieces (the costly part, which the half-million-year steps were added on 19 Oct to tame) stays, and the 2 MB picture is uploaded on each step. During play that is an upload every few frames; the card does not mind, but the JavaScript fill is still the thing that made play step on Safari, and nothing has been gained but the per-pixel loop.

*One fixed picture of the plates as they are today, with the card trying each plate's turn per pixel.* This is wrong, not merely slow. "Which plate is here at this age" has no closed form from today's picture: you would turn each of 416 plates (46 past the reach) and test whether the pixel lands inside that plate's region, per pixel, per frame, and overlapping plates would need a rule. It is the id raster's question asked 416 times.

**The third way, which fits.** The card draws polygons; so draw the pieces. Each piece is triangulated once at load (three.js has an ear-clipping triangulator; the 5,058 points become a few thousand triangles, with long triangles subdivided so they hug the sphere, since a triangle 20° across sags about one and a half percent of the radius, visible at the limb). Its corners are today's coordinates on the unit sphere; its texture coordinates are today's longitude and latitude, into today's picture, the grain and the cooling map. Its plate's rotation at the age (one quaternion, interpolated in JS between the two nearest samples as `plateMatrixAt` does now) is the mesh's own rotation. The card draws each piece where it is, with the skin shader of section 1 deciding the look by age. The "table of turns" is then 416 quaternions a frame (6.6 KB), or 46 plus a few blobs past the reach; the full table of 34,624 samples (about 1 MB as a float texture) never needs to go to the card, because only this frame's turn does. The guessed ages and the invented ages work the same way: the motion model gives a quaternion per body per frame, exactly as it gives one to `bodyMatrixAt` today, and a blob is a mesh like any other, its ring built from the seed and shrunk by the crust curve.

Three things the id raster did quietly that the meshes must do openly:

- *Seams between pieces.* The raster closes hairline gaps with a 1.5-texel stroke; the land fills do the same with a 1.6-pixel stroke. Adjacent pieces of one plate share edges only approximately, so a mesh leaves the same hairlines, and worse at the limb. Answers: draw each piece a touch larger (an outline band mesh under it, in its own colour, which the shelf rim of the old ages already is), or weld shared edges at load for pieces on one plate. This is the one place the 2D engine spent days (the blue squares and their jiggle, 19 Oct), and it will come back in a new form.
- *Overlaps.* Where pieces overlap, the raster's last fill wins. On the card two meshes at the same radius fight; a tiny radial lift per piece, or a draw order, settles it. The order should be the raster's, so the picture matches.
- *The far side.* Free: the sphere's depth hides it.

**The ice outlines are the same idea.** `iceMask` fills the ICE-6G rings into a texture per age in JavaScript; on the card they are polygons too, and a cross-fade between two ages is two opacities. The deep-time ice is a latitude band and needs no polygon.

**If the id picture is still wanted** (nothing identified needs it), the card makes it: draw the same meshes with a flat colour per plate into a 1,024 by 512 equirectangular render target, once per age change, no readback. That is the first way done by the card, and it keeps `placeAt`'s question answerable from a texture. The pick against the meshes answers it better.

## 4. The motion model

`runSim` stays in JavaScript, and nothing in it assumes the 2D canvas. It is pure geometry on the unit sphere: each body's shape for touching is a few discs along each piece (`discsOf`, in the tangent plane at the ring's centre), velocities are vectors through the Earth's centre, collisions push bodies apart along the great circle between the two touching discs by their masses, clusters stick and break along a random rift line, and the output is a quaternion per body per step, interpolated between steps (`simQuat`). Its inputs are the pieces' rings as unit vectors and the model's last quaternion at the reach; its randomness is all from the seed. It hands the card exactly what the plan wants: one turn per body per frame, 46 cratons past the reach and up to 9 blobs before the oldest rock.

Measured today in the pane on the M1: the plate file arrives in about 85 ms (cached); the cratons' run takes 60 to 100 ms per seed, once, and every age after that is a lookup. *Another guess* reruns it. On the iPad that is a short pause when the first guessed age is reached and when the word is tapped, as it is now.

One thing near the model does assume the flat map: the blobs are placed within about 55° of the equator because "a blob on a pole is a bar across the flat map". A sphere has no such bar; the flattened morph of section 6 would. Leave it until the flat end is settled.

The blobs' shapes (`blobSet`: ninety-six points, three gentle harmonics, placed clear of each other) and the shrinking by the crust curve (`makeBlobs`, `shrunk`) are geometry too and stay in JS. The shrink could be one number per blob in a vertex shader, which would save rebuilding the mesh as the curve moves; a rebuild of nine rings is cheap enough either way.

## 5. The ground: Blue Marble

**What the globe reads today.** `art/maps/world.webp`, 2,000 by 1,000, 154 KB, coloured by the map's height ramps (Spec-Maps, *The ground colours*: buff, ochre, grey, violet-grey, white for the land; the sea's blues by depth), multiplied by `world-vegetation.webp` (one quiet green by tree cover); drawn into a 1,024 by 512 texture. Height and nothing else, by the 18 Sep ruling.

**The Blue Marble sources.** NASA's *Blue Marble: Next Generation* (Reto Stöckli, NASA Earth Observatory, 2005) is twelve monthly cloud-free composites of 2004 from MODIS at 500 m per pixel, in three variants: the land alone over a flat sea; with topography (shaded relief); with topography and bathymetry (the sea floor painted). NASA's Visible Earth offers each month at three sizes:

| Size | Pixels per degree | Metres per pixel at the equator | JPEG | PNG |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5,400 × 2,700 | 15 | about 7.4 km | about 2 MB | about 14 MB |
| 21,600 × 10,800 | 60 | about 1.85 km | about 26 MB | about 179 MB |
| 86,400 × 43,200 (eight tiles of 21,600 × 21,600) | 240 | 500 m | — | about 1.4 GB a month (a third-party figure) |

The file sizes are the ones Visible Earth lists; the 500 m figure comes from the GRASS GIS wiki and should be checked on download. The licence: NASA says its content, including texture maps for 3D models, is generally not subject to copyright in the United States and may be used for educational purposes with NASA acknowledged as the source; the Earth Observatory asks for the credit "NASA Earth Observatory". No key, no server, which is what Spec-Maps requires. The 2012 *Blue Marble* (Suomi NPP) is a hemisphere, not a map, and NASA's GIBS tile service is a service; neither serves here.

**Which variant.** The land-only one, over CW's own sea. The bathymetry variant paints the sea floor in NASA's blues, and the floor is Drain the Sea's to paint (grey, the held note); the topography variant bakes a shaded relief whose light cannot move, and a lit ball brings its own light. Downsampled to the tier table: 8,192 by 4,096 at full (about 4.9 km a pixel, the pyramid's level 3), 4,096 at middle, 2,048 at floor. As WebP those are perhaps 6 MB, 2 MB and 600 KB; a guess until one is made. One month, or a blend: the picture carries seasonal snow, and in a January picture the Alps and the Himalaya are white.

**The five grades with the photograph on top**, per *Honesty in pictures*:

- *Ground and measured* (this side of 200 million years): the photograph, carried on the plates. The look-by-age rule still cuts it off: today's picture blends out to bare rock from 300 to 450 million years, so the photograph shows a Pangaea of today's forests only in the measured ages, where the places are measured and the forests are the rule's.
- *Inferred and fitted* (to 1,000): drawn solid with crisp edges. Today they take the same skin as the measured ages (rock by then anyway). To show the step from measured to inferred the picture could lose its relief and some of its colour, a solid flat paint of the same hues with the outline crisp, so a child sees it go from photograph to drawing at 200 million years.
- *Guessed and invented*: painted, softer, washed, the edges uneven. That is already the look (the softened outlines, the drowned wash, the translucent shelf, the dark crust from 21 Oct). Keep it.

**The open question, named and not resolved.** With Blue Marble on the globe, the globe this side of the seam shows a photograph while the map beside it shows a coloured height. Step 8 of 20 Oct made the two agree ("the globe this side of the seam shows what the map shows"); this would undo that on purpose. Three rules of Spec-Maps meet it: *no green as a height* (the photograph's green is a forest, so it passes); *the earth: buff, ochre, grey, violet-grey, white, blue* (the map's palette, which a photograph is not); *no red on the earth, ever* (the photograph's Sahara is orange and Australia is red-brown, under vermilion marks). And *white means ice*, which a January photograph's snow breaks. Michael said the shift from photograph to paint when she lowers the sea or moves the continents signals the move from data to model; the shift from the photograph on the ball to the painted height on the flat map is a different one, between two views of the same today, and the morph of section 6 would make it visible as one continuous zoom. His to rule: the photograph on the globe only, the photograph on both (the pyramid re-rendered from Blue Marble, which ends height-as-colour), or the globe keeping the map's colours and Blue Marble waiting.

## 6. Close in, the map

**What there is.** The pyramid `art/maps/world-tiles/`, levels 0 to 5 (the prompt said 0 to 6; level 6 was never rendered: 7,384 files, 113 MB, level 5 at 32,768 by 16,384, about 1.2 km a pixel), equirectangular 512-px WebP tiles; the ice and vegetation layers as 256-px tiles where there is any; the shelf (to 130 m), the floor (to level 4), the age and the stripes as data tiles; contours whole to level 2 and per column from level 3. `map.js` picks the level from pixels per degree at the centre, loads the tiles in view, and bends the frame from plate carrée at the world toward Mercator at a 20° span (*The world that moves*).

**Tiles drawn onto the ball.** Equirectangular is a sphere's own parameterisation: a tile covering 22.5° by 22.5° at level 3 is exactly a patch of the sphere, and draping it needs no reprojection, only a patch mesh with the tile's corners as its texture coordinates. Far out, levels 0 to 2 are whole textures (level 2 is 4,096 by 2,048, the middle tier's largest). Close in, levels 3 to 5 are patches chosen by how big they would be on screen, loaded for the view and dropped when they leave it: a quadtree globe, which is what Cesium and Google Earth are. It is a real piece of work: which patches to show, seams between patches at different levels, the poles where the tiles pinch to a point, and the layers (ice, vegetation multiplied, shelf, floor, age, stripes) as the same quadtree. `map.js` already knows how to choose and fetch tiles; the scene should borrow its loader and take textures from it rather than grow its own.

**A handover to `map.js` at some zoom.** The seam already does this at 2.6 million years: a short fade between two paintings of one view. The same fade could happen at a span, say 60°, from the ball to the flat map. It is cheap and keeps `map.js` whole, but it is two approaches again, and she would see a perspective ball become a flat Mercator-bent picture in the fade, the shapes changing as it went.

**Something better: the morph.** The sphere mesh's vertices move, as she zooms in, from their places on the ball to their places on the map's flat frame; the texture coordinates do not change, because the tiles and the pictures are equirectangular on both; the camera goes from perspective to flat; the Mercator bend is the map's own `fwY` applied to the vertices' latitude. Far out it is a ball; by the regional span it is the map's frame pixel for pixel, so the map's mark layer sits over it unchanged and the map's gestures take over. The flat deep map of today is the end state of the morph, which answers section 0's point 4 as well. *The world that moves*' fade from Mercator at 20° to plain equirectangular at 120° stays exactly as it is, as the flat end's own bend; the morph continues it past 120° into a sphere, which is what an equirectangular world always wanted to be. What she would see: the ball swells toward her, the horizon straightens and leaves the screen, the far side is gone, and by the time the Alps fill the view she is on the map she knows. Nothing is handed over; the camera is the map's view.

The morph does not save the tile work: at level 5 no single texture is possible, so patches are needed whichever way. It only makes the join invisible. The honest cost of this section is the largest in the report (section 10), and it is the step that decides whether there is one Earth or two.

## 7. What Drain the Sea needs from a round globe

Building on the 24 Oct audit, which stands: one owner of the year, the probe off the bracket, one owner of the sea, the colours into the style file, the ice back on. Those are the bench's to do on the 2D map now and are not repeated. What is new with a round Earth:

**The sea, lowered** — *the Earth's.* The globe already lowers the sea this side of the seam (step 8, 20 Oct): at the ground grade it reads the floor's depth from the pyramid's level-1 floor tiles and paints floor that is above the new sea as land by its height above the new shore. On the card that is the sphere's fragment shader with the floor as a depth texture and the sea level as a number: where the depth is less than the lowering, the bared floor in the held note's grey ramp with relief from the floor's slope, elsewhere the glassy water. Close in, the floor's data tiles (levels 0 to 4) feed the same shader as patches. Two surfaces are possible and honest on a sphere: the floor as a displaced surface (the real heights, exaggerated and said so) and the water as a second, translucent sphere whose radius is the sea level, so that lowering the sea is literally lowering a sphere. That is the picture the held note's "glassy water over the grey floor" wants, and it is a design choice for the bench, not a necessity.

**The floor's age and the stripes** — *the Earth's.* Layers in Spec-Maps' sense, already rendered as data tiles and read by `map.js`'s data-layer block. On the ball they are textures blended over the floor by a strength each, as patches close in. The colour tables (the age ramp's seven stops, the two stripe tones) move to `cw-style.js` first, as the 24 Oct audit asks.

**The spreading ridges and the arrows** (the held note, on by default) — *the Earth's.* Lines on the sphere from the plate-boundary model, the ridge as a soft orange glow line; drawn in the scene.

**The probe on a sphere** — *the Earth's ring, the story's words.* The standard says one finger turns the ball; Spec-Drain says one finger moves the probe and never the map. They meet where the held note already put them: a *Probe* word turns the probe on and off, off by default, so touching the Earth turns it, and with the probe on, one finger moves the ring over the ball and the ball holds still. The spec's edge drift becomes the ball turning slowly to keep the ring in view, capped, stopping when her finger stops. Two fingers turn and zoom while the probe is out (`twoFinger`, the bench's switch). The reading is a ray from her finger to the sphere, giving a longitude and latitude, then `map.sample` as now, which reads the data tiles in JavaScript. The ring lives in the scene, lying on the surface (section 2); the strip, the age in words, the needle from the polarity table, and the probe's mark on the timeline stay as they are. The probe shelf file rides the map's surface today (`pixelOf`, `lonLatAt`, `panBy`, `sample`); it would take the Earth's equivalents behind the same names.

**The depth gauge** — *the story's.* HTML and SVG beside the stage as now; its drag sets the sea. The 24 Oct audit's point about whose sea it is comes first.

**The water ball at true scale** — *the story's, in the scene.* The water drained so far, from the bench's volume table (`ocean-volume.json`), as a real sphere: the radius is the cube root of the volume, the whole ocean about 1,370 km across against the Earth's 12,742, a little over a tenth of the diameter. A real sphere beside the Earth, lit by the same light, the five blues of the page's `drawBall` turning into one glassy material from the style file. Where it sits is Michael's: today it sits on New Mexico; in 3D it can sit in space beside the Earth, or on the surface where the water would have been, and in space it is at true scale by construction, which the flat ellipse only claimed.

**One thing the round globe makes worse.** The spec's "a tap on the sea slides the map in to a zoom where the stripes are a comfortable width" is the map's `fit`; on the ball it is a camera move toward the spot, and with the morph it ends on the map's frame, which is where the stripes are readable. Without the morph the probe lives on the ball at the ball's resolution, and the stripes do not show until the tiles are on the ball.

## 8. The inside

**What the Cut Open sketch is** (`prototypes/3d-sketches/early-earth.html`, 364 lines, an artifact page without the page standard): three.js r147 from jsDelivr with OrbitControls; a sphere missing a quarter (`SphereGeometry` with `phiLength` of 270°) and two half-disc `CircleGeometry` faces closing the cut; the outside painted per pixel in JavaScript into a 512 by 256 canvas texture whenever the slider moves (sixteen seeded plate centres making cracks where two are near, 26 seeded rafts growing with the slider, a sea tint, the cracks as an emissive map that breathes); the two cut faces 1,024-px canvases redrawn *every frame* and re-uploaded (the mantle gradient, 280 specks stirring in four cells, the liquid core, two drips per face with six melt blobs each rising to the raft beside, the crust band, a trench glow, the sea line, the rafts, and on one face six labels with leader lines). Scale: the core at 3,480 of 6,371 km, true; the crust at 0.965 of the radius, which is 220 km, five to thirty times the real crust; the page says "several times thicker".

**What *Cut open* needs.**

- *The partial sphere and the faces:* `cutaway.js` on the shelf already does this in the standard's way: a sphere with a quarter out, two faces, and the faces drawn by a TSL function of `q`, the point's place on the face with the centre at zero and the surface at one, so a page draws the inside by radius and angle, the same on both faces, with `side` to vary the seed. `setCut(on)` closes the ball and hides the faces: that is the word *Cut open* and its undoing. The face is picked by raycast and `radiusOf(hit)` says how deep she touched.
- *The cut-face drawing* rewritten as that function: the mantle and core by radius are gradients (easy); the stirring specks become a noise flow from `noise.js` (the drift of `cells3` is made for this); the drips are a few parametrised shapes by angle and phase, which is how the sketch makes them, so they translate; the melt blobs the same; the labels leave the face and become the honesty block and leader lines in HTML or projected. Re-uploading two megapixel canvases a frame is what the sketch did; on the iPad it is the thing to stop doing.
- *The scale note:* `honest.js` gives the block under the stage ("Honest about the picture": what, says, bracket) and `still.js` writes the same lines onto the kept picture. The standard wants the note on the picture itself with a thin bracket beside the stretched part; `honest.js` puts it under the stage. The crust needs the note and the bracket; the core and mantle, to scale, say nothing.
- *The inside by age, lined up with the outside.* One age drives both. From 4,567 to 4,400 the outside is the molten sequence; the inside then is a magma ocean, so the face is one glow with the crust appearing at the rim exactly as the outside's crust progress `t` says, and the Moon's flash melting the face from the top down, which the words already say. From 4,400 to 2,500 the sketch's inside (drips, melt, rafts) is right, and the outside is the invented blobs then the guessed cratons: the rafts at the cut's rim must be where a blob's or a craton's mesh meets the cut, which the face function can read from the same state (the pieces' meshes end at the cut plane; the face's crust band continues them). After that the drips are wrong: the inside becomes plates sinking as slabs, and today's Earth has a solid inner core (1,220 km across, which formed somewhere between half a billion and one and a half billion years ago; the sketch rightly has none in the Hadean). So there are three insides by era (molten, dripping, plates) and the cut face shows which one the age is in, blending between them as the outside blends. The inside is *inferred* at best today (seismology) and *guessed* before; per the standard it is painted, not photographed, and its sentence should say so beside the outside's grade.
- *The cut and the gestures:* the wedge faces the camera's home view; the one-finger turn turns the wedge away. Either the wedge follows the camera (always facing her, which hides nothing she turned to see) or it stays with the ball (she turns the ball to look into the wedge, which is how the sketch works). The sketch's way is honest; a word *Cut open* that also turns the cut toward her is the kinder one.

**The shelf pieces the Earth needs first, and which exist.** All six on the shelf exist as of today, committed at 12:37 and hours old (section 0): `scene.js` (251 lines: the WebGPU renderer with the WebGL 2 fallback, the three tiers by timing frames, the trackball turn, pinch, two-finger move, tap to pick, *Reset view*, reduced motion with *Let it move*, the phone's still with its line); `cutaway.js` (72 lines); `noise.js` (45 lines: `noise3`, `fbm3`, `hash3`, `cells3` in TSL); `glow.js` (38 lines, the Sun's back-glow); `honest.js` (48 lines); `still.js` (78 lines, the 4,096-px kept picture). `lens.js` does not exist. The Earth needs them in this order: `scene.js` (with the Earth's own turn, section 0 point 7), `noise.js` (the grain, the molten crust, the stirring), `honest.js` and `still.js` (the picture bench's job, replacing `globe-250.html`), then `cutaway.js` when *Cut open* is built. Not `glow.js`: the Earth's haze is a rim, and the standard's "one glow per object at most" is better spent on nothing. The shelf is ES modules on an import map, the first on the site; the Time Machine would gain an import map and a `type="module"` script beside its classic ones, which the standard's "everything else as before" did not foresee and CLAUDE.md allows ("prefer ES modules if modularization deepens").

## 9. The standard

**three.js r186 is in the site** as of today: `js/vendor/three-r186/` holds `three.webgpu.js` (2.28 MB), `three.core.js` (1.46 MB), `three.tsl.js` (37 KB) and the licence, 3.6 MB on disk (about 740 KB gzipped over the wire, the shelf session's figure), loaded by `experiments/sun-3d.html` through an import map; npm 0.186.1, tagged 24 Sep 2026, the release the standard names. Committed today (`ac41b13`), registered in `MANIFEST.md` and the experiments index, not pushed.

**What the WebGL 2 fallback costs this Earth.** Little. The Earth needs no compute (no particles), its shaders are texture reads and a few mixes per pixel, and a float texture for a plate table (if one is ever wanted) is ordinary on WebGL 2. The cost is the same page on an older iPad, which the tiers answer. The one thing to check on the fallback is texture size: a 2017 iPad's WebGL 2 allows 4,096 or 8,192 per side depending on the chip, and the full tier's 8,192 picture must be refused gracefully where it is not allowed, which `scene.js` does not yet check.

**What the Earth's tiers would turn down.** The pixel ratio (the shelf does it); the ground picture (8,192, 4,096, 2,048); the sphere's segments (160 by 80, then fewer); the noise octaves in the crust, the molten and the ice; the deepest tile level she can reach close in (5, 4, 3); the cut face's detail; the kept picture never (always full). Shadows: none at any tier. A lit ball has a day side and a night side, which is light, not a shadow map; the standard's shadow row does not apply.

**The one thing that must survive the floor.** The continents where they were at the age she set, moving as the year moves, with the word for how we know it readable (solid or painted). That is the globe's reason to exist and it is what the 2D engine fought hardest to keep (the fast flag, the half-resolution skin, play stepping). For Drain the Sea add the sea lowering; for the inside, the crust sinking, which is the standard's own example. The plates moving is the guess in the prompt, and it is right, with the knowing attached to it.

**Where the 2D globe does something the standard forbids.** It runs on phones (section 0, point 3). It has no tiers: one half-resolution skin for every machine, which is a floor with no full. It sits on parchment, which the standard allows (the page stays parchment; only a scene in space may go near-black); a lit Earth in the Time Machine stays on parchment, and a cut-open Earth on its own page may want the warm near-black. It never measured its frame rate on the iPad against a number; "it runs, and he liked it" was the test.

**What the standard asks for that the globe has never done.** Pinch (the disc does not zoom); two fingers moving the view; *Reset view* as a word (the map's `reset` is the nearest); the marks of knowing in the picture itself (today the guessed and invented pieces are softened and washed, which is half of it; the inferred and fitted ages look like the measured); a scale note (nothing to note on a true ball; the flat view is a projection, not a stretch); the kept picture at full tier with its notes and credit (the picture bench's `cwSave` was a PNG of the canvas, no notes); reduced motion (the globe never moves on its own, so it passes by accident); the ten-minute heat check and the 1 GB memory budget, never measured; the frame-rate budget of 60 on the iPad at full, never measured.

**Where the standard and the rulings disagree, for Michael.** The one-finger turn (the standard: as you would turn a globe in your hand, which `scene.js` makes a trackball) against the deep-time ruling 3 (north up at rest, the poles the limits, a drag right moves the surface east). A globe on a stand turns like a globe on a stand. The Earth should keep ruling 3 and the standard should say which gesture a thing with an axis gets.
