# Have You Thought of a Story?

Every morning the same question, and every morning the same answer.

"Have you thought of a story?"

"No."

That went on for days. I'll get to the morning it stopped, but I have to start with the rain, because the rain is the reason there was a question at all.

## The rain

In the summer of 1816 the poet Lord Byron rented a house on a hill above Lake Geneva, in Switzerland. He was twenty-eight and the most famous poet in Europe, and he had just left England, where a good many people were glad to see him go. With him was his physician, John Polidori, who was twenty and had been a physician for less than a year.

Ten minutes' walk down the hill, in a smaller house by the water, were three more English people. Percy Shelley was a poet too, twenty-three, not yet famous. Claire Clairmont was eighteen. And Claire's stepsister, Mary Godwin, was eighteen as well. Mary had grown up in London in a house where writers came to dinner. Her father was a famous one, and her mother, who died when Mary was eleven days old, was more famous still. The three of them walked up the hill to Byron's house nearly every day.

It should have been a summer of boats and walks. Instead it rained. It rained for days at a time, cold rain, in June, and the lake went grey and the mountains disappeared. Nobody in that house knew why. Nobody in Europe knew why. The reason was a volcano on the far side of the world, which had blown up the year before and thrown so much dust into the high air that it dimmed the sun for a year. That is its own story. Here it is enough to know that it rained, and five people were shut indoors with the candles lit in the afternoon.

## The dare

To pass the time they read to each other. Someone had a book of ghost stories, German ones translated into French, and they read them aloud in the evenings with the rain on the windows. One night Byron said: we will each write a ghost story.

Everybody agreed. Then almost nobody did it. Byron wrote a few pages about a man who dies strangely in a graveyard in Turkey, and lost interest. Shelley started something about his own childhood and dropped it. Polidori had an idea about a lady with a skull for a head, who had been punished that way for looking through a keyhole, and he could not think what to do with her.

Mary had nothing.

## Nothing

She wanted a story that would make the reader afraid to look behind her. She thought about it all day. Nothing came. She thought harder, and less came. She said later that it is the worst feeling a writer has: you ask, and nothing answers.

And each morning somebody asked, "Have you thought of a story?" and each morning, she wrote, she "was forced to reply with a mortifying negative." Mortifying is the kind of embarrassed that makes you want to leave the room.

## The talk

What happened next did not happen while she was trying.

Byron and Shelley talked. They talked for hours, late into the night, about everything, and Mary sat and listened and said almost nothing. One night the talk was about life: what it is, whether anyone would ever find out what makes a body alive, and whether a dead body could be made to move again.

Shelley had heard that an English physician named Erasmus Darwin had kept a piece of vermicelli in a glass case until it began to move by itself. He hadn't, quite. The real story was about tiny animals in water, and it had been garbled on its way across Europe. Mary knew that; when she wrote the talk down she was careful to say she was reporting what people said he had done, not what he did.

And there was electricity. Thirty years earlier an Italian named Luigi Galvani had made the legs of a dead frog kick by touching them with two metals, and ever since, people had argued about whether electricity was the thing that made living things alive. When Mary was five, in London, a man named Aldini had run electricity through the body of a hanged man in front of a crowd. The jaw moved. One eye opened. A hand clenched. The whole city talked about it, and Mary grew up in that city.

So perhaps a corpse could be brought back. Perhaps the parts of a creature could be put together and given warmth. That was the talk. It went past midnight.

## The night

She went to bed and could not sleep. Then, with her eyes shut, she saw something. Not a dream. She was awake, and she said so.

She saw a student kneeling beside a thing he had put together. She saw the thing stretched out, a shape like a man, and then some engine was worked and it stirred. The student, sick at what he had made, ran from the room and hoped it would die on its own. He slept. He opened his eyes, and it was standing by his bed, looking at him.

She opened her own eyes, frightened, and tried to get the real room back: the dark, the shutters, the lake she knew was out there beyond them. The picture would not go. And then she saw what she had. What had frightened her would frighten anyone. She only had to write down what she had seen.

In the morning, when the question came, she said yes.

## What she wrote

She began that day. The first sentence she wrote is still in the book, some way in, at the start of the chapter where the creature wakes: "It was on a dreary night of November, that I beheld the accomplishment of my toils." Everything in the book before that sentence was written afterward, to lead up to it.

She thought it would be a few pages. Shelley told her it could be a whole book, and it took her most of a year. By the time she finished she had married him and was Mary Shelley, which is the name people know her by now. When the book came out, on New Year's Day 1818, there was no name on it at all. She was twenty. Nearly everyone who guessed guessed Shelley.

*More:*

The student's name is Victor Frankenstein. The thing he makes has no name; in the book it is the creature, or the wretch, or the monster. Nearly everyone now calls the monster Frankenstein, which is the maker's name, not the made thing's.

There is no lightning. Mary never says how the student does it; he refuses to tell, which is what makes it a story about science and not a story about magic. The lightning, the bolts in the neck, and the flat head all belong to a film made in 1931.

The volcano was Tambora, in what is now Indonesia, and its story is the loudest sound in recorded history. The summer it spoiled is *The Year Without a Summer*. Byron sat in the same dim house that July and wrote a poem called "Darkness," about the sun going out.

Polidori did not waste the summer either. He took Byron's abandoned pages, the man who dies strangely, and turned them into *The Vampyre*, the first vampire story in English. It was published in 1819 under Byron's name by mistake, which annoyed them both.

Byron had a daughter, six months old, whom he left behind in England that spring and never saw again. Her name was Ada. She has her own story, and it is about a machine.

Mary told all this in 1831, when the book was fifteen years old and the publisher asked her how a girl of eighteen had come to write it. Polidori kept a diary that summer, and the two do not quite agree. His diary says the ghost stories were begun "by all but me" on June 17; her account has her sitting blank for days. Most historians think the whole thing took a few days, not weeks, and that Mary, remembering fifteen years later, remembered the misery as longer than it was. Nobody can settle it. The diary is short and hers is the only account of the night.

The book she wrote in 1816 and the book she revised in 1831 are not the same. The 1818 one is the stranger and the better, and it is the one to read.

## Notes for us (not the child)

**The beat.** One: nothing came while she was trying, and then it came, at night, as a picture, out of talk that was not about ghost stories at all. Everything else (the house, the rain, the dare, the frog, the hanged man) is there to carry the reader to the night. If a cut is needed, cut from "The talk," never from "Nothing" or "The night."

**What changed from the seeds.** The two nodes (frankenstein, villa-diodati) were panel-plus-more: every fact, no scene. This puts the reader in the house and moves in order: rain, dare, nothing, talk, night, morning. The unhappy facts about the party (Shelley's abandoned wife, Claire's pregnancy, Mary's lost baby) are out. They are true, but they are not the beat and they would pull a ten-year-old off it. They belong in the Diodati or Claire stories if those get written. Byron's disgrace is one clause. The Arctic frame is out entirely (see the hub's correction). The "science fiction, not magic" point moved to More.

**Voice.** First person only for narrating ("I'll get to the morning it stopped," "I have to start with the rain"). No reactions reported as mine. No sentence faces her. The closest to a moral is "What happened next did not happen while she was trying," which is a fact about the sequence and stays on Mary; watch it on the read-through and cut if it feels like a lesson. The ending is a fact (no name on it; everyone guessed Shelley), which opens the hidden-names thread without saying so.

**Names she may not know.** Byron, Shelley, Polidori, Claire, Erasmus Darwin, Galvani, Aldini all get their two sentences in place. Mary's parents are not named (Wollstonecraft and Godwin) because the names would carry nothing yet; "her mother was more famous still" does the work. The year 1816 has no anchor ("the year after Waterloo" assumes Waterloo). The timeline carries the distance; if a prose anchor is wanted, suggest one.

**Words.** "Mortifying" is explained in its sentence. "Physician" not "doctor." "Dare" not "wager" (a wager is money). No "test." "Creature" and "monster" both used, as the book does.

**Honesty checks.** *"The most famous poet in Europe"*: fair for 1816; Goethe is the other candidate, and "poet" rather than "writer" keeps it safe. *"Glad to see him go"*: the separation scandal; true and vague on purpose. *"Physician for less than a year"*: Polidori took his Edinburgh MD in August 1815 at nineteen. *"Nearly every day"*: Polidori's diary supports it. *"Nobody in Europe knew why"*: reports of the eruption had reached London by late 1815, but no one connected them to the weather; the story says "knew why," not "had heard of it." *Byron's fragment*: the narrator's companion Darvell dies in a Turkish cemetery; "dies strangely in a graveyard in Turkey" is fair. *Shelley's story*: Mary says "founded on the experiences of his early life"; nothing survives. *"Thought harder, and less came"*: a gloss on "blank incapability of invention"; check that it reads as hers. *Vermicelli*: Mary's word; the 1831 text says "a piece of vermicelli in a glass case." The modern reading (vorticella, a microscopic animal) is the standard one and is stated as "tiny animals in water." *Galvani*: 1780s, frog legs, two dissimilar metals; "thirty years earlier" is right for 1786–1791 vs 1816. *Aldini*: Forster's jaw quivered, the left eye opened, the right hand rose and clenched, the legs moved; "the whole city talked about it" is a gloss on the extensive press coverage. Mary was five (born 30 Aug 1797). *"She said so"*: "I did not sleep, nor could I be said to think." *The engine*: "on the working of some powerful engine." *The room*: "the dark parquet, the closed shutters, with the moonlight struggling through, and the sense I had that the glassy lake and white high Alps were beyond." *"Some way in, at the start of the chapter where the creature wakes"*: vol. I ch. IV in 1818, ch. V in 1831; the story avoids the number. *"Most of a year"*: June 1816 to about May 1817. *Married*: 30 Dec 1816. *Published 1 Jan 1818*: Lackington, 500 copies, anonymous, with a preface by Percy and a dedication to Godwin, which is why guesses went to Percy. *"She was twenty"*: 20 in January 1818. *"By mistake"*: the New Monthly Magazine printed *The Vampyre* as Byron's; both denied it. *Dates disagreement*: Polidori 17 June, "The ghost-stories are begun by all but me"; the majority reading (e.g. Charles Robinson's edition of the Frankenstein notebooks) puts the proposal around 16 June and the vision within a few days; stated as "most historians think" and "nobody can settle it." *"The 1818 one is the stranger and the better"*: an opinion, stated as one, and Michael's to vouch for or cut.
