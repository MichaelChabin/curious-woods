## 1. Little balls

**Summary (36 words)**

**More (333 words)**

```
About 600 million years ago.
```

Near the town of Weng'an ("wung-AHN"), in Guizhou, in southern China, phosphate is mined from a layer of dark rock. Phosphate is dug for fertiliser. But geologists who break the rock and soak it in acid, the same acid that is in vinegar, find something else left at the bottom of the dish: little balls, thousands of them, each about half a millimetre across, about the size of a grain of sand.

Under a microscope, the balls are made of cells. Some hold two cells, some four, some eight, and some many hundreds, packed together like soap bubbles.

That is what an animal egg looks like as it begins to divide. In 1998 two teams, one led by Shuhai Xiao and one by Chia-Wei Li and Junyuan Chen, said these were animal embryos, the oldest ever found.

So is that what they are?

Nobody is sure. In 2007 another team said they were more likely giant bacteria, like some that live on the seafloor today. In 2011 a team led by Therese Huldtgren looked inside the balls without breaking them, using a machine that makes very bright X-rays. In some of the cells they saw what seemed to be nuclei, and in others what looked like the balls breaking open to release something. They argued that the balls were single-celled creatures, perhaps distant relatives of animals, but not animals. In 2016 a Chinese team argued back that some of the balls divide in a way only animal embryos do. Other scientists say the balls may simply have divided unevenly, which proves nothing either way.

Even the age is argued. The rock at Weng'an has never been dated in a way everyone accepts, so geologists match it with dated layers elsewhere in China. One matching says about 609 million years. Another says no older than about 587.

What would settle it is a ball that grew up: a fossil of the same creature, older and bigger, in the same rock. None has been found.

## 2. Gaskiers

**Label:** Gaskiers
**Age:** 580 Ma · ± 0.5 Ma · radiometric (uranium–lead) on ash beds below and above the glacial rubble: between 580.90 ± 0.40 and 579.88 ± 0.44 Ma (Pu 2016)
**Tail:** none — a dated moment
**Known from:** glacial rubble in Newfoundland, with ash beds above and below it
**Place now:** Gaskiers, St. Mary's Bay, Avalon Peninsula, Newfoundland, Canada (46.87 N, 53.62 W)
**Kind:** earth event
**Replaces:** sketch *Snowball two*'s "a smaller one at 580"; a new mark

**Summary (35 words)**
About 580 million years ago. Ice came back, but not over the whole Earth this time. Its rubble lies on the shore of Newfoundland. A few million years later, the oldest large fossils appear. *More*

**More (350 words)**

```
About 580 million years ago.
```

On St. Mary's Bay, on the Avalon Peninsula of Newfoundland, is the small community of Gaskiers. The rock along its shore is a jumble: stones of every size, some of them scratched, set in hardened mud. In some layers single stones sit in fine, even mud, as if they had been dropped into it from above.

That is what ice leaves. A glacier grinds up rock and carries it, and scratches the stones as they scrape against each other. An iceberg drifting over the sea drops its stones one at a time into the mud below as it melts. This was an ice age, and it is named after Gaskiers.

There are thin beds of volcanic ash in the rock too, a few metres below the rubble and just above it. Ash can be dated, and in 2016 Judy Pu and her colleagues dated it. The ice came after about 580.9 million years ago and was gone by about 579.9 million. Dates from another part of Newfoundland narrowed it further, to perhaps less than 340,000 years, which is short for an ice age this old.

So was it a third snowball, like the two before it?

The team said no. Ice that covered the whole Earth would have lasted millions of years before it melted, as the snowballs did. This ice came and went too fast. Rubble that may be of about the same age is found on at least eight of the old continents, and some of it formed nearer the equator than the poles, but the ice probably did not close over the whole sea.

In 2024 another team found stones dropped by ice hundreds of metres lower in the rock, which would mean the cold began earlier and lasted between one and nearly five million years. That is still argued.

About forty kilometres away, at Mistaken Point, the rock laid down after the ice holds the oldest large fossils that most geologists accept. They appear a few million years after the ice, perhaps six, perhaps nine. Whether the ice had anything to do with them, nobody knows.

## 3. Mistaken Point

**Label:** Mistaken Point
**Age:** 574 Ma · ± 0.7 Ma · radiometric (uranium–lead) on ash in the upper Drook Formation, the oldest fossil layer there (574.17 ± 0.66 Ma); the famous E Surface is 565.00 ± 0.64 Ma
**Tail:** none
**Known from:** thousands of imprints of soft bodies, buried by volcanic ash where they lived
**Place now:** Mistaken Point Ecological Reserve, Avalon Peninsula, Newfoundland, Canada (46.64 N, 53.21 W)
**Kind:** place
**Replaces:** sketch *Fronds in the dark* (the Charnia half is now *Charnia*)

**Summary (40 words)**
About 574 million years ago. Fronds grew on a dark seafloor, too deep for sunlight. Ash from volcanoes buried them where they stood. They are among the oldest large living things ever found, and nobody knows what they were. *More*

**More (313 words)**

```
About 574 million years ago.
```

At Mistaken Point, at the southeastern tip of the Avalon Peninsula of Newfoundland, tilted sheets of rock slope down to the sea. On the rock are thousands of imprints: fronds like feathers, spindles, discs. Some are a few centimetres long and some nearly two metres. Visitors walk out with a guide, and before they step on the rock they put on cloth booties, so that their shoes won't wear the fossils away.

In June 1967 Shiva Balak Misra, a student from India at Memorial University in St. John's, was mapping the rocks here with his assistant, Paul Thompson, when he found them.

The creatures lived on the seafloor and were buried where they stood by ash from volcanoes, which drifted down through the water and covered them like snow. The ash can be dated. The best-known surface is about 565 million years old, and the oldest fronds at Mistaken Point are about 574 million.

The seafloor was deep. The layers above and below the fossils were laid down far below the reach of storm waves, perhaps hundreds of metres down, perhaps more. Sunlight does not reach that far. So the fronds were not plants or seaweed: there was no light for them to live on.

Look closely at a frond and each branch is made of smaller branches shaped like it, and each of those of smaller branches still. A shape built that way has a great deal of surface for its size.

None of the fronds has a mouth, a gut or a leg. Many palaeontologists think they lived by soaking up food dissolved in the water, through all that surface. What kind of living thing they were is argued: some think they were early animals, unlike any alive now; nobody has shown it for certain.

Similar fronds turn up in England, in Russia and in Australia, but the oldest are here.

**More (349 words)**

```
About 562 million years ago.
```

In the summer of 1956, Tina Negus, who was fifteen, persuaded her family to go picking bilberries in Charnwood Forest, in Leicestershire, though what she was really looking for was fossils. On a rock in an old quarry, half hidden by roots, she saw the shape of a leaf, or a fern, pressed into the stone.

She had read that the rocks of Charnwood were Precambrian ("pree-KAM-bree-un"): older than the time, about 539 million years ago, when fossils of shells and bones begin to fill the rocks. Most geologists then thought there were no fossils in Precambrian rocks. She told her geography teacher, and the teacher told her the same.

So it was left there.

In the spring of 1957, Roger Mason, a schoolboy in Leicester, was climbing in an old quarry in the forest with two friends, Richard Blachford and Richard Allen, and he saw the same kind of shape. He made a rubbing of it with paper and pencil and showed it to his father, who knew a geologist at the university, Trevor Ford. Ford came to look. Quarrymen lifted out the block, and in 1958 Ford named the fossil *Charnia masoni* ("CHAR-nee-uh MAY-son-eye"), after the forest and after Roger.

*Charnia* was a frond, like a feather standing on the seafloor, with branches made of smaller branches. It had no mouth and no gut. Nobody is sure what it was.

It was not the first fossil found in Precambrian rock. Strange imprints had been found in Australia and Namibia before. But those had been taken to be younger. Charnwood's rocks were known to be Precambrian, so *Charnia* could not be explained away, and it helped convince geologists that large living things existed before the Cambrian. In 2015 the rock was dated: about 562 million years old.

The fossil itself is in Leicester Museum & Art Gallery, on New Walk.

Fifty years later, in 2007, Tina Negus and Roger Mason met at a meeting in Leicester to celebrate the find. Roger Mason said she had seen the frond before he did, and that nobody had taken her seriously.

```
About 558 million years ago.
```

On the shores of the White Sea, in the far north of Russia, cliffs of sandstone sixty to a hundred metres high stand over the water. A few years before 2018, Ilya Bobrovskiy, a young geologist studying for his doctorate at the Australian National University, came here by helicopter. He hung over the edge of the cliffs on ropes, dug out great blocks of sandstone, threw them down, washed them and searched them, again and again, looking for one fossil.

The fossil was *Dickinsonia* ("dick-in-SO-nee-uh"): a flat oval with ribs across it, like a bathmat on the seafloor. The smallest are a few millimetres long and the biggest more than a metre.

Reg Sprigg named it in 1947, after Ben Dickinson, one of the leaders of the mines department of South Australia, where it had first been found. Sprigg thought it was a jellyfish. Since then it has been called a worm, a lichen, a giant single cell, and a kind of life with no living relatives.

Nobody could settle it by looking, because the shape could be read so many ways. Bobrovskiy was looking for something else. Some of the White Sea fossils still carry a thin film, all that is left of the creature's body, and in the film are molecules that last for hundreds of millions of years.

Living things make fatty molecules called sterols, and different kinds of life make different ones. Fungi make one kind, green algae another. Animals make almost only cholesterol. When Bobrovskiy and his colleagues measured the film, nearly all of it was the cholesterol kind. In 2018 they argued that *Dickinsonia* was an animal, one of the oldest yet found.

Some palaeontologists were not convinced. One pointed out that a few fungi make cholesterol too; another doubted that the film was really *Dickinsonia*'s own. Many now think it was an animal, but where it fits among animals is still argued.

*Dickinsonia* had no mouth that anyone has found. In 2025 two scientists argued that the film suggests it fed on the mats of microbes that covered the seafloor.

In 1972 Gerard Germs, a geologist working in Namibia, described small fossils from limestone in the south of the country. Each was a tube a few millimetres across, made of little cones stacked one inside the next, like a pile of paper cups. He named them *Cloudina* ("cloud-EE-nuh"), after Preston Cloud, an American geologist who studied the oldest fossils.

The tubes were made of shell, the same stuff as a snail's shell or a seashell. The oldest yet found are in the Tsaus Mountains of southern Namibia, about 550 million years old. They are among the oldest shells anyone has found. Some living thing must have built each tube around itself, and palaeontologists generally think it was a small animal, but nobody knows what kind.

Shells are hard, so they last in rock when soft bodies do not. They also do something else. They protect what is inside.

So what were they protecting it from?

In the Dengying Formation of Shaanxi, in China, *Cloudina* tubes lie in rock of about the same age. In 2003 Hong Hua, Brian Pratt and Lu-Yi Zhang looked at nearly a hundred of them, and found that almost one in five had a small round hole bored through its wall. The holes are 15 to 85 thousandths of a millimetre wide, about as fine as a hair, or finer.

Holes like that, in shells today, are made by animals that drill through a shell to eat what is inside. So the holes in *Cloudina* are the oldest signs yet found of one animal hunting another.

The hunter has never been found. It may have had no hard parts at all, so that only its holes were kept.

There is one more clue. Another kind of shelly tube lies in the same rock, and none of those had holes. Whatever made the holes chose *Cloudina*.

Fortune Head is a point of land on the Burin Peninsula of Newfoundland, a short walk from the town of Fortune. From the lighthouse a path runs to the cliffs, where the layers of rock lie open to view. Taking a fossil from them is against the law.

In 1992 geologists from many countries agreed that one level in these cliffs, a little more than two metres up in one band of rock, marks the start of the Cambrian ("KAM-bree-un"), the stretch of time when fossils of animals with hard parts begin to fill the rocks.

They chose the level not by a shell but by a burrow. It is called *Treptichnus pedum* ("trep-TICK-nus PEE-dum"), and it is a tunnel in the mud that branches again and again, as if something with a front and a back pushed forward, backed up and pushed again, feeding as it went. Nobody has found the animal that made it.

In 2001 a team found the same burrow about four metres below the level. The line stays where it was agreed. It says when, not who was first.

After it, the rocks change. Within about twenty million years they hold shells, spines and armour, legs with joints and eyes, and by about 520 million years ago trilobites, armoured animals with many legs. Hard parts last, so these animals were kept.

But soft animals rot and leave little. How long were animals living before they had shells?

In rocks in Oman more than 635 million years old, there are molecules that some scientists read as made by sponges, which are animals. In 2021 another team argued that they could have come from algae, changed by time in the rock. Gene clocks, which estimate when animals split apart by counting the differences in their genes, put the first animals somewhere between about 830 and 650 million years ago. Nobody has found a fossil animal that old that everyone accepts.

Maotianshan ("maow-tyen-shahn") is a hill a few kilometres east of the town of Chengjiang ("chung-jyahng"), in Yunnan, in southern China. On the afternoon of Sunday, the first of July 1984, Hou Xianguang ("ho shyen-gwahng"), a palaeontologist from Nanjing, split a slab of yellowish mudstone on the hill and saw a white half-circle in it. He thought at first it was the shell of an unknown crustacean, an animal like a shrimp.

He kept splitting. Before long it was clear that the mudstone of Maotianshan held more than shells.

Most animals leave nothing in rock but their hard parts, if they have any. At Chengjiang, fine mud buried animals so quickly, and kept them so well, that their soft parts were kept too: guts, gills, eyes, the legs of animals with no shell at all. About 250 kinds have been found since, most of them known from nowhere else.

Among them is *Haikouichthys* ("high-koo-ICK-thiss"), a small, fishlike animal described in 1999, one of the oldest relatives of fish yet found, and so of everything with a backbone. Another is *Amplectobelua*, a swimming hunter with a pair of spiny grasping arms at its head. Its biggest fossils suggest it could grow to almost a metre.

The rock is dated by tiny crystals of zircon in it. The crystals formed in older rock and were washed in with the mud, so the date is the oldest the rock can be: about 518 million years. In older books it is 525 or 530.

In 2012 Chengjiang was made a World Heritage Site. There is a museum on the shore of nearby Fuxian Lake, and the rocks around the hill where Hou Xianguang split his slab are still giving up new animals.

High on Fossil Ridge, in Yoho National Park in British Columbia, between Wapta Mountain and Mount Field, is a small quarry in dark shale. The walk there and back is about 24 kilometres, with a climb of about 900 metres, and it can be made only with a guide.

Charles Walcott, the head of the Smithsonian Institution in Washington, found the fossils near here at the end of August 1909, and opened the quarry the next year. He and his family came back for years, until 1924, and took more than 65,000 fossils away. Like the fossils at Chengjiang, about twelve million years older, they kept animals' soft parts.

The strangest story in the quarry belongs to *Anomalocaris* ("uh-NOM-uh-lo-CARE-iss").

In 1892, before Walcott came, a curved, jointed piece of fossil from these mountains was taken to be the body of a shrimp-like animal without its head, and named *Anomalocaris*, "strange shrimp." In 1911 Walcott described a ring of plates with a hole in the middle as a jellyfish, and a flat body with flaps as a kind of sea cucumber.

Between 1978 and 1985 the pieces came together. Simon Conway Morris saw that the "jellyfish" belonged to the "sea cucumber", and Derek Briggs saw that the "shrimp" was a limb. Then in 1985 Harry Whittington and Derek Briggs showed that all three were parts of one animal. The "shrimp" was a grasping arm, one of a pair at the front of the head. The "jellyfish" was a mouth. The body swam by waving its flaps. It was a hunter, one of the biggest animals of its time.

In 2012 the puzzle turned once more. Allison Daley and Jan Bergström showed that the ring-shaped mouth and the body with flaps belonged to a close cousin, *Peytoia*. *Anomalocaris* had a mouth of its own, of a different shape.

The complete fossils of *Anomalocaris* are about twenty centimetres long. Some of its loose arms are much bigger, so it may have grown to nearly twice that length.

## 10. The map clears

**Label:** The map clears
**Age:** 500 Ma · ± 10 Ma · estimate; a time on the bar, not a measurement
**Tail:** none
**Known from:** the magnetism in rocks, which says how far north or south a continent was, and fossils, which say which shores were near each other

**Summary (39 words)**

**More (324 words)**

```
About 500 million years ago.
```

At Manuels River, in Conception Bay South, near St. John's in Newfoundland, the river cuts through dark shale full of trilobites, armoured sea animals with many legs. One of them is *Paradoxides davidis*, which can be bigger than a man's hand. It was found first near St Davids, in Wales, and named after David Homfray, a fossil collector.

On the other side of Newfoundland, the trilobites in the old rocks of the west coast are different. They are kin to the trilobites of Greenland and of the far northwest of Scotland.

So why would the two sides of one island be so different?

In 1966 the Canadian geophysicist J. Tuzo Wilson asked a question in the title of a paper: "Did the Atlantic close and then re-open?" His answer was yes. Western Newfoundland and northwest Scotland were once on one shore of an older ocean, and eastern Newfoundland, England and Wales on the other. The old ocean closed, and the two sides were pressed together. Then the Atlantic opened, but along a different line, and it left a piece of each shore stuck to the other.

That is how a map of the world 500 million years ago is made. The magnetism locked into a rock as it formed tells how far it was from the poles, but not how far east or west. Fossils help with the rest: animals that lived along the same shore are alike, and animals on opposite shores of a wide ocean are not.

The map that comes out has the southern lands, Africa, South America, Antarctica, Australia, India and Arabia, joined in one great continent called Gondwana, which reached from near the South Pole to beyond the equator. North America sat on the equator, apart. The sea was high, and shallow seas covered much of the land.

Before this, there are too few fossils with hard parts to use this way, and the map is far less sure.
