# Timeline stories

An event is anything that can be placed on a timeline and a map: a person, a volcano, a book, a crater, a crop. Every event carries three kinds of prose. Together they make a small story that she can find by tapping, on any timeline or map in Curious Woods.

## The rule that comes first: assume nothing

She knows only what Curious Woods has already given her. So an event never names a thing as if she has heard of it.

Write: "Homer is thought to have been a travelling poet who recited stories about the Trojan War. The first known written copies of his stories appear at this time."

Not: "Homer's Iliad and Odyssey written down."

The first version tells her who Homer was, what he did, and what happened at this date. The second assumes she knows all three.

## How a More is written (30 Sept 2026, from Michael's edits of the two samples)

A More follows Story-Voice.md. These habits come from Michael's rewrites of the two samples, which are the model (Timeline-Samples.md).

**Short paragraphs that turn.** A paragraph of one line either asks the question the next part answers ("So what did the builders eat?") or sums up what has just been said. Use them to pace the story.

**"Someone," not "somebody."**

**Explain only what the story leans on.** The assume-nothing rule is for the names and ideas the event depends on. A word the story does not lean on (flint) needs no explaining.

**Estimates that show size are welcome.** How many people, how long it took. Hedge them ("could have," "must have") and never pretend to be exact. Their job is to make the size of the thing felt.

**One argument at a time.** If the event has a real open question, keep to it. A side story, however good, is cut when it competes with the main question.

**Leave out what cannot be explained honestly in the space.** A mechanism that is more complex than it sounds (the tides slowing the Earth's spin) is cut, not half-explained. A half-explanation is worse than none.

**Open in the scene.** Put her where it happened, with what could be seen and heard, before saying what it was. Anything not recorded about this moment is said of the thing in general ("when the Moon covers the whole Sun, stars come out"), not claimed for this place and day.

**A More may end on what is still to be found.** "Most of the hill has not been explored" is a fact and an honest invitation. This stretches Story-Voice.md's "end on a fact or an image" for Mores only: an open question is allowed as the ending when it is true.

## Wars and religion (30 Sept 2026, Michael)

**Wars are tragic.** Some cannot be left out; Alexander is one. When a war appears, the event says what happened, what it cost in lives, money and effort, and what changed as a result. Where nobody recorded the cost, the event says so. It never tells a war as an adventure.

**Religion: plain words, no sides, no scripture as evidence.** Generic religious words are fine: temple, priest, god, pilgrimage. Two things are not. An event never favours one religion or sect over another. And an event never treats a religious text alone as evidence that something happened: Moses is not a historical person on the timeline. A fact that involves religion is stated as a fact: Giordano Bruno was burned in Rome in 1600.

Religious words still follow the honesty rule. Where calling something a temple is only a guess, the event says whose guess it is.

**For now, no calendar events.** Caesar's calendar, year 1 and the Gregorian reform are left out. They are more complex than they look (the several kinds of year), and they touch on religion.

## The three kinds of prose

**1. The label.** The words on the timeline or map. The label says what the event is about, as briefly as it can while still being clear. Where possible it is unique, so that no two events share a label. *Chicxulub impact*, *Eclipse stops a battle*, *Faraday's generator*.

**2. The summary.** It appears when she taps the event's mark on a timeline or its place on a map. It is 20 to 40 words. It includes the date, as a plain year. It says plainly what happened, so that she knows which event this is without having read anything else. It ends with the word *More*.

**3. The More.** It appears when she taps *More*. It is a story that stands alone: 250 to 350 words, in its own movable window, and it may carry pictures. Its date line gives the after-the-ice count and the ordinary date together (see Dates). It follows Story-Voice.md like any other story: one beat, open on something she can see, end on a fact or an image, never on a lesson. The window is the picture-in-a-window of the Rulings (backdrop, drag handle, close), so it opens in a thing she already knows how to shut.

If there are references, they go at the bottom of the More, and they do not count toward the 250 to 350 words.

## Dates

**Where zero is.** Timeline marks are placed as if year 0 was 12,026 years ago, when the ice began to melt. In the event store, years are astronomers' years (negative for BC), and each timeline adds its own zero, per Spec-Maps.md. After the Ice adds 10,000.

**The after-the-ice count** appears in two places: the subtitle of a full story that happens at a particular time and place, and the date line of a More. It gives the count and the ordinary date together: "11,905 years after the ice, or 1905"; "9,000 years after the ice, or 1000 BCE, or 3,026 years ago."

This reverses part of the 15 Sept Dates ruling, which had retired the count from story text.

**The date line of a More is set on stepped lines** (30 Sept 2026, Michael, an experiment). Each way of giving the date gets its own line, and each line is indented further than the one above:

```
About 500 years after the ice
      or about 9500 BCE
            or about 11,500 years ago.
```

```
9,415 years after the ice
      or 28 May 585 BCE
            or 2,611 years ago.
```

A recent date with only two parts ("11,905 years after the ice, or 1905") takes two lines. The build makes the steps with CSS, not with spaces typed into the text. A full story's subtitle may follow the same form; that is for Michael to decide once he has seen it on a page.

**In prose, and in the summary,** plain years, as before: 1905, 1000 BCE. "65 million years ago" is fine in prose.

**The missing year 0.** Calendars have no year 0 (1 BCE is followed by 1 CE), so a BCE date and its after-the-ice count can be a year apart. We ignore this. Dates that old are approximate, and nothing a child can do on the timeline makes the difference show.

**Deep time.** A timeline that reaches further back moves its zero earlier, so that every year on it is a positive number. For example, a timeline that reaches the dinosaurs might put its zero at the Chicxulub impact, or earlier. Each timeline says where its zero is and what it calls it. We aim to be consistent; where that causes trouble, we work it out case by case.

## Who writes events

Two sources, one pool.

**Stories write their own.** A story may write events that appear on its own timeline and map. Those events then join the larger pool, where any other timeline or map can use them.

**We also generate them.** A large and growing number of events is written directly, in quantity, for the timelines. The prompt for that is still to come; it will be built around the approved samples in Timeline-Samples.md.

In both cases the event's words are written once and checked once. A story that borrows an event does not rewrite its label, summary or More. It may add its own one-sentence "why," in its own voice, tying the event to that story. In Hokusai's story, Tambora's "why" is "and the next year Europe had no summer"; in Mary Shelley's, it will be the rain at Lake Geneva.

## What an event record holds

For the build: an id; the year (astronomers' year) and how well it is known (Spec-Maps.md: `exact`, `year`, `decade`, `century`, `millennium`); a place; a kind and a weight (Spec-Maps.md); the label; the summary; the More, its pictures (with source and licence for each) and its references; and links to any story it connects to. A borrowing story keeps its "why" in its own file. The date line is stored as its parts (count, ordinary date, years ago), not as one string, so the build can step it.
