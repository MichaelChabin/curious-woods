# Editor pass 5

## The page for Michael

**What was read.** Deep-time events, batch 1 (ten events, the oldest grains to cyanobacteria) and batch 2 (twelve, the 17-hour day to the thaw of the second snowball). Every label, summary, More, field, note and Checked line.

**The verdict.** Both batches stand once the fixes below are in. The numbers are good: I found no slip between million, billion and thousand, no age that disagrees with its source, and no figure that disagrees with the same figure elsewhere, except one rounding. All 22 are inside the counts, before and after the changes. There are five real faults, none of them large, and one habit.

- *Bangiomorpha* claims too much. The summary and the More call it the oldest fossil that can be named to a group alive today. Fossil cyanobacteria nearly twice as old are named to a living group without argument. The claim is true only of cells with nuclei, which is how Butterfield puts it and how our own *Cells with nuclei* puts it. Both now say so. The summary also called a fossil "the oldest living thing".
- *Big thwack* dates the best oxygen measurements to 2025. The paper (Fischer and others, *PNAS* volume 121) came out in December 2024.
- *The thaw* says geologists marked the Ediacaran spot with a plaque in 2004. The choice was ratified in 2004; the marker was unveiled on 16 April 2005, by the Premier of South Australia.
- *The thaw* also says a recent study blames "dark volcanic dust". I opened the paper. It argues for dust darkening the ice and does not say the dust was volcanic. The word goes.
- *Gathering* says hafnium "slowly turns into tungsten". The kind of hafnium that did so was used up within the Earth's first hundred million years or so, which is why the clock reads only that time. The sentence is now in the past tense and says the kind is gone.
- The habit: "scientists" fourteen times, all but two in batch 1, which was written a day before the rule was pressed. Each is now the exact word (geochemists, planetary scientists, climatologist, palaeontologists) or a name.

**Three smaller things in her text.** In *Snowball one*, the Budyko sentence had two *its* pointing at two things and ended on a line I had to read twice; it is rewritten in the same number of ideas. In *Kenorland*, Kenora is made from the first *two* letters of each name, not the first letters, and Bleeker's "ca." now gets a clause, since she cannot be expected to know it. In *Bangiomorpha*, the More leaned on mitochondria and cyanobacteria, which are explained only in other events; a More stands alone, so the sentence now explains itself.

**The "not confirmed" lines.** About two dozen items across the two files. Most never touch her text (coordinates, a reference's volume, traditional owners who are rightly not named). Of those that do:

- Cut or softened: "grey" for the shale in the Transvaal cores and for the rock at Sturt Gorge (no source gives the colour); the Slave craton has none of those dykes "that anyone has found"; the coast "may have begun" to split by 750 million; banded iron's silica layers are "red or grey", not "paler".
- Confirmed by opening a source: the ANU survey (more than 100,000 zircons, more than 5,000 older than four billion, so "one in twenty" is fair).
- Confirmed from my own knowledge, and marked as such below: animals kept off the Hamelin Pool mats by the salt; Flin Flon on the Trans-Hudson belt; Yellowknife on the Slave craton; Rodinia holding most of the land; the Rooney 2015 reference; the argument over how long the cap took.

**Results under five years old.** I searched for replies to three. The 4.16-billion-year date for Nuvvuagittuq (2025): no published reply found, and the one outside geologist quoted by the press supported it; "Not everyone is convinced yet" stays, with a Watch line. The Marinoan's four million years (2025): I opened the paper and it says what we say; I did not search further for a reply. And one that bears on *Bombardment*, though the More does not mention it: a crater in the Pilbara claimed in March 2025 as 3.47 billion years old, answered in July 2025 (younger than 2.7 billion), and since re-dated by its own finders to 3.02 billion. None of those is "from this time", so "The Earth has kept no crater from this time" is still true. It is a good story for another day.
