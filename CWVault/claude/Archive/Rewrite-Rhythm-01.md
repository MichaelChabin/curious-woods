<!-- MORE | Events-Batch-01.md | 1. Maize | 275 words -->

A wild grass called teosinte ("tay-oh-SIN-tay") grows on the hillsides of southwest Mexico. Its ear is about as long as your little finger and holds only five to twelve seeds, and each seed is locked inside a case so hard that you could break a tooth on it.

This grass is the ancestor of corn.

How do you get from one to the other?

Someone had to choose. Every year, the people who gathered teosinte kept some seeds to plant, and if they kept seeds from the plants they liked best, with bigger ears or softer cases, the next year's plants were a little more like that. Nobody planned corn. It happened a little at a time, over thousands of years, because people kept choosing.

We know roughly where it started because of the plant's own genes. Every kind of maize in the world is most closely related to the teosinte that grows in the Balsas River valley. In a rock shelter there, archaeologists found grinding stones with tiny grains of maize starch still stuck to them, and the grains are about 8,700 years old.

The earliest maize cobs that survive, from a dry cave in Oaxaca, are about 6,250 years old, and they are about as long as the top joint of your thumb.

A geneticist named George Beadle once heated teosinte seeds in a pan, and they popped. So one of the first things people could have done with teosinte, even before it changed, was make popcorn.

In the 2020s more maize was grown than any other grain.

Teosinte still grows wild in those hills, and it still has its hard little seeds.

<!-- END -->

<!-- MORE | Events-Batch-01.md | 2. Storegga slide | 293 words -->

In the Shetland Islands, north of Scotland, there is a layer of sand in the peat. The peat was made by plants, slowly, in quiet water, and the sand does not belong there. It was thrown in by the sea, and in some places it lies about twenty metres above where the sea was at the time.

Something threw the sea that high.

Off the coast of Norway, the sea floor ends at a steep edge, and beyond it the water is thousands of metres deep. About 8,200 years ago, a piece of that edge broke loose and slid down into the deep. It covered an area almost as big as Iceland, and the mud and sand that moved would bury the whole of Scotland about forty metres deep.

When that much sea floor moves at once, the water above it moves too. That is a tsunami: a wave caused not by wind but by the ground under the sea. The wave spread across the North Sea in a few hours, and its sand is found in Norway, Scotland, the Faroe Islands and Shetland.

Where the North Sea is now, there was land.

The ice had melted, and the sea was rising, but slowly. Between Britain and the rest of Europe lay low hills, marshes and rivers, where people hunted and fished. Archaeologists call it Doggerland. By the time of the slide much of it was already underwater, and what was left may have been islands.

Did the wave finish Doggerland? Some archaeologists think it did, and others think the islands lasted a while longer. Nobody knows how many people lived there, or how many were lost.

Fishing boats still pull up bones and stone tools from the bottom of the North Sea.

<!-- END -->

<!-- MORE | Events-Batch-01.md | 3. Writing at Uruk | 303 words -->

A piece of clay about the size of a bar of soap has marks pressed into it. Some of the marks are round holes, some are little wedges, and one looks like a jar with a pointed bottom.

The round holes and wedges are numbers. The jar means beer.

This is some of the oldest writing in the world. It was found in the ruins of Uruk, a city in what is now southern Iraq, which about 5,300 years ago was one of the largest cities anywhere. Archaeologists have dug up about five thousand tablets like it there.

What did the first writers write about?

Mostly, things to count: how much barley came in and how much went out, how many sheep, how much beer, how many workers and how much each was paid. Almost every early tablet is a record of this kind. A few are lists of words, which may have been how the writers learned the signs.

The writer held a sharpened reed and pressed it into clay while the clay was still soft, and then the tablet dried hard. A dried tablet lasts a very long time, and if a building burns down around it, it bakes and lasts even longer.

Some tablets end with a sign that may be a person's name: Kushim. It appears on several records of barley. If it is a name, Kushim is one of the first people in history whose name we can read, and what we know about Kushim is that he or she was keeping accounts. But the sign may be a title instead of a name, and nobody is sure.

Over the next thousand years, the signs became wedge-shaped marks called cuneiform ("kyoo-NAY-i-form"), and people used them to write laws, letters, stories and poems.

It started with beer and barley.

<!-- END -->

<!-- MORE | Events-Batch-01.md | 4. Ötzi | 290 words -->

In September 1991, two hikers crossing a pass high in the Alps saw a brown shape sticking out of melting ice. It was a man's head and shoulders, and they thought he was a climber who had died a few years before.

He had died about 5,300 years before.

He is called Ötzi ("UHT-see"), after the Ötztal, the valley below. The ice had kept not only his body but everything he carried, and nothing else like it has ever been found from that time.

What did a man carry, 5,300 years ago, on a walk through the mountains?

He had an axe with a blade of almost pure copper, and a bow of yew wood, taller than he was and not yet finished. His quiver held fourteen arrows, only two of them ready to shoot. He had a flint knife, a fur hat, a pouch with a fire-making kit, and shoes with bearskin soles, stuffed with grass to keep his feet warm.

Even his last meal is known. His stomach held ibex meat and bread made from an early kind of wheat.

He was about forty-five years old. His skin carries sixty-one tattoos, made of short lines rubbed with charcoal, and most are on his back and legs, over places where his joints would have ached.

For ten years, everyone thought he had died of cold. Then, in 2001, an X-ray showed a stone arrowhead in his left shoulder. Someone had shot him from behind, and he probably died soon after. Nobody knows who shot him, or why.

Ötzi is now in a museum in Bolzano, Italy. He is kept in a cold room, as cold as the glacier was, and visitors can look at him through a small window.

<!-- END -->

<!-- MORE | Events-Batch-01.md | 5. Drains of Mohenjo-daro | 279 words -->

Along a street of baked brick runs a drain. It is covered with more bricks, so the dirty water flows out of sight, and smaller drains come into it from the houses on each side. Many of the houses had a room with a brick floor for washing, and some had a toilet.

This was about 4,500 years ago.

The city is called Mohenjo-daro ("mo-HEN-jo DAH-ro"), and it stands beside the Indus River in what is now Pakistan. Tens of thousands of people may have lived there. Its streets were laid out in straight lines, and its bricks were made to the same proportions, as if everyone followed the same rules.

It was one of many cities along the Indus, and they traded with cities as far away as Iraq.

So why don't we know more about them?

Because nobody can read what they wrote.

The people of the Indus carved signs on small square stones called seals, often above a picture of an animal. Thousands of seals have been found, and there are about four hundred different signs. But most inscriptions are only four or five signs long, nobody knows what language they wrote, and no stone has been found with the same words in the Indus script and in a script we can read. Many people have claimed to read it, and none has convinced the others.

So we can walk the streets of Mohenjo-daro and see how carefully it was built, but we cannot read a single word its people left.

By about 1900 BCE, the city had been left empty, and nobody knows exactly why.

The seals are small enough to hold in your hand.

<!-- END -->

<!-- MORE | Events-Batch-01.md | 6. Campo del Cielo | 271 words -->

In a field in northern Argentina lies a lump of metal as heavy as five elephants. It is dark and pitted, with smooth hollows like thumbprints, and it is made of iron and nickel.

It came from space.

About 4,500 years ago, a mass of iron came down through the air over this plain and broke apart as it fell. The pieces hit the ground along a line almost twenty kilometres long and made more than twenty craters, the biggest of them more than a hundred metres across.

A piece of rock or metal from space that reaches the ground is called a meteorite. Iron meteorites are rare, because most meteorites that fall are stone.

The people who lived on the plain knew about the iron long before anyone wrote it down. In 1576, Spanish soldiers went looking for a great mass of iron that the local people used for their weapons, and they found it. The Moqoit people, who still live there, call the place Piguen N'onaxa. The Spanish name, Campo del Cielo, means field of the sky.

How do we know when it fell?

Trees were burned when the pieces hit, and the charcoal has been dated. The dates cluster around 4,000 to 4,700 years ago, and that is as close as anyone can get.

More than a hundred tonnes of the iron have been found, in pieces from the size of a pea to the size of a car. The largest, called Gancedo, weighs about thirty tonnes, and it is the third-heaviest meteorite ever found.

The great irons still lie on the plain, and people can touch them.

<!-- END -->

<!-- MORE | Events-Batch-01.md | 7. Thera erupts | 304 words -->

Today, big ships sail into a bay on the Greek island of Santorini and anchor in water hundreds of metres deep. Cliffs rise all around them, striped red, black and white.

The bay is the inside of a volcano.

About 3,600 years ago, the island, then called Thera, erupted. It threw out enough ash and rock to bury a large city many times over, and when the eruption was over, the middle of the island had fallen in, and the sea poured into the hole.

People lived on Thera. Their town, now called Akrotiri, was buried in ash, and the ash kept it: houses two and three storeys high, with pictures painted on the walls of swallows, boys fishing and blue monkeys. Archaeologists have found almost no bodies there, and few valuable things, so the people seem to have left in time. Where they went, nobody knows.

What happened to Crete?

Crete is a large island about 110 kilometres to the south, where a people now called the Minoans lived, in towns with great palaces. Ash from Thera fell on Crete, and at Palaikastro, on Crete's eastern end, geologists have found a layer of sea sand, broken pottery and shells thrown far inland by a tsunami.

In 1939, a Greek archaeologist named Spyridon Marinatos suggested that the eruption destroyed the Minoans. It is a good story, and for a long time many people believed it. But digging since then shows that the Minoan palaces carried on for several generations after the eruption. They were hurt, but not destroyed, and what finally ended them is still argued about.

Even the date is argued about. Some methods give about 1600 BCE and others give about 1500, and the argument has run for fifty years.

The ships anchor where the middle of the island used to be.

<!-- END -->

<!-- MORE | Events-Batch-01.md | 8. Oldest written song | 273 words -->

A broken clay tablet has two parts, separated by a line. Above the line are the words of a song, and below the line are names and numbers.

The names and numbers are the music.

The tablet was found in the 1950s in the ruins of Ugarit ("OO-ga-rit"), a port on the coast of what is now Syria, where about 3,400 years ago merchants spoke and wrote in many languages. This song is in Hurrian, a language that is only partly understood today. It is a song to Nikkal, a goddess of orchards.

The instructions below the line are for a lyre, a small stringed instrument, held in the arms, whose strings run from a sound box up to a crossbar. The instructions name pairs of strings, and each name is followed by a number. The names of the pairs are known from other tablets, so the tablet tells a player which strings to play, and something about how many times.

So what does it sound like?

That is the trouble. Nobody agrees.

We don't know how the lyre was tuned, or whether each pair of strings was played together or one after the other. We don't know whether the numbers mean how many times to play, or something else. We don't know how the words fit the notes, or how fast it went.

Since the 1970s, several people have worked it out, each carefully, each in a different way, and their versions sound nothing alike. Some play the notes one after another, as a tune, and others play them two at a time.

Every recording you can find is someone's best guess.

<!-- END -->

<!-- MORE | Events-Batch-01.md | 9. Rhind papyrus | 309 words -->

A scroll of papyrus, a kind of paper made from reeds, is about five metres long. It begins with a promise: it will show the right way to reckon, and with it a way into the knowledge of everything that exists.

Then come more than eighty problems, with their answers.

The scribe who copied it wrote his name at the start: Ahmes. He says he copied it from an older scroll, written more than two hundred years before. That makes it one of the oldest books of mathematics in the world, and Ahmes one of the first people we know by name to have written mathematics down.

The problems are practical: how to share loaves of bread among ten men, how much grain fits in a round store, how steep to make the side of a pyramid.

How did an Egyptian scribe multiply?

By doubling. Suppose you want 13 times 12. Write 1 and 12. Double both: 2 and 24. Double again: 4 and 48. Again: 8 and 96. Stop there, because the next double, 16, is more than 13.

Now look down the left side for numbers that add up to 13. 1 and 4 and 8 make 13. Add the numbers beside them, 12 and 48 and 96, and that makes 156, which is 13 times 12.

That is all there is to it: nothing but doubling and adding, and no table of times to learn. It works for any two whole numbers, because every whole number can be made by adding some of 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and so on.

In 1858 a Scottish lawyer named Alexander Henry Rhind bought the scroll in Luxor, which is why it carries his name instead of the scribe's.

It is in the British Museum. At the very start of the scroll, the scribe's name is still there: Ahmes.

<!-- END -->

<!-- MORE | Events-Batch-01.md | 10. Lapita voyagers | 299 words -->

A piece of broken pottery lies on a beach in Tonga. Its surface is covered with fine patterns: rows of tiny dots, pressed into the wet clay with a toothed stamp before it was fired.

Pottery with these patterns has been found on islands across more than four thousand kilometres of the Pacific.

It is called Lapita pottery, after a beach in New Caledonia where it was dug up in 1952. The people who made it lived at first on the islands near New Guinea, and then, a little more than three thousand years ago, they began to sail east.

East of the Solomon Islands, the islands get farther apart. Before then, people had crossed plenty of sea, but usually with the next island in sight or nearly so. The Lapita sailors went where no land could be seen, and kept going. From Vanuatu to Fiji is about 850 kilometres of open water.

How do you find an island you have never seen?

Nobody knows exactly how the Lapita sailors did it. Later Pacific navigators found their way by the stars, the shape of the waves, the clouds that gather over land, and birds that fly out to fish and back to land at night, and the Lapita sailors probably knew some of the same things.

They did not travel light. They brought pigs, dogs and chickens, and plants to grow: taro, yams, breadfruit. Everywhere they landed, they were the first people ever to live there.

About 2,850 years ago they reached Tonga. At a place called Nukuleka, archaeologists have found some of the oldest Lapita pottery in Tonga, dated by the coral tools buried with it.

Their descendants kept sailing, and over the next two thousand years they reached Hawaiʻi, Aotearoa (New Zealand) and Rapa Nui (Easter Island).

<!-- MORE | Events-Batch-01.md | 12. Homer written down | 280 words -->

A long poem called the Iliad tells about a few weeks near the end of a war at a city called Troy, and it is more than fifteen thousand lines long. Another, the Odyssey, tells about a soldier's ten-year journey home from that war, and it is about twelve thousand lines.

For a long time, nobody wrote them down.

The Greeks said both poems were made by a poet called Homer. Nobody knows who Homer was, when he lived, or whether he was one person. What seems likely is that poems like these were sung by travelling poets, who performed them from memory to anyone who would listen.

How could anyone remember fifteen thousand lines?

In the 1930s, an American scholar named Milman Parry went to the mountains of what was then Yugoslavia. There he met singers who could not read or write, and who sang long poems about old wars, some as long as the Odyssey. They did not memorise the words exactly. They knew thousands of ready-made phrases and scenes, and they rebuilt the poem each time they sang it. The Iliad is full of the same kind of phrases: "rosy-fingered dawn," "swift-footed Achilles."

The Greeks had begun writing with an alphabet not long before, around 800 BCE, and at some point in the next two hundred years, someone wrote the poems down. Nobody knows who, or exactly when.

That first written copy is lost. The oldest pieces we have were found in Egypt, on scraps of papyrus about four hundred years younger, and the oldest complete copy of the Iliad was made about 1,200 years after that, in the 900s.

It is in a library in Venice.

<!-- END -->

<!-- MORE | Events-Batch-01.md | 13. Kalinga war | 283 words -->

A message from a king is carved on a rock face in India. Part of it reads:

"A hundred and fifty thousand people were deported, a hundred thousand were killed and many times that number perished."

The king is talking about his own war.

His name was Ashoka ("a-SHOH-ka"), and he ruled most of India. About 2,300 years ago, eight years after he became king, he invaded Kalinga, a land on the east coast of India, and won.

Kings of that time carved their victories on stone. Ashoka carved something else. He said the killing and the suffering of the families filled him with sorrow and regret, and that from then on, the only conquest worth making was a conquest by what he called dhamma: doing right by others. He turned seriously to Buddhism, stopped making war, and had messages carved on rocks and tall stone pillars across his kingdom.

What did his messages say?

Treat servants and workers fairly. Respect your parents, and respect the beliefs of others. Don't kill animals needlessly. Plant trees along roads for shade, and dig wells. He wrote that he had set up care for sick people and sick animals.

What did the war cost? Only Ashoka's own numbers survive, because nobody else counted, and there is no way to check them. We don't know what the war cost in money or effort. We know it made him change how he ruled.

After Ashoka, his writing was forgotten, and for two thousand years nobody could read it. In 1837, a scholar named James Prinsep worked out the letters.

The rocks inside Kalinga itself carry Ashoka's messages too. On them, the passage counting the dead is left out.

<!-- END -->

<!-- MORE | Events-Batch-01.md | 14. Eratosthenes measures the Earth | 318 words -->

In the town of Syene, in the south of Egypt, there was said to be a deep well. On one day a year, at noon, the Sun shone straight down into it and lit the water at the bottom. On that day the Sun was directly overhead, and a stick standing upright cast no shadow at all.

On the same day, at noon, in Alexandria, far to the north, an upright stick did cast a shadow.

Why the difference?

Eratosthenes thought he knew. He was the chief librarian at Alexandria, in charge of the greatest library of the time, and like other Greek scholars he took it that the Earth is a ball, and that the Sun is so far away that its rays arrive side by side. If the ground were flat, the stick in Alexandria would cast no shadow either, so the shadow meant the ground between the two cities curved.

So he measured how much. The shadow in Alexandria showed that the Sun was one fiftieth of a full circle away from straight overhead, so the distance from Alexandria to Syene had to be one fiftieth of the way around the Earth.

That distance was known: about 5,000 stadia. A stadion was a Greek unit of length, about the length of a running track. Fifty times 5,000 is 250,000 stadia, and that was his size for the whole Earth.

How close did he come?

Nobody knows exactly. There were several different stadia in use, of different lengths, and nobody knows which one he meant. Depending on the answer, he was within about two percent of the true size, or about sixteen percent too big. Either way, one man with a stick and a known distance had measured the whole Earth without leaving Egypt.

His own book is lost. We know his method from a writer named Cleomedes, who described it about three hundred years later.

<!-- END -->

<!-- MORE | Events-Batch-01.md | 15. Antikythera mechanism | 296 words -->

A lump of green, crumbling bronze sits in a museum in Athens. On one side, if you look closely, you can see the teeth of a gear wheel about as wide as your hand.

Nobody expected gears from ancient Greece.

In 1900, sponge divers sheltering from a storm off the small island of Antikythera found a shipwreck on the sea floor, and over the next year they brought up bronze and marble statues, glass, pottery and coins. The ship had sunk about 2,100 years ago. The lump of bronze came up with everything else, and for months nobody paid it much attention. Then, in 1902, it split open, and an archaeologist named Valerios Stais saw the gear.

What was it?

It took most of the next century to find out. From the 1950s, a historian of science named Derek de Solla Price studied it, and in the 1970s he had it X-rayed. In 2005, a team scanned the pieces in layers, the way a hospital scans a body, and read writing on them that had been hidden for two thousand years.

It had been a wooden box about the size of a shoebox, with a handle on the side, and turning the handle turned dozens of bronze gears inside, of which about thirty survive. On the front, pointers showed where the Sun and Moon were among the stars, and a little ball, half silver and half dark, turned to show the phase of the Moon. On the back, dials showed which months would have an eclipse, and when the great athletic games were held.

Nobody knows who made it, who it was for, or where the ship was going with it.

To unwind it, you drop the cocoon into hot water, so that the gum softens and the thread comes away in one long strand. Twist several strands together and you have thread strong enough to weave.

Who first did it, and when?

The oldest silk cloth anyone has found comes from Qingtai ("ching-TIE"), in central China, from about 5,500 years ago. Babies who had died were buried there in large clay jars, and two scraps of silk cloth were found with them. The cloth is burnt black, but it is still woven cloth.

There are older traces. At Jiahu ("jyah-HOO"), also in central China, archaeologists tested soil from under the bodies in graves about 8,500 years old, and in two of the graves they found tiny traces of silk. There is no cloth left, only the stuff that silk is made of, and nobody can tell whether it came from wild silkworms or ones that people raised.

Wild silkworms still live in China. The silkworm that people raise is a different kind, bred from the wild one over thousands of years. Its moths cannot fly, and like the wild ones, they do not eat, and they live only long enough to lay eggs.

An old Chinese story says that silk was discovered by an empress named Leizu, when a cocoon fell into her cup of hot tea. It is a story.

Every silkworm moth that people raise depends on them. Not one of them can fly away.

<!-- MORE | Events-Batch-03.md | 12. A wagon on a pot | 350 words -->

A clay pot, found in southern Poland, has pictures scratched into its side. One is tiny and very simple: a box with a small circle at each corner, and a long line out in front. It is a wagon, seen from above, with four wheels and a pole to hitch an animal to.

The pot was dug up at Bronocice ("bro-no-CHEE-tseh"), near the city of Kraków, in the 1970s. It is about 5,500 years old, and its wagon is one of the oldest pictures of a wheel anywhere.

Where was the wheel invented?

Nobody knows. It seems to turn up in several places at once. Within a few hundred years of the Bronocice pot, there are signs for wagons on clay tablets from Uruk, in what is now Iraq. There are wheel ruts, two tracks side by side pressed into the ground, under an old grave mound in northern Germany. And in a marsh in Slovenia there is a real wheel.

The wheel from the marsh is made of two planks of ash wood, held together by cross-pieces. It is 72 centimetres across, about as tall as a table. Its axle, made of oak, was found too. The wheel was fixed tight to the axle, so wheel and axle turned together.

People had been making round things for thousands of years. So why did the wheel take so long?

Because the wheel is the easy part. The axle is hard. The archaeologist David Anthony points out that the axle, and the hole it turns in, have to be almost perfectly round and smooth, or they grind and stick. A thick axle rubs too much, and a thin one breaks under the load. Getting it right takes careful woodwork.

So which came first: the drawing in Poland, the signs in Iraq, or the ruts in Germany? Archaeologists still argue. Some think the idea spread fast from one place. Others think more than one people worked it out.

The wheel from the marsh is in a museum in Ljubljana. It is more than 5,000 years old, and it still has its axle.

<!-- MORE | Events-Batch-03.md | 13. The Uluburun ship | 336 words -->

In 1982 a sponge diver named Mehmed Çakır came up from the sea floor off the south coast of Turkey, and said he had seen "metal biscuits with ears."

They were slabs of copper.

Archaeologists dived on the spot for eleven summers. The wreck lay more than forty metres down, so each dive was short, and they made more than twenty-two thousand of them. The ship had sunk about 3,300 years ago, and it is called the Uluburun ship ("oo-loo-boo-ROON"), after the cape where it lies.

It was carrying about ten tonnes of copper, in flat slabs with a stubby handle at each of the four corners. Those were the ears. The ship was also carrying about one tonne of tin.

Ten to one. That is the recipe for bronze.

Copper alone is soft, and a copper blade bends and soon loses its edge. Melt about one part tin with ten parts copper and you get bronze, which is much harder, and which can be poured into a mould to make an axe, a sword, a pot or a statue. By the time this ship sailed, people had been making bronze for well over a thousand years.

So why carry the two metals separately?

Because they came from different places. Copper is found in many places, and tin in only a few. In 2022 a team of archaeologists and chemists argued that about a third of the tin came from mines in what is now Uzbekistan, thousands of kilometres to the east, and the rest from Kestel, in Turkey. Other specialists answered the next year that the evidence cannot show it. The argument is not settled.

The ship carried other things too: glass, ebony, ivory, amber from the shores of the Baltic Sea, and a small gold beetle with the name of Nefertiti, a queen of Egypt. Nobody knows for certain where it was going, or who owned it.

The copper and the tin are in a museum in Bodrum, farther west along the same coast.

<!-- MORE | Events-Batch-03.md | 14. Caral | 313 words -->

More than thirty flutes, made from the bones of pelicans and condors, were found at Caral, in Peru. They are more than 4,000 years old.

Caral ("kah-RAHL") stands in the valley of the Supe River, about twenty-three kilometres inland from the Pacific coast. Almost no rain falls there, but the river comes down from the Andes mountains, and along its banks the valley is green.

The city had six great mounds, built in steps like pyramids, with flat tops and stairways, and the biggest has a base longer than a football field. There were sunken round plazas, houses and workshops, and perhaps three thousand people lived there.

How do we know how old it is?

The builders filled the insides of their mounds with stones, carried in bags woven from reeds, and they left the bags in place. Reeds can be dated. In 2001 the archaeologist Ruth Shady, who began digging at Caral in 1994, published the dates with two colleagues, Jonathan Haas and Winifred Creamer, and the oldest bags were about 4,600 years old. Caral was being built at about the same time as the great pyramids of Egypt, on the other side of the world.

Who were the people of Caral?

They grew cotton, squash and beans, and down on the coast, other people fished for anchovies and sardines. Shady thinks the two traded: the farmers gave cotton for fishing nets, and the fishers gave dried fish.

The people of Caral made no pottery, and no writing has been found. Shady has said she found a quipu, a bundle of knotted strings like those that people in the Andes used much later to keep records. By 2026 she had not published it, so nobody else could judge.

No signs of war have been found at Caral, so far.

Someone carved the flutes and played them. Nobody knows what music they made.

<!-- MORE | Events-Batch-03.md | 15. The Great Pyramid | 330 words -->

On a sheet of papyrus, a kind of paper made from the stems of a marsh plant, someone kept a diary. Day by day, it records a crew of about forty men loading blocks of white limestone onto a boat, carrying them along the Nile, and delivering them to a building site called the Horizon of Khufu.

The man who kept the diary was named Merer ("MEH-rer"), and he was the inspector in charge of the crew. His diary was found in 2013 by the archaeologist Pierre Tallet and his team, in caves near the Red Sea, where boats had once been stored. It is the oldest papyrus with writing on it that anyone has found.

The building site was the Great Pyramid.

It was built as a tomb for Khufu ("KOO-foo"), a king of Egypt. It was made of about two million three hundred thousand blocks of stone, and it stood 146 metres high. For nearly 3,800 years, nothing people built anywhere was taller.

Who built it?

A Greek writer, Herodotus, who visited Egypt about two thousand years later, said that a hundred thousand men worked on it, and others have said they were slaves. What has been found since tells a different story.

In 1990 a tourist's horse stumbled on a buried mud-brick wall near the pyramids, and behind it was a cemetery for the workers, with hundreds of graves. Their bones show hard lives, and also care: broken arms and legs that had been set, and had healed. Nearby, archaeologists have dug up a town where pyramid workers lived, with bakeries and the bones of the cattle, sheep and goats they ate.

How many workers were there? Estimates run from about ten thousand to forty thousand, and nobody knows exactly. Most archaeologists now think they were not slaves, but people working for the king for a time, in gangs with names like "Friends of Khufu".

Pieces of Merer's diary are now in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo.
