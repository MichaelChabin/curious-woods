## 1. Recovery

**Label:** Recovery
**Kind:** period
**Span:** 66 to 56 Ma · both edges dated boundaries at points in the rock: 66.00 (El Kef, Tunisia) and 56.00 (Dababiya, near Luxor, Egypt)
**Length:** 10 million years
**Official name:** Paleocene, "the old part of the Eocene", from Greek *palaios*, old; named by Wilhelm Philipp Schimper in 1874
**Known from:** fossil leaves, pollen and bones in rock laid down by rivers and swamps, and shells in seafloor mud
**Opens to:** nothing yet
**Inside it:** events *The asteroid* (at the start edge), *Fern spike* (at the start edge), *Hot spike* (at the end edge). Plots: *Sea level*, *Oxygen*.
**Index:** the elephant line begins: *Eritherium*, about 60 million years ago in Morocco, about 5 kilograms, the size of a large cat. No horse yet.
**Pictures wanted:** a giant fossil leaf from Cerrejón (Smithsonian Tropical Research Institute or Florida Museum; licence to ask); a *Titanoboa* vertebra beside a modern anaconda's (Florida Museum; licence to ask).

**Summary (33 words)**
Forests grew back, and a warm, wet world filled up again. Mammals were growing, but in the tropics the giants were still reptiles: crocodiles, turtles, and a snake as long as a bus. *More*

**More (338 words)**

```
From about 66 to 56 million years ago.
```

In La Guajira, in the far north of Colombia, the homeland of the Wayuu people, there is a coal mine, one of the biggest in the world. The coal was once a swamp. In the rock between the seams of coal lie thousands of fossil leaves, many of them big, from a forest that grew there about 58 million years ago. They are the oldest rainforest leaves found in the tropics of the Americas. The trees belong to families that still grow in rainforests today: palms, beans, the avocado's family, the banana's.

That forest was hot and wet, hotter than the same place is now, with more than two and a half metres of rain a year. In its rivers lived crocodile cousins, big turtles, and a snake about thirteen metres long, called *Titanoboa* ("TY-tan-o-BO-a"), the biggest snake known. No mammal has been reported there.

That is what is new: forests and their animals, built again after the asteroid (*The asteroid*). Just after the impact, ferns had covered the ground (*Fern spike*). By about 58 million years ago, as Cerrejón shows, there was rainforest near the equator again.

Mammals were growing, though not yet big. At Corral Bluffs, in Colorado, the biggest weighed about half a kilogram just after the impact, and about fifty kilograms, the size of a wolf, seven hundred thousand years later. By the end of this stretch a few plant-eaters were as heavy as a cow. In Morocco, about 60 million years ago, lived *Eritherium*, the size of a large cat, the oldest animal known of the elephant line.

The world was warm all over, with no ice sheets at the poles.

Ten million years is about a seventh of the Mammals line. It is nearly twice as long as the whole Ice line.

Geologists call this stretch the Paleocene ("PAY-lee-o-seen"). Wilhelm Schimper, who studied fossil plants, gave it the name in 1874. It means "the old part of the Eocene", the stretch that comes next.

It ended in a sudden heat (*Hot spike*).

- Jason Head and others, "Giant boid snake from the Palaeocene neotropics reveals hotter past equatorial temperatures," *Nature*, 2009. *Titanoboa*.
- Emmanuel Gheerbrant, "Paleocene emergence of elephant relatives and the rapid radiation of African ungulates," *PNAS*, 2009. *Eritherium*.
- Stephen Brusatte, *The Rise and Reign of the Mammals* (Mariner Books, 2022). For a parent.

## 2. Heat

**Label:** Heat
**Kind:** period
**Span:** 56 to 34 Ma · both edges dated boundaries at points in the rock: 56.00 (Dababiya, near Luxor, Egypt) and 33.9 (Massignano, near Ancona, Italy)
**Length:** 22 million years
**Official name:** Eocene, "dawn of the recent", from Greek *eos*, dawn, and *kainos*, new; published by Charles Lyell in 1833, from a name William Whewell suggested
**Known from:** fossils of plants and animals far north of where their kind lives now, and the chemistry of shells in seafloor mud
**Opens to:** nothing yet
**Inside it:** events *Hot spike* (at the start edge), *Antarctica freezes* (at the end edge). Plots: *Sea level*, *Oxygen*.
**Index:** the horse begins: *Sifrhippus*, at the very start, in the Bighorn Basin of Wyoming, about 5.5 kilograms, the size of a cat, shrinking to about 4 during the *Hot spike*. The elephant line: *Moeritherium*, near the end, in the Fayum of Egypt, about 235 kilograms, the size of a big pig.
**Pictures wanted:** the fossil forest stumps of Axel Heiberg Island, or the Strathcona Fiord beds on Ellesmere (Geological Survey of Canada or Carnegie Museum; licence to ask); a living dawn redwood (Commons; licence to check).

**Summary (35 words)**
The warmest world since the dinosaurs died. For most of it there were no ice sheets anywhere. Forests grew in the far north of Canada, with alligators, giant tortoises and tapirs living among the trees. *More*

**More (320 words)**

```
From about 56 to 34 million years ago.
```

On Ellesmere Island, in Nunavut, in the far north of Canada, the ground is bare rock and snow for most of the year, and winter is months of darkness at about forty below. In that rock lie the stumps and leaves of a swamp forest that grew there about 52 million years ago. Most of its trees were dawn redwoods, a kind that still grows wild in China. Among their roots lived alligators, giant tortoises, monitor lizards and tapirs.

The island stood almost as far north then as it does now. Its winters were still dark, for about four months at a time. But they were mild, near freezing, and the summers were warm.

That is what is new: heat. The stretch began with a sudden jump in warmth (*Hot spike*). About 50 million years ago the Earth stayed hotter, for longer, than at any time since the dinosaurs died. The average temperature of the whole surface was perhaps 27 degrees; today it is about 15. For most of this stretch there were no ice sheets anywhere.

Mammals filled the forests. In Wyoming, at the very start of the stretch, lived *Sifrhippus*, one of the oldest horses known, about the size of a cat. In Egypt, near the end, lived *Moeritherium* ("meer-ee-THEER-ee-um"), an animal of the elephant line, the size of a big pig, with a long body and short legs.

After about 50 million years ago, the Earth slowly cooled.

Twenty-two million years is a third of the Mammals line. It is about four times as long as the Ice line.

Geologists call this stretch the Eocene ("EE-o-seen"), Greek for "dawn of the recent". Charles Lyell, a Scottish geologist, published the name in 1833. He sorted rocks by how many of their fossil shells belong to kinds still alive. In rocks of this age, only a few do.

It ended when ice began to stay on Antarctica (*Antarctica freezes*).

**References**

- Gordon Inglis and others, "Global mean surface temperature and climate sensitivity of the early Eocene Climatic Optimum (EECO), Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum (PETM), and latest Paleocene," *Climate of the Past*, 2020. How warm.
- Ross Secord and others, "Evolution of the earliest horses driven by climate change in the Paleocene-Eocene Thermal Maximum," *Science*, 2012. *Sifrhippus*.
- Stephen Brusatte, *The Rise and Reign of the Mammals* (Mariner Books, 2022). For a parent.

**Notes for us.** The scene is Ellesmere because it shows heat by where it is: crocodile relatives near the pole. The More says "about four months" of dark, not the CU Boulder release's "six", which is wrong at about 77 to 78° N (the Axel Heiberg release says four). "No ice sheets", not "no ice": short-lived Antarctic glaciers in the Eocene are argued. Research pass: Eberle and Greenwood 2012, Strathcona Fiord and Bay Fiord, about 52 to 53 Ma (late Wasatchian), *Metasequoia*, *Glyptostrobus*, alder, birch, walnut, ginkgo; *Allognathosuchus* (an alligator), the giant tortoise *Hadrianus*, monitor lizards, a boa, the tapir *Thuliadanta*, *Coryphodon*, primates; winters near freezing (0 to 4 °C), summers 20 °C or more; mean annual 10 to 19 °C by different methods; palaeolatitude about 77° N; today's winter about −38 °C (CU Boulder 2010). Inglis 2020, EECO global mean about 27.0 °C (23.2 to 29.7); today about 15 (preindustrial about 14). Secord 2012, *Sifrhippus sandrae* at Cabin Fork, about 5.4 kg shrinking about 30 percent to about 3.9 kg, then up to about 7 (the paper's own 5.6 to confirm); Cabin Fork is where *Hot spike* stands, so the horse is told here in the period's own clause and the event keeps "Some animals grew smaller". *Moeritherium lyonsi*, Qasr el Sagha Formation, about 37.2 to 33.9 Ma, about 235 kg (Larramendi 2016). Eocene: Lyell, *Principles of Geology*, vol. 3, 1833; Whewell's coinage (letter of 1831); Zittel's figures, about 3 percent of species living for the Eocene. Lengths: 22.1 of 66 is 33 percent; against 5.3, 4.2. Agreement: *Hot spike* (start; it causes the edge, so the period opens on its aftermath in a clause); *Antarctica freezes* 33.9 (end, in a clause). For the checkers: "about forty degrees below freezing" (−38 °C, so "about forty below"); "dawn redwoods, a kind that still grows wild in China"; "monitor lizards and tapirs"; "almost as far north then as it does now"; "warmer than at any time since the dinosaurs died" (the EECO against the later Cenozoic: true; against the PETM peak, which was warmer and is inside this stretch: the sentence says "about 50 million years ago", and the PETM was hotter still for a short time; consider "warmer, for longer, than at any time since"); "meer-ee-THEER-ee-um"; "EE-o-seen"; "Scottish geologist".

Confirmed: Lyell 1833 with Whewell, *eos*; 33.9 unchanged; Secord 2012, Cabin Fork, about 5.4 shrinking about 30 percent to 3.9; *Moeritherium lyonsi* about 235 kg; Ellesmere's −38 °C winters; dawn redwood wild only in China; alligators, giant tortoises, monitor lizards, tapirs; almost the same latitude; about four months of dark; winters near freezing; 27 and 15; Lyell Scottish, about 3.5 percent of Eocene shells living; the lengths; all references.

## 3. Cooling

**Label:** Cooling
**Kind:** period
**Span:** 34 to 23 Ma · both edges dated boundaries at points in the rock: 33.9 (Massignano, near Ancona, Italy) and 23.04 (Lemme-Carrosio, Piedmont, Italy); the chart moved the second from 23.03 to 23.04 in v2024/12
**Length:** 11 million years
**Official name:** Oligocene, "few of the recent", from Greek *oligos*, few, and *kainos*, new; named by Heinrich Ernst Beyrich in 1854
**Known from:** bones in old river mud and ash on the Great Plains, and the chemistry of shells in seafloor mud, which records the ice
**Opens to:** nothing yet
**Inside it:** events *Antarctica freezes* (at the start edge). Plots: *Sea level* (the fall at about 34 million years), *Oxygen*.
**Index:** the horse: *Mesohippus*, with three toes, about 60 centimetres at the shoulder, the size of a greyhound. The elephant line: *Phiomia*, in the Fayum of Egypt, about 1,200 kilograms.
**Pictures wanted:** the striped walls of the Badlands, South Dakota (National Park Service, public domain); a *Mesohippus* skeleton (Commons or the South Dakota School of Mines museum; licence to check).

**Summary (32 words)**
Ice settled on Antarctica and stayed. The world grew cooler and drier, the sea fell, and in many places thick forest thinned to open woodland. In it lived horses with three toes. *More*

**More (306 words)**

```
From about 34 to 23 million years ago.
```

In South Dakota there is a country of steep, striped hills and gullies that the Lakota call *mako sica*, "bad lands". The stripes are layers of mud and ash laid down by rivers. The lower layers, a little older than this stretch, hold the bones of alligators and of big animals that lived in wet forest. The layers just above them, from about 34 to 30 million years ago, hold the bones of animals of open woodland: small horses called *Mesohippus* ("mez-o-HIP-us"), with three toes on each foot and about as tall as a greyhound, and plant-eaters called oreodonts, as common there as zebras are on the Serengeti today.

That change in the rock is the change in the world. It began when an ice sheet grew across East Antarctica, over a few hundred thousand years, and the sea fell by about fifty-five to seventy metres (*Antarctica freezes*). There has been ice on Antarctica ever since.

That is what is new: a cold pole. As ice grew at one end of the Earth, the whole Earth grew cooler, and in many places drier.

Some animals were enormous. In Asia lived *Paraceratherium* ("para-sera-THEER-ee-um"), a hornless cousin of the rhinoceros, nearly five metres tall at the shoulder, one of the biggest land mammals ever found. In Egypt, animals of the elephant line had grown heavier than a cow.

Eleven million years is a sixth of the Mammals line. It is about twice as long as the Ice line.

Geologists call this stretch the Oligocene ("OL-i-go-seen"), Greek for "few of the recent". Its rocks hold a few more shells of kinds still alive than older rocks do. Heinrich Ernst Beyrich, a German geologist, gave it the name in 1854.

In the Badlands, rain wears the hills back a little every year, and new bones come out of the layers.

**References**
- Marcelo de Lira Mota and others, "Multi-proxy evidence for sea level fall at the onset of the Eocene-Oligocene transition," *Nature Communications*, 2023. The ice and the sea.
- Felisa Smith and others, "The evolution of maximum body size of terrestrial mammals," *Science*, 2010. *Paraceratherium* and the growth of mammals.
- National Park Service, "Geologic Formations: How Badlands Buttes Came to Be," Badlands National Park. The layers.
- Stephen Brusatte, *The Rise and Reign of the Mammals* (Mariner Books, 2022). For a parent.

**Notes for us.** The scene is the Badlands because the change of the stretch is visible in one wall: the Chadron Formation below (about 37 to 34 Ma, wet, "like the Everglades", alligators and brontotheres) and the Brule above (about 34 to 30, cooler and drier, wooded floodplains giving way to savanna; NPS). Oligocene: Beyrich 1854, from shell beds in Belgium and Germany; "few recent" (paleogene.org). Lakota *mako sica* (NPS). Lengths: 10.86 of 66 is 16 percent, "a sixth"; against 5.3, 2.05. Agreement: *Antarctica freezes*: "over a few hundred thousand years, an ice sheet grew across East Antarctica, the sea fell by about seventy metres… There has been ice there ever since", copied; *Sea level* plot ("About 34 million years ago ice began to stay on Antarctica, and the sea fell"). The ending is the erosion, a fact (the Badlands erode about an inch a year: NPS, to confirm). For the checkers: "a country of steep, striped hills"; "bones of alligators… in the lower layers"; "herds of… oreodonts" (herds are inferred: soften to "plant-eaters called oreodonts" if unconfirmed); "nearly five metres tall"; "one of the biggest land mammals ever found"; "heavier than a cow"; Beyrich "German geologist"; "OL-i-go-seen", "mez-o-HIP-us", "para-sera-THEER-ee-um"; the Badlands ending.

## 4. Grass

**Label:** Grass
**Kind:** period
**Span:** 23 to 5.3 Ma · both edges dated boundaries at points in the rock: 23.04 (Lemme-Carrosio, Piedmont, Italy) and 5.333 (Eraclea Minoa, Sicily)
**Length:** 18 million years (17.7)
**Official name:** Miocene, "less recent", from Greek *meion*, less, and *kainos*, new; published by Charles Lyell in 1833
**Known from:** bones and grass husks in volcanic ash and river sand, tiny pieces of glass that grasses leave in old soils, and the teeth of animals that ate grass
**Opens to:** nothing yet
**Inside it:** events *Grasslands*, *Hot-country grasses*, *Walking upright*. Plots: *Sea level*, *Oxygen*.
**Index:** the horse: at Ashfall, five kinds of horse, some with three toes and some with one, with tall-crowned teeth for grass. The elephant line: giants, among them *Deinotherium*, up to about 4 metres at the shoulder and perhaps 10 tonnes.
**Pictures wanted:** the rhino skeletons in the Hubbard Rhino Barn at Ashfall Fossil Beds (University of Nebraska State Museum; licence to ask); a *Deinotherium* skull with its downturned tusks (Commons; licence to check).

**Summary (35 words)**
Grass spread over open country, in some places and then in more. Horses grew taller teeth to grind it. Late in the stretch, grasses that like heat and sun covered much of the warm world. *More*

**More (348 words)**

```
From about 23 to 5.3 million years ago.
```

In Nebraska, near the village of Royal, there is a barn built over a hole in the ground. In the hole lie the skeletons of rhinos, horses and camels, whole, as they died about 12 million years ago at a waterhole. A volcano more than a thousand kilometres away, in what is now Idaho, had erupted, and the wind carried its ash across the plains. The ash drifted into the waterhole until it lay two or three metres deep. The animals breathed it in and died over days and weeks: the birds first, then the horses and camels, and the rhinos last, after about a month.

Some of the rhinos have grass seeds in their mouths and among their ribs, from what they ate last.

That is what is new: grass, enough of it to cover open country. The oldest grasslands yet found in North America are about 22 million years old (*Grasslands*). Grass, and the grit on it, is hard on teeth, and over this stretch many grass-eaters grew teeth with tall crowns, which take longer to wear down. At the waterhole lived five kinds of horse, some with three toes and some with one.

The world was warm at first, warmest about 16 million years ago, and then it cooled. Late in the stretch, grasses that grow best in hot, bright, dry places spread over much of the warmer part of the world (*Hot-country grasses*). In what is now the desert of northern Chad, about 7 million years ago, lived an ape that may have walked upright (*Walking upright*).

The elephant line had giants. *Deinotherium* ("dine-o-THEER-ee-um"), with tusks that curved down from its lower jaw, stood up to about four metres at the shoulder.

Nearly eighteen million years is about a quarter of the Mammals line.

Geologists call this stretch the Miocene ("MY-o-seen"), Greek for "less recent". Charles Lyell published the name in 1833. Fewer of the fossil shells in its rocks are of kinds still alive than in the rocks above it.

Under the barn, some of the rhino mothers still carry their unborn young.

**References**
- Michael Voorhies and Joseph Thomasson, "Fossil grass anthoecia within Miocene rhinoceros skeletons: diet in an extinct species," *Science*, 1979. The husks.
- Jon Smith and others, "First U-Pb zircon ages for late Miocene Ashfall Konservat-Lagerstätte and Grove Lake ashes from eastern Great Plains, USA," *PLOS ONE*, 2018. The date and the volcano.
- Caroline Strömberg, "Evolution of grasses and grassland ecosystems," *Annual Review of Earth and Planetary Sciences*, 2011. Grass, continent by continent.
- Stephen Brusatte, *The Rise and Reign of the Mammals* (Mariner Books, 2022). For a parent.

In the north-west of Greenland, at a camp called NEEM, a team from fourteen countries drilled down through the ice sheet. In July 2010 the drill reached the rock below, about two and a half kilometres down. Near the bottom the ice was folded, like a rug pushed against a wall, and the team had to put its layers back in order. ## Lucy

**Label:** Lucy
**Age:** 3.18 Ma · a maximum: she is a little younger · argon–argon on crystals in volcanic ash below her: the Kada Hadar Tuff, 3.18 ± 0.01 Ma, about a metre below the skeleton, so she is a little younger than that; an ash about 18 metres below is about 3.22
**Tail:** none — one skeleton, not a first; her kind is older, back to about 3.9 Ma
**Known from:** about two fifths of one skeleton, in a gully at Hadar
**Place now:** Hadar, Afar Region, Ethiopia (about 11.17 N, 40.63 E; the find spot, AL 288, to confirm); the bones in the National Museum of Ethiopia, Addis Ababa
**Kind:** object (a fossil that is the thing itself)
**Replaces:** none; new

**Summary (37 words)**
About 3.2 million years ago. A small adult of a kind that walked upright died near a river in what is now the Afar Region of Ethiopia. About two fifths of her skeleton was found in 1974. *More*

**More (338 words)**

```
About 3.2 million years ago.
```

On 24 November 1974, at Hadar, in the Afar Region of Ethiopia, Donald Johanson and Tom Gray had spent the morning looking for fossils in the heat. On the way back to their car, Johanson saw a piece of arm bone lying in a gully. Near it lay a piece of skull, and more: a thigh bone, ribs, part of a hip. Over the next weeks the team gathered several hundred pieces of bone. Fitted together, they made about two fifths of one skeleton.

That night the camp played a Beatles song, "Lucy in the Sky with Diamonds", and the skeleton was called Lucy. In Ethiopia she is also called Dinkinesh, Amharic for "you are marvellous".

Lucy was a grown-up, a little over a metre tall. Her brain was about the size of a chimpanzee's. Her hip and her thigh bones show that she walked upright on two legs. Her arms were long and strong, and her fingers were curved. Whether her kind still spent much time in trees is argued.

How old is she? Volcanic ash lies in layers in the rock at Hadar, and ash holds crystals that keep time: some of the argon in them was made, slowly and steadily, after the ash fell. The ash about a metre below Lucy is about 3.18 million years old. So she is a little younger than that.

She is not the oldest of her kind. Bones of the same kind, *Australopithecus afarensis* ("os-TRAY-lo-PITH-e-cus af-ar-EN-sis"), run from about 3.9 to 2.9 million years ago. The next year, at Hadar, the team found the bones of at least seventeen more, now called the First Family.

How Lucy died is argued. In 2016 John Kappelman and others said that her broken bones look like the bones of someone who fell from a tall tree. Others think the bones broke after she was buried, as the weight of the ground pressed on them.

Her bones are kept in the National Museum of Ethiopia, in Addis Ababa. Visitors there see a copy.
