# Tyrannosaurus rex: five finds

Tyrannosaurus rex is found only in the last formations of the Cretaceous in western North America (Hell Creek, Lance, Frenchman, Scollard, Willow Creek and their equivalents). The top of its range is the K–Pg boundary, dated by Ar/Ar on sanidine from the boundary coal in Garfield County, Montana, at about 66.0 million years ago (66.043 ± 0.043 Ma, Renne et al. 2013; 66.052 ± 0.008/0.043 Ma, Sprain et al. 2018). The bottom is less sure: the base of the Hell Creek Formation is put at no older than about 68 Ma by magnetostratigraphy (Fowler 2017), and at about 67.1 Ma or older from the first U–Pb dates in the lower Hell Creek (Roberts et al. 2026). That gives the range of about 68 to 66 Ma.

A note on the numbers. Ar/Ar dates are given as "± a / b": a is the uncertainty of the measurement alone, b also counts the uncertainty in the standards and decay constants. Fowler (2017) recalibrated older Ar/Ar dates to a different standard, which makes them about 0.2 Myr younger (on his chart the boundary coal date becomes 65.836 Ma). The two sets of numbers should not be mixed.

Three facts carry most of the ages below:
- **The K–Pg boundary (IrZ coal), Hell Creek region, Montana:** 66.052 ± 0.008/0.043 Ma (Ar/Ar on sanidine, pooled; Sprain et al. 2018). Renne et al. (2013) dated the same coal at Hauso Flats, Garfield County, at 66.043 ± 0.011/0.043 Ma. A U–Pb date for the boundary (Clyde et al., as cited by Roberts et al. 2026) is 66.021 ± 0.024 Ma.
- **The Null coal, uppermost middle Hell Creek, about 30 m below the boundary:** 66.289 ± 0.051 Ma at Bug Creek, McCone County (Ar/Ar, Sprain et al. 2015); 66.347 ± 0.151/0.159 Ma at Thomas Ranch (Sprain et al. 2018). The magnetic reversal C30n/C29r lies about 2 m below the Null coal and is dated at 66.311 ± 0.051/0.067 Ma (Sprain et al. 2018). The upper third of the Hell Creek therefore covers roughly the last 0.25–0.3 Myr of the Cretaceous.
- **The lower Hell Creek, Garfield County:** two ash beds dated by U–Pb on zircon (CA-ID-TIMS) at 66.929 ± 0.020 Ma and 66.850 ± 0.026 Ma, southwest of Jordan, Montana (Roberts et al. 2026). The authors call these "the first absolute ages for the lower portion of the Hell Creek Formation anywhere." Sprain et al. (2018) estimate the whole formation lasted about 1.8 Myr; Roberts et al. (2026) estimate about 1.08 Myr in their study area.

## Sue (FMNH PR 2081)

**Found:** Sue Hendrickson of the Black Hills Institute (BHI) found it on 12 August 1990. BHI (led by Peter Larson) dug it out that summer.

**Where:** On the ranch of Maurice Williams, Cheyenne River Indian Reservation, north of Faith, South Dakota. County: not confirmed. PBDB says "county unclear"; Paleofile gives Meade County. Hell Creek Formation, in a sandstone channel fill. The site has no published measured height in the formation that I could find.

**Now:** The Field Museum of Natural History, Chicago, in its own room in the Griffin Halls of Evolving Planet (moved there in 2018). The skeleton on display is real bone. The skull on the skeleton is a cast. The real skull is displayed in a freestanding case nearby, where visiting scientists can study it.

**Age:** The Field Museum says "about 67 million years ago." No date from the site itself.

**How the age is known:** No rock at or near the Sue quarry has been dated. Its age rests only on its being in the Hell Creek Formation. Fowler (2017) notes that the South Dakota Hell Creek lacks good dating. It is bounded only by the ammonite *Jeletzkytes nebrascensis* in the Fox Hills Formation beneath it (the *J. nebrascensis* zone begins about 68.2 Ma). Fowler copied the Montana ages across to South Dakota. Those Montana ages come from Ar/Ar dates on ashes in Garfield and McCone Counties (Sprain et al. 2015, 2018) and from magnetic reversals. Paul, Persons & Van Raalte (2022) placed Sue in the "lower" part of the Hell Creek and Lance ("lower, lower middle and possibly middle"). They made Sue the type of their *T. imperator*, now treated as a synonym of *T. rex*. Carr et al. (2022) replied that this framework splits the formation into thirds without a measured position for each specimen and without independent age control. Sources therefore disagree on whether Sue is in the lower or a higher part of the formation, and nothing ties it to a date closer than "Hell Creek, roughly 67 to 66 Ma."

**Word:** worked out.

**PBDB:** occurrence 1210698, collection 157189 ("Sue" site (PROXY)). Listed as "Tyrannosaurus imperator n. sp." (Paul et al. 2022), accepted name *Tyrannosaurus rex*. PBDB notes "FMNH PR 2081 (= BHI 2033)."

## The holotype (CM 9380)

**Found:** Barnum Brown of the American Museum of Natural History, 1902 (collected 1902 and 1905). It was first catalogued as AMNH 973. Henry Fairfield Osborn named *Tyrannosaurus rex* from it in 1905.

**Where:** Quarry No. 1 on the old Max Sieber Ranch, near Sheba Mountain, about 16 miles northwest of Jordan, Montana. Then Dawson County, now Garfield County. This is the Hell Creek type area. PBDB still lists the formation as "Lance", the older name. Brown recorded the bed as "220 feet above Ft. Pierre," in "flinty blue sandstone nearly as hard as granite" (quoted in PBDB).

**Now:** Carnegie Museum of Natural History, Pittsburgh (bought from the AMNH in 1941). The mounted skeleton contains the real bones of the back, shoulder, hip and legs. The head on the mount is a reproduction. Only eight of the skull's roughly 65 bones were found. The museum built a complete cast skull from casts of those eight and casts of skull bones from Museum of the Rockies specimens. The 2025 Carnegie Magazine says the real skull bones are kept in storage, in the "Big Bone Room" or "Little Bone Room." Some secondary sources say the real skull is shown in a case nearby; the museum's own 2025 text says storage.

**Age:** The Carnegie Museum says 66 million years old.

**How the age is known:** No date was obtained at Brown's quarry. The age rests on the quarry's being in the Hell Creek Formation of Garfield County. That formation is dated at its top by Ar/Ar on sanidine from the boundary coal, 66.052 ± 0.008/0.043 Ma (Sprain et al. 2018). Within it, the Null coal (about 66.3 Ma) and the C30n/C29r reversal mark the base of the upper third. Brown's "220 feet above the Pierre" has not been converted, in anything I found, into a height within the Hell Creek as now defined. Brown counted the Colgate Sandstone in his Hell Creek; it is now placed in the Fox Hills, per Fowler 2017. Paul et al. (2022) placed CM 9380 in their "upper" zone of the formation. That placement is disputed in method (Carr et al. 2022).

**Word:** worked out.

**PBDB:** occurrence 487593, collection 49005 ("Quarry No. 1, Sieber Ranch (AMNH)"). Notes "CM 9380 = AMNH 973."

## Stan (BHI 3033)

**Found:** Stan Sacrison, an amateur fossil hunter, found the first bones in 1987. He first took them for *Triceratops*. The Black Hills Institute dug out the skeleton in 1992.

**Where:** Private land about 8 km northwest of Buffalo, Harding County, South Dakota (McKenzie Butte map sheet). Hell Creek Formation, in fine channel-fill sandstone. PBDB gives the site as Kirk Johnson's plant locality KJ9239 (Denver Museum locality 517), in plant zone HCIII of Johnson's Marmarth composite section. PBDB records it as 16.2 m below the Hell Creek–Fort Union contact, which is taken as the K–Pg boundary "based on calculations in Johnson (2002)."

**Now:** Sold at Christie's, New York, on 6 October 2020 for $31.8 million including fees, after a settlement among the BHI owners. It is now at the Natural History Museum Abu Dhabi, which opened on 22 November 2025, catalogued NHMAD 2020.00001. Stan is shown there in a scene of two T. rex fighting. Whether the displayed skeleton is the real bone or a cast: not confirmed. The museum's descriptions call it "70 per cent complete" and "67 million years old" but do not say. About 30 casts of Stan have been sold to other museums (Wikipedia, Specimens of Tyrannosaurus).

**Age:** The Abu Dhabi museum says 67 million years old. The site's position, 16.2 m below the boundary, points to a younger age, near the end of the Cretaceous; no date has been published for the site itself.

**How the age is known:** No rock at the Stan site has been dated. Its age is reasoned from its position in a measured section: 16.2 m below the K–Pg boundary, placed by plant zones (Johnson 2002). The boundary itself is dated elsewhere by Ar/Ar (in southwestern North Dakota by Hicks et al. 2002, and in Montana by Renne et al. 2013 and Sprain et al. 2018, 66.052 Ma). In southwestern North Dakota, Hicks et al. (2002) extrapolate the C30n/C29r reversal to about 26 m below the boundary (as summarised by Fowler 2017). My own inference, not a published statement: if that spacing holds at the Stan site, Stan lies inside chron C29r, which would make it younger than the C30n/C29r reversal (66.311 Ma in Montana; Sprain et al. 2018). Paul et al. (2022) placed Stan in the "upper" part of the formation, as a *T. regina*, now treated as a synonym of *T. rex*.

**Word:** worked out.

**PBDB:** occurrence 566954, collection 48193 ("KJ9239 (DMNH 517)", aka "Stan T-rex site"). Listed as "Tyrannosaurus regina" (Paul et al. 2022), accepted name *Tyrannosaurus rex*.

## The Wankel rex, "the Nation's T. rex" (MOR 555)

**Found:** Kathy Wankel, a rancher, found it in 1988 (Labor Day weekend) while hiking with her family. The Museum of the Rockies, led by Jack Horner, excavated it with the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers (PBDB: 1988–1991). Wikipedia's table gives 1990 for the excavation.

**Where:** An island on the south side of Fort Peck Reservoir, inside the Charles M. Russell National Wildlife Refuge, McCone County, Montana. This is Army Corps of Engineers land, so the skeleton is federally owned. Museum of the Rockies locality HC-069. Hell Creek Formation, in "a low grade, shallow (<2 m deep) sandy meandering channel" (PBDB).

**Now:** National Museum of Natural History (Smithsonian), Washington, D.C., on a 50-year loan from the Army Corps of Engineers since 2014. It is catalogued there as USNM 555000. It stands in the David H. Koch Hall of Fossils – Deep Time (opened 8 June 2019), which was the first time the real skeleton was assembled into a standing mount. The skull on the mount is a cast; Smithsonian Magazine coverage says the real skull was too fragile to exhibit. I could not open the Smithsonian page itself, so the exact wording is not confirmed. The Museum of the Rockies, Bozeman, shows a cast.

**Age:** No date has been given for the specimen itself. By its position it falls in roughly the last 0.3 Myr of the Cretaceous, about 66.3 to 66.05 Ma.

**How the age is known:** Horner, Goodwin & Myhrvold (2011) list MOR 555 among skeletons from the upper third (their unit "U3") of the Hell Creek in their study area, "with documented stratigraphic and locality information." In the same McCone and Garfield County sections, the upper third begins at the Apex Sandstone. That is just above the Null coal, dated by Ar/Ar on sanidine at 66.289 ± 0.051 Ma at Bug Creek, McCone County (Sprain et al. 2015), and about 2 m above the C30n/C29r reversal (66.311 Ma). The upper third ends at the boundary coal, 66.052 Ma (Sprain et al. 2018). So the age is reasoned from the specimen's position in the formation, between dated ashes measured in nearby sections. No ash at the Wankel quarry itself has been reported as dated. Paul et al. (2022) also put it high in the formation; it is the type of their *T. regina*, now a synonym of *T. rex*.

**Word:** worked out.

**PBDB:** occurrence 1210694, collection 157187 ("MOR HC-069, Wankel T-Rex"). Listed as "Tyrannosaurus regina n. sp." (Paul et al. 2022), accepted name *Tyrannosaurus rex*. PBDB notes "MOR 555/USNM 555000."

## Scotty (RSM P2523.8)

**Found:** Robert Gebhardt, a high-school principal from Eastend, on 16 August 1991. He was prospecting with Royal Saskatchewan Museum palaeontologists Tim Tokaryk and John Storer. Full excavation began in June 1994, and collecting ran until 2003 (PBDB).

**Where:** Chambery Coulee, on the north side of the Frenchman River valley, southeast of Eastend, Saskatchewan (RSM locality 72F07-0022). Frenchman Formation, in "iron-stained, heavily-cemented sandstone." The deposit was a low-energy point bar of a meandering river, rich in plant remains (PBDB, from Tokaryk & Bryant 2004).

**Now:** Royal Saskatchewan Museum. A full-size cast has stood in the CN T.rex Gallery in Regina since 17 May 2019. The real bones are kept at the museum's T.rex Discovery Centre in Eastend, which also shows a cast (since 2013). The curator said the real bones are too heavy and fragile to mount. Which real bones, if any, are on public view at Eastend: not confirmed.

**Age:** Persons et al. (2019) and press coverage say about 66 million years old (one wiki source says about 68; I did not rely on it).

**How the age is known:** No date from the Scotty site was found. The Frenchman Formation's age is reasoned from three things. First, magnetostratigraphy: almost all of the formation is reversed, in chron C29r, the last chron of the Cretaceous; its lowest beds may reach the top of C30n (Lerbekmo 1999; Lerbekmo & Braman 2002). Second, pollen: the Frenchman lies within the late Maastrichtian *Wodehouseia spinata* pollen zone. Third, in southwestern Saskatchewan the K–Pg boundary lies at the Frenchman's contact with the overlying Ravenscrag Formation, at or near the base of the Ferris coal (Fowler 2017, S2 Text). The Frenchman is therefore the same age as the upper part of the Hell Creek. The reversal at the base of C29r is dated in Montana at 66.311 Ma and the boundary at 66.052 Ma (Sprain et al. 2018). Eberth & Kamo's U–Pb date on the Battle Bentonite, in the Battle Formation beneath it, 66.936 ± 0.047 Ma (as cited by Roberts et al. 2026), gives an older limit. How far the Scotty bed lies below the boundary: not confirmed.

**Word:** worked out.

**PBDB:** occurrence 529533, collection 54105 ("Eastend 'Scotty' site, RSM loc. 72F07-0022", aka Chambery Coulee). Listed as *Tyrannosaurus rex* (Tokaryk & Bryant 2004).

## Two more, a sentence each

**Trix (RGM 792.000; PBDB occurrence 2069689, collection 279858).** Found on 27 May 2013 by Blaine and Michele Lunstad, amateur fossil hunters, in the "East Pasture" of the Murray Ranch, about 50 km southwest of Jordan (Kaskes 2016), Garfield County, Montana, in the Hell Creek Formation. It was excavated in August–September 2013 by Naturalis with the Black Hills Institute. It is now mounted at Naturalis Biodiversity Center, Leiden, the Netherlands: mostly real bone, with missing parts filled by casts and 3D prints. Its age is **worked out**, and it is the best-placed specimen here. Kaskes et al. (2016) placed the site in chron C30n, about 10 m below the first appearance of the pollen *Aquilapollenites collaris*, giving roughly 66.7 to 67.2 Ma. Roberts et al. (2026) matched it, by pollen and stratigraphy, to the level of the "Dueling Dinosaurs" quarry about 8 km away. Two ash beds there bracket the quarry and are dated by U–Pb on zircon at 66.929 ± 0.020 Ma and 66.850 ± 0.026 Ma, with a model age for the fossil bed of 66.897 +0.023/−0.028 Ma. Roberts et al. "conclude that both specimens are roughly the same age," but note they "cannot demonstrate" that the two sites are in the same sandstone body. The date is from rock 8 km from Trix, not at its site, so it is not "measured" by this file's rule. Sources: Roberts, E. M., et al. 2026, PLoS ONE 21(5): e0328861, https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0328861 (preprint https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.07.10.664044); Kaskes, P., et al. 2016, JVP Program and Abstracts (as cited by Roberts et al.); Kaskes, P. 2016, Grondboor & Hamer 2016(1), https://natuurtijdschriften.nl/pub/1020065/G-H2016nr1_art413-Kaskes-T-rex.pdf; PBDB occurrence 2069689; Wikipedia, "Trix (dinosaur)."

**Black Beauty (TMP 1981.006.0001; PBDB occurrence 1210695, collection 157188).** Found in 1980 by Jeff Baker, a high-school student fishing on the Crowsnest River near Crowsnest Pass, Alberta. It was excavated 1981–1982 with the Royal Tyrrell Museum (Philip Currie), at "T. rex Coulee" near Cowley, in the lower member of the Willow Creek Formation. It is held by the Royal Tyrrell Museum of Palaeontology, Drumheller; whether the real bone is now on display there: not confirmed. Its age is **worked out**. The base of the Willow Creek Formation is the Kneehills Tuff, dated elsewhere at 66.97 Ma (Fowler's recalibration of Hicks et al. 2003), and PBDB puts the site about 1.87 km west of a K–Pg boundary locality. No date from the site itself was found. Sources: PBDB occurrence 1210695; Fowler 2017, S2 Text, entry "Willow Creek Fm"; Wikipedia, "Specimens of Tyrannosaurus."

## Jane

Jane (BMRP 2002.4.1; PBDB occurrence 1688887, collection 239512) was found in 2001 by a Burpee Museum of Natural History expedition near Ekalaka, northwestern Carter County, Montana, in the Hell Creek Formation. It is at the Burpee Museum, Rockford, Illinois. PBDB still lists it as a juvenile *Tyrannosaurus rex*, following Thomas et al. (2025), a pterosaur paper that cites the Jane field jacket. Zanno & Napoli, "Nanotyrannus and Tyrannosaurus coexisted at the close of the Cretaceous," Nature 648: 357–367 (published online 30 October 2025; https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-09801-6), describe a near-mature skeleton, NCSM 40000 (the tyrannosaur of the "Dueling Dinosaurs"). They conclude that *Nanotyrannus* is distinct from *Tyrannosaurus*, sits outside Tyrannosauridae, and contains two species: *N. lancensis* and a new species, *N. lethaeus*. Jane is figured throughout their diagnosis of the two species. The Burpee Museum says the study "identified Jane as a new species — *Nanotyrannus lethaeus*." Whether Jane is the formal holotype of *N. lethaeus* is not confirmed: the paper's text is behind a paywall and the Burpee page does not say. The question is not closed. Steve Brusatte said he is "not yet ready to proclaim every smaller tyrannosaur skeleton to be *Nanotyrannus*," and Holly Woodward Ballard called the findings hypotheses (National Geographic, 30 Oct 2025). For this database: as of the Nature paper, Jane should not be counted as *T. rex* without a flag. Sources: Zanno & Napoli 2025 (abstract and figure captions); Burpee Museum, "Jane: NOT a juvenile Tyrannosaur??", https://burpee.org/Jane/; National Geographic, https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/nanotyrannus-t-rex-dueling-dinosaur-fossil-debate; PBDB occurrence 1688887.
