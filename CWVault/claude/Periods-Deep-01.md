## 1. Molten

**Label:** Molten
**Kind:** period
**Span:** 4,600 to 4,000 Ma · both edges dated boundaries, rounded: the chart has 4,567 (the oldest grains in meteorites, ratified 2022) and 4,031 ± 3 (the oldest rock, Acasta, since 2023)
**Length:** 600 million years
**Official name:** Hadean, after Hades, the Greek god of the dead and the underworld he ruled; named by Preston Cloud in 1972
**Known from:** a few zircon crystals, smaller than grains of sand, and some moon rocks and meteorites
**Opens to:** nothing yet
**Inside it:** events *Oldest grains*, *Gathering*, *Moon formed*, *Black Earth*, *Blue Earth*, *Grey Earth* (at the end edge). Plots: *Day length* and the inside line of *Sun and inside* begin at *Moon formed*; *Minerals*, *Continents* and the Sun line begin at 4,567.
**Index:** none
**Pictures wanted:** a painting of the magma ocean with the close Moon (NASA or ESA artwork; licence to check); a Jack Hills zircon in cathodoluminescence (John Valley, University of Wisconsin; licence to ask).

**Summary (35 words)**
The whole surface of the Earth was melted rock, under a sky of steam. Within a few million years it froze over, the steam rained down, and there was a sea. The Moon was new. *More*

**More (335 words)**

```
From about 4.6 to 4 billion years ago.
```

At the start of this stretch there was nowhere to stand. The whole surface of the Earth was melted rock, glowing, and perhaps hundreds of kilometres deep. Above it hung air made of rock vapour and steam. The Earth had just been hit by another young planet, and the Moon had formed from the wreck (*Moon formed*). Through a gap in the steam, the Moon might have looked fifteen or twenty times wider than it does now, because it was that much closer.

The name *Molten* fits only the beginning. Models say the surface froze over within a few million years. Then the steam rained out, and the Earth had a sea. At first the sea was far hotter than boiling water. It stayed liquid because the thick air above it pressed down so hard.

By 4.4 billion years ago there was probably cool water at the surface. A crystal that formed then still remembers it (*Blue Earth*). So for nearly all of these 600 million years the Earth had a solid crust and a sea, and rocks from space kept falling on both.

What is new in this stretch is the planet itself: the Earth, with its Moon, then a crust, then a sea.

Almost nothing from it survives. There is no rock from most of it, only tiny crystals of zircon, a mineral so hard that it outlasts the rock it grew in. The oldest rock that nearly every geologist accepts formed beside the Acasta River in northern Canada, about 4.03 billion years ago (*Grey Earth*). That rock marks the end of this stretch.

Six hundred million years is about an eighth of the Earth's whole life. It is five sixths as long as the whole Animals line, from the first animals to now.

Geologists call this stretch the Hadean ("hay-DEE-un"), after Hades, the Greek god of the dead and the underworld he ruled. Preston Cloud, an American geologist, gave it the name in 1972.

Whether anything was alive in it, nobody knows.

**References**
- Kevin Zahnle and others, "Emergence of a habitable planet," *Space Science Reviews*, 2007. The magma ocean freezing, the steam raining out, the hot sea under heavy air, the close Moon.
- Simon Wilde, John Valley, William Peck and Colin Graham, "Evidence from detrital zircons for the existence of continental crust and oceans on the Earth 4.4 Gyr ago," *Nature*, 2001. Water by 4.4 billion.
- Jaana Halla and others, "Ratification of the base of the ICS Geological Time Scale: the GSSA for the Hadean lower boundary," *Episodes*, 2024. The official start.
- Robert Hazen, *The Story of Earth* (Viking, 2012). For a parent.

**Notes for us.** The scene is the magma ocean, the only moment the name describes; the More says so at once, which turns the name's weakness into its point. Research pass: Zahnle 2007: rock-vapour air condensed in about a thousand years, the surface froze in about two million (others up to about five, Elkins-Tanton 2008); then a steam atmosphere rained out into an ocean of about 500 kelvin (about 230 °C) under about 100 bars of carbon dioxide; the Moon formed at about three Earth radii (about 19,000 km against 384,400 today, so about twenty times closer and twenty times wider: a model). Hadean coined by Preston Cloud, *American Journal of Science* 1972 ("A working model of the primitive Earth"); from Hades, god and then place; "Hell" and "the unseen" are loose glosses, avoided. Chart v2026/06: base 4,567 (ratified 2022), top 4,031 ± 3 (since 2023/09, from the ten oldest zircon ages at Acasta); our 4,600 and 4,000 are Michael's roundings (10 Oct: a small discrepancy is fine). 600 of 4,600 is 13 percent, "about an eighth"; the Animals line is 720, so "nearly as long". Agreement: *Blue Earth* 4,404 ± 8 and "probably water"; *Grey Earth* 4,031 ± 3, "the oldest rock that nearly every geologist accepts" (the event's words). "Rocks from space kept falling" is general; *Bombardment* (3,900, argued) sits in Oceans and is not named here. "Hay-DEE-un" follows Merriam-Webster; "HAY-dee-un" is also heard. For the checkers: "perhaps hundreds of kilometres deep"; "about twenty times wider"; "far hotter than boiling water… stayed liquid because the thick air pressed down"; "within a few million years"; Cloud 1972 and "American geologist".

**Checked (10 Oct 2026, four independent checkers and one for numbers).** Changed: the Moon "might have looked fifteen or twenty times wider" (the model is an *if*, and *Moon formed* says fifteen or twenty); "five sixths as long as the whole Animals line" (600 of 720; "nearly" was loose); the plots in Inside it (the Sun line begins at 4,567, only the inside line at the Moon). Confirmed: Cloud 1972 and the title; Hades as god and place; both pronunciations accepted (Merriam-Webster lists "HAY-dee-un" first); chart edges 4,567 (2022) and 4,031 (2023); surface frozen in about two million years, a 500-kelvin ocean under about 100 bars (Zahnle 2007); Acasta 4.03, nearly every geologist; all four references. Not confirmed: nothing; "hundreds of kilometres deep" is cautious (some estimates run to 2,000).

## 2. Oceans

**Label:** Oceans
**Kind:** period
**Span:** 4,000 to 2,500 Ma · both edges dated boundaries: 4,000 is our rounding of the chart's 4,031 ± 3; 2,500 is a boundary fixed by agreement (1990), not by a layer of rock
**Length:** 1.5 billion years
**Official name:** Archean, from the Greek for "ancient", which comes from the word for "beginning"; named by James Dwight Dana in 1872
**Known from:** the oldest surviving rock, from about 4 billion years ago, and mounds built by microbes, from about 3.4 billion
**Opens to:** nothing yet
**Inside it:** events *Grey Earth* (at the start edge), *Bombardment*, *Stromatolites*, *Kenorland*, *Cyanobacteria* (at the end edge). Plots: *Oxygen* (begins at 4,000, near nothing), *Continents* (land a few hundredths of the surface), *Sun and inside*, *Day length*, *Minerals*.
**Index:** none
**Pictures wanted:** the stromatolites at Strelley Pool, Pilbara (Geological Survey of Western Australia or Commons; licence to check); living stromatolites at Hamelin Pool, Shark Bay, as a picture of what the mounds were like (Commons; licence to check).

**Summary (37 words)**
A world of sea, with a few low islands and almost no other dry land. There was no oxygen in the air. In shallow water, microbes built mounds, some of the oldest life we can point to. *More*

**More (338 words)**

```
From about 4 to 2.5 billion years ago.
```

In the Pilbara of Western Australia, near a waterhole called Strelley Pool, there is rock that was once the floor of a shallow sea. In it are low mounds and cones, row on row for kilometres. Microbes probably built them, about 3.4 billion years ago, layer on layer, as living mats caught grains of sand and grew up through them (*Stromatolites*).

Stand on that sea floor and almost everything around is water. For most of this stretch there was very little dry land, mostly small islands. By some estimates, even near its end only two or three parts in a hundred of the Earth's surface stood above the sea. Today it is about thirty.

The air held no oxygen to breathe. Nobody knows the colour of the sky. Near the end, a haze may have come and gone, each time for less than a million years.

The Sun was about a fifth dimmer than now, yet the sea did not freeze. Why not is still argued. The days were short. Layers laid down by the tides in South Africa, about 3.2 billion years ago, have been read as days of thirteen to fifteen hours, though the geologists who read them disagree.

What is new in this stretch is rock that survives, and life we can point to. Before it there are only crystals; from it there are whole mountainsides. And in its rocks are the oldest signs of life that most geologists accept.

All of that life was microbes, each too small to see. Near the end, some of them were probably already using sunlight to split water and give off oxygen, but almost none of the oxygen reached the air (*Cyanobacteria*).

A billion and a half years is a third of the Earth's whole life. It is about twice as long as the Animals line.

Geologists call this stretch the Archean ("ar-KEE-un"), from the Greek word for "ancient", which comes from the word for "beginning". James Dwight Dana, an American geologist, gave it the name in 1872.

**References**
- Abigail Allwood, Malcolm Walter, Balz Kamber, Craig Marshall and Ian Burch, "Stromatolite reef from the Early Archaean era of Australia," *Nature*, 2006. The scene.
- Nicolas Flament, Nicolas Coltice and Patrice Rey, "A case for late-Archaean continental emergence from thermal evolution models and hypsometry," *Earth and Planetary Science Letters*, 2008. How little land there was.
- Aubrey Zerkle and others, "A bistable organic-rich atmosphere on the Neoarchaean Earth," *Nature Geoscience*, 2012. The haze that came and went.
- Andrew Knoll, *A Brief History of Earth* (Custom House, 2021). For a parent.

**Notes for us.** The scene is Strelley Pool, where the Stromatolites event already stands; the More leans on it in one paragraph and leaves the event its story. The research pass killed two scenes I had planned: the "orange sky" (Arney 2016's "pale orange dot" is the planet seen from space in a model, and Zerkle 2012 finds three to five hazy spells between about 2,650 and 2,500 Ma, each under a million years) and the "green ocean" (Matsuo 2025 is about the colour of light under water, late in the stretch). The name: the oceans are older than the period (*Blue Earth*), so the More gives the name its honest sense, a world almost all sea. Research pass: Flament 2008, 2 to 3 percent emerged by about 2,500 Ma; Bindeman 2018, rapid emergence about 2,500; land today 29 percent; Sun about 74 percent of today's at 4,000 Ma and about 82 at 2,500 (Gough's formula, in Feulner 2012); the faint young Sun puzzle (Sagan and Mullen 1972), not solved; day about 13 hours at 3,200 Ma from tidal layers in the Moodies Group, Barberton (Eulenfeld and Heubeck 2023, argued); no free oxygen (less than a hundred-thousandth of today, Lyons 2014). Archean: Dana 1872 per Etymonline; *arkhaios*, "ancient", from *arkhē*, "beginning". The chart's start is 4,031 (Michael, 10 Oct: keep 4,000). Agreement: *Plants*, *Continents* ("a few hundredths of land before 2.5 billion, by some estimates"); *Cyanobacteria* ("probably already", "may hold a whiff"; the whiff is contested more sharply since Slotznick 2022, and the More does not lean on it). For the checkers: "row on row for kilometres" (Allwood: a reef over about 10 km); "mainly volcanic islands"; "about thirty" parts in a hundred today; "a fifth dimmer" (about 18 to 26 percent dimmer across the stretch; "about a fifth" is the middle); "most geologists accept" for Strelley Pool; Dana and 1872; "ar-KEE-un".

**Checked (10 Oct 2026, four independent checkers and one for numbers).** Changed: the summary's "some of the oldest life" (the Dresser stromatolites, about 3.48, are older than Strelley Pool); the mounds "probably" built by microbes, which "caught grains of sand" (Allwood: most plausibly; origin not proven); "mostly small islands" ("volcanic" had no source); the day "thirteen to fifteen hours… the geologists who read them disagree", to agree with the *Day length* plot. Confirmed: Strelley Pool about 3.43, more than 10 km of outcrop; 2 to 3 percent land by 2.5 billion, by some estimates; land today about 29 percent; haze spells under a million years, 2.65 to 2.5 billion (Arney 2016, from Zerkle 2012 and Izon 2015); the Sun a fifth dimmer; the faint young Sun unsolved; Dana 1872, *arkhaios*; all references. Not confirmed: none.

## 3. Oxygen

**Label:** Oxygen
**Kind:** period
**Span:** 2,500 to 1,800 Ma · both edges dated boundaries fixed by agreement (1990), not by layers of rock
**Length:** 700 million years
**Official name:** the first part of the Proterozoic, "earlier life" (from Greek *proteros*, earlier, and *zoe*, life), named by Samuel Franklin Emmons in the late 1880s; geologists split this part into the Siderian, Rhyacian and Orosirian, from Greek words for iron, a stream of lava and a range of mountains
**Known from:** rock that records the air: red soils and sandstones, banded iron, and sulphur in shale
**Opens to:** nothing yet
**Inside it:** events *Cyanobacteria* (at the start edge), *Short days*, *Great freeze*, *Oxygen in the air*, *Red rocks*, *Banded iron*, *Nuna* (at the end edge). Plots: *Oxygen* (the rise at about 2,430 and the coming and going to about 2,220), *Day length* (about 17 hours at 2,460), *Minerals*.
**Index:** none
**Pictures wanted:** the banded iron walls of Joffre Gorge or Dales Gorge, Karijini (Commons or Parks and Wildlife WA; licence to check); a polished slab of banded iron (Commons; licence to check).

**Summary (35 words)**
Stripes of iron were settling on the sea floor, and ice came and went. Then, for the first time, oxygen stayed in the air. There was only a little, but it turned the ground red. *More*

**More (344 words)**

```
From about 2.5 to 1.8 billion years ago.
```

In Karijini, in the Pilbara of Western Australia, the walls of Joffre Gorge are striped. Reddish-brown bands rich in iron lie between grey and white ones, thousands of them, each laid down on the floor of a sea about 2.46 billion years ago. They record slow wobbles in the Earth's spin, which depend on how near the Moon was, and read that way they say a day then lasted about seventeen hours (*Short days*).

At the start of this stretch the air still held almost no oxygen. Then, about 2.43 billion years ago, the oxygen made by microbes in the sea began to stay in the air (*Oxygen in the air*). For about two hundred million years it came and went, and only by about 2.2 billion years ago was it there for good.

That is what is new: oxygen in the air, for the first time.

How much there was is argued. Even at its highest it was probably well below today's, and near the end there was far less. But a little was enough to change the ground. Iron in rock rusts when oxygen reaches it, and soils and sand began to turn red (*Red rocks*). Many minerals that need oxygen to form could form for the first time.

The oxygen may also have brought the cold. Methane, a gas that kept the Earth warm, is destroyed by oxygen. Ice spread several times in this stretch, and at least once it reached land that lay near the equator (*Great freeze*).

All the life was still microbes. The deep sea still had no oxygen at all.

Seven hundred million years is about as long as the whole Animals line.

Geologists call this stretch the first part of the Proterozoic ("pro-ter-o-ZOH-ik"), Greek for "earlier life". Its three parts have names that mean iron, a stream of lava and a range of mountains.

Near its end, great beds of banded iron were laid down once more, around Lake Superior (*Banded iron*), and most of the land may have come together into one great continent (*Nuna*).

**References**
- Simon Poulton and others, "A 200-million-year delay in permanent atmospheric oxygenation," *Nature*, 2021. Oxygen coming and going, and staying from about 2.22 billion.
- Ashley Gumsley and others, "Timing and tempo of the Great Oxidation Event," *PNAS*, 2017. The first rise and the first ice.
- Timothy Lyons, Christopher Reinhard and Noah Planavsky, "The rise of oxygen in Earth's early ocean and atmosphere," *Nature*, 2014. How much, and how sure.
- Nick Lane, *Oxygen: The Molecule That Made the World* (Oxford University Press, 2002). For a parent.

**Notes for us.** The scene is Joffre Gorge, where two events already stand (*Banded iron* is dated at the Gunflint, 1,878; the Karijini iron is about 2,450 to 2,500, the older end of the same story; the More's "about two and a half billion" is Karijini's); it lets the stripes show both the period's opening and its clock without retelling either event. The cause of the cold is told as "may", because the Huronian trigger is favoured, not settled; whether any of this ice was a full snowball is argued and left out. The official name: there is no single unit for 2,500 to 1,800 (the Paleoproterozoic runs to 1,600); "the first part of the Proterozoic" is honest, and the three period names are the nicer fact. Research pass: Poulton 2021, oxygen first above a hundred-thousandth of today at about 2,430, coming and going until about 2,220; Gumsley 2017, 2,460 to 2,426; levels argued: up to about 10 percent of today's by some (Ossa Ossa 2022 discussion), "much lower than modern" by others; mid-Proterozoic perhaps below a tenth of a percent (Lyons 2014, Planavsky 2014); Lantink 2022, about 17 hours at 2,460; Huronian glaciations about 2,450 to 2,220. Proterozoic: Emmons, 1887 letter or 1888 (sources differ), so "the late 1880s"; Siderian *sideros* iron, Rhyacian *rhyax* stream of lava, Orosirian *oroseira* mountain range (strati.ch, the ICS's own page to confirm). Agreement: *Oxygen* plot (rise at 2,430, band to 2,220); *Short days* (2,459, about 17 hours); *Oxygen in the air* 2,430 ± 20; *Red rocks* 2,310 (oxidised ground may go back to about 2,450); *Great freeze* 2,450. For the checkers: Joffre Gorge's bands "red… between grey and black"; "thousands of them"; "a few parts in a hundred of today's" at the highest (could be "a tenth"); "far less near the end"; "as far as land that lay near the equator" (the Huronian low-latitude evidence; check it is not the Makganyene alone); "pro-ter-o-ZOH-ik".

**Checked (10 Oct 2026, four independent checkers and one for numbers).** Changed: Joffre Gorge's bands "reddish-brown… between grey and white" (Utrecht's description), dated 2.46 billion (as *Short days*), not linked to *Banded iron*, which is Lake Superior's 1.88 and now has its own clause near the end; the seventeen-hour day read from "slow wobbles in the Earth's spin", not the Moon's pull (Lantink 2022); how much oxygen: "even at its highest it was probably well below today's" (estimates run from much lower than modern to, in one model, above it); ice "at least once" near the equator (Makganyene; the Huronian low-latitude result is questioned); *Cyanobacteria* added to Inside it. Confirmed: Poulton 2021, 2.43, two hundred million years, 2.22; Gumsley 2017; Lyons 2014; red soils; methane and oxygen as a possible cause; Emmons, letter of 1887; Siderian, Rhyacian, Orosirian meanings; Nuna about 1.82; Lane 2002. Not confirmed: none.

## 4. Big cells

**Label:** Big cells
**Kind:** period
**Span:** 1,800 to 720 Ma · both edges dated boundaries fixed by agreement, not by layers of rock (1,800 since 1990; 720 provisional, pending a point in the rock, perhaps at the start of the first snowball, about 717)
**Length:** 1.1 billion years
**Official name:** no single name: the end of the Paleoproterozoic, the whole Mesoproterozoic, and the Tonian (the middle of the Proterozoic, "earlier life"); some geologists call it the boring billion
**Known from:** fossils of single cells and ribbons in shale, mounds built by microbes, and chemistry in the rock
**Opens to:** nothing yet
**Inside it:** events *Nuna* (at the start edge), *Cells with nuclei*, *Bangiomorpha*, *Rodinia cracks*. Plots: *Oxygen* (low and flat), *Day length*, *Minerals*.
**Index:** none
**Pictures wanted:** the ribbon fossils of the Gaoyuzhuang Formation, Hebei (Zhu and others 2016, *Nature Communications*, open access, CC BY; to confirm); *Bangiomorpha* under the microscope (Nicholas Butterfield; licence to ask).

**Summary (40 words)**
A quiet sea for a billion years, with little oxygen and, as far as anyone has found, no ice. In it lived a new kind of cell, bigger than a bacterium, with a nucleus. Some stuck together, and grew long. *More*

**More (349 words)**

```
From about 1.8 billion to 720 million years ago.
```

In the hills of Hebei, in northern China, there is shale that was mud on the floor of a shallow sea about 1.56 billion years ago. Split it open and some layers hold dark ribbons, a few of them as long as a school ruler. Each ribbon was a living thing made of many cells, probably using sunlight like seaweed.

A sea like that would have looked calm. There were no plants on land and no animals, and as far as anyone has found, there was no ice. The air held a little oxygen, how much is argued, and the deep water held none. In places the water stank of rotten eggs. The Moon was closer than now, and a day lasted about eighteen or nineteen hours.

For a long time this stretch seemed so steady that a geologist wrote, in 1995, that never in the Earth's history "did so little happen to so much for so long". It has been called the boring billion. That may be unfair. Oxygen seems to have risen and fallen in pulses, and the continents came together into one (*Nuna*), broke apart, and came together again into another, called Rodinia, which began to crack near the end (*Rodinia cracks*).

What is new is the cell with a nucleus: a cell that keeps its genes in a small bag of their own. Such cells are often ten times as wide as bacteria, and they are the kind that every plant, animal and fungus is made of. The oldest fossils that may be of them are about 1.64 billion years old (*Cells with nuclei*). About 1.05 billion years ago a red seaweed on what is now Somerset Island, in Nunavut, left the oldest signs of sex (*Bangiomorpha*).

A billion years and more is nearly a quarter of the Earth's whole life. It is one and a half times as long as the Animals line.

Geologists have no single name for it. It is the middle of the Proterozoic, "earlier life".

Just after it ended, ice spread from the poles almost to the equator (*Snowball one*).

**References**
- Shixing Zhu and others, "Decimetre-scale multicellular eukaryotes from the 1.56-billion-year-old Gaoyuzhuang Formation in North China," *Nature Communications*, 2016. The scene.
- Roger Buick, David Des Marais and Andrew Knoll, "Stable isotopic compositions of carbonates from the Mesoproterozoic Bangemall Group, northwestern Australia," *Chemical Geology*, 1995. "So little… for so long".
- Timothy Gibson and others, "Precise age of *Bangiomorpha pubescens* dates the origin of eukaryotic photosynthesis," *Geology*, 2018.
- Andrew Knoll, *Life on a Young Planet* (Princeton University Press, 2003). For a parent.

**Notes for us.** The scene is the Gaoyuzhuang ribbons, because they show "big" in a way she can hold (a ruler) and they sit mid-stretch; the More names the period's events in clauses. "Big cells" is safe: no eukaryote fossils older than 1,800 are widely accepted (*Grypania*, about 1,870, is contested; the Francevillian of Gabon, 2,100, widely doubted). Research pass: Zhu 2016, ribbons up to 30 cm long and 8 cm wide, probably photosynthetic, 1.56 billion; Miao 2024, 1.63 billion multicellular eukaryotes in the Chuanlinggou; oldest accepted single-celled eukaryotes about 1.65 billion; oxygen 0.1 to 10 percent of today's, argued (Planavsky 2014); deep water anoxic, some coastal water sulphidic (Canfield 1998, Poulton); day just over 18 hours at 1.4 billion (Meyers and Malinverno 2018), about 19 hours stalled from 2 to 1 billion (Mitchell and Kirscher 2023), hence "eighteen or nineteen"; "boring billion" usually credited to Martin Brasier (*Secret Chambers*, 2012) and the idea to Buick, Des Marais and Knoll 1995, whose quotation this is; Nuna assembled by 1.8, breakup by 1.3 to 1.2 (Zhao 2004; *Nuna* says "until somewhere around 1,500 to 1,400", which is loose enough to agree); Rodinia assembled 1,300 to 900, rifting from 825 (Li 2008), agreeing with *Rodinia cracks* 827. Cryogenian base about 720, provisional (Shields 2022). Agreement: *Cells with nuclei* 1,640 ± 10 (Chuanlinggou, part of the Changcheng Group); *Bangiomorpha* 1,047; *Snowball one* 717. "Somerset Island, in Nunavut": the fossil is from the Hunting Formation there; the date came from Baffin Island. ## 5. Animals

**Label:** Animals
**Kind:** period
**Span:** 720 Ma to now · the start a dated boundary fixed by agreement (the start of the Cryogenian, provisional); moved from a guessed 700 on 10 Oct
**Length:** 720 million years
**Official name:** none for the whole; it starts about 180 million years before the Phanerozoic, Greek for "visible life", which begins 539 million years ago (named by George Chadwick in 1930)
**Known from:** fossils of animals with hard parts, from about 539 million years ago; before that, rare soft fossils and argued chemistry
**Opens to:** Soft bodies, Trilobites, Dinosaurs, Mammals
**Inside it:** every event from *Snowball one* (717) to now: fifty-two, with the two new ones. By line: Soft bodies, ten; Trilobites, twelve; Dinosaurs, seven; Mammals, eleven, and the twelve of batch 6. Plots: *Sea level* (from 540), *Oxygen*, *People* (from 315,000 years ago).
**Index:** the horse and the elephant line, followed down the Mammals line as events (batch 3)
**Pictures wanted:** a living sponge, as the animal the oldest argued chemistry points to (Commons; licence to check); a blue whale beside a frond from Mistaken Point, drawn to one scale (to make).

**Summary (30 words)**
The first animals were probably small and soft, and left almost nothing behind. Then they grew, a millionfold in a hundred and fifty million years: fronds, trilobites, fish, dinosaurs, whales. *More*

**More (334 words)**

```
From about 720 million years ago to now.
```

At the start of this stretch, ice was about to spread over almost the whole Earth (*Snowball one*). Somewhere in the sea there were probably animals already. Nobody has found them. They were probably small and soft, and they left almost nothing behind.

How can anyone say they were there? From their descendants. Every living animal carries genes copied from its parents, with small changes each time. Counting the differences between the genes of living animals, and how fast such changes pile up, gives a rough date for their common ancestor. The answers run from about 830 to 650 million years ago. In rock in Oman more than 635 million years old there is also a chemical that some scientists read as made by sponges, the simplest animals. Others think algae made it. That is still argued.

What is new is animals: living things made of many cells that eat other living things. Most of them move.

Then they grew. The oldest big fossils that may be animals are about 574 million years old: fronds and quilted shapes on the sea floor, some nearly two metres long (*Mistaken Point*). From about 600 to 450 million years ago the biggest living things became about a million times bigger. Then came trilobites, fish, trees, dinosaurs, and today the blue whale, the heaviest animal known.

The four stretches below are named for what was most noticed in each, not for what arrived first. There were no trilobites at the start of Trilobites, and no dinosaurs at the start of Dinosaurs. Mammals had already lived for about a hundred and forty million years when Mammals began.

Seven hundred and twenty million years is about a sixth of the Earth's whole life.

Geologists have no single name for it. Their Phanerozoic ("fan-er-o-ZOH-ik"), Greek for "visible life", starts later, about 539 million years ago, where fossils of animals with hard parts begin to fill the rocks (*Animals, kept*).

When the first animal lived, and what it looked like, nobody knows.

At Mistaken Point, on the south-eastern tip of Newfoundland, the sea has worn the cliffs away to bare, tilted sheets of rock. On them lie thousands of fossils: fronds like feathers, branched like ferns, some a hand long and some nearly two metres. About 565 million years ago they stood up from the mud of a deep sea floor, where sunlight probably did not reach. Then ash from a volcano settled over them and kept them where they stood (*Mistaken Point*).

Nothing in that scene could bite. None of the fronds had a mouth. Many of them may have fed by soaking up food from the water through their skin.

That is what is new: big living things with soft bodies, the oldest big fossils with complicated shapes. Some, like *Dickinsonia*, an oval quilt up to more than a metre long, were animals: its fossils still hold cholesterol, a fat that is the mark of animals (*Dickinsonia*). What many of the others were, nobody knows.

The stretch began in ice. Twice, ice spread from the poles almost to the equator, first for about fifty-seven million years (*Snowball one*), then for a few million (*Snowball two*). For about a third of this stretch the Earth was white. When the second ice melted, it melted fast (*The thaw*).

Near the end, about 550 million years ago, small animals built tubes of shell. The oldest yet found are in Namibia. In China, some of the tubes have tiny round holes bored through them, the oldest signs yet found of one animal hunting another (*Bites and shells*).

A hundred and eighty million years is a quarter of the Animals line. It is nearly three times as long as everything since the dinosaurs died.

Geologists split this stretch in two. The Cryogenian ("cry-o-JEN-ee-un"), "birth of ice", is the time of the two snowballs. The Ediacaran ("ee-dee-AK-a-run") is named after the Ediacara Hills in South Australia. It was added in 2004, the first new period in more than a hundred years.

At its end, animals with hard parts begin to fill the rocks (*Animals, kept*).

At Chengjiang, in Yunnan, in southern China, there is shale that was mud on the floor of a shallow sea about 518 million years ago. Mud slid over the animals living there and buried them so quickly that their soft parts were kept: legs, guts, eyes. About 250 kinds of animal have been found in it (*Chengjiang*). Many crawled on the sea floor. Some swam above it. Some had claws for catching the others.

Among them, as in rocks of this age all over the world, are trilobites ("TRY-lo-bites"), some of the commonest fossils of the time. A trilobite was an armoured animal with many jointed legs. Most were about the size of a finger; the biggest known was longer than a child's arm. Their eyes had lenses made of a clear mineral, calcite. The lenses were stone even while the animal lived, so they last, and they are among the oldest eyes that can be studied lens by lens. More than twenty thousand kinds have been named.

Yet there were no trilobites at the start of this stretch. The oldest found are about 520 million years old, so for nearly the first twenty million years there were none to find.

What is new is a world of hard parts: shells, spines, jaws, eyes. And, in the middle of the stretch, the land turned green. Plants came ashore (*Plants ashore*), then small animals (*Animals ashore*), then forests (*Forests*), then a fish with fins nearly like legs (*Tiktaalik*), and then eggs that could be laid on dry land (*Land egg*).

Trilobites were most various a little under 500 million years ago and for a while after, and then they dwindled. By the end there were only a few kinds left, small ones.

Two hundred and ninety million years is two fifths of the Animals line. It is longer than the Dinosaurs line and the Mammals line put together.

Geologists call this stretch the Paleozoic ("pay-lee-o-ZOH-ik"), "ancient life".

The last trilobites died in the Great Dying, with four in five kinds of sea animal (*The Great Dying*).

In the Karoo of South Africa, a dry country of flat-topped hills, there are layers of old river mud from just after the Great Dying. Nine in ten of the bones found in them belong to one animal: *Lystrosaurus* ("liss-tro-SAW-rus"), a stocky plant-eater about a metre long, with two tusks and a beak (*Lystrosaurus*).

That emptiness is the scene. All the land was joined in one continent, Pangaea, and much of its middle was desert. In the tropics the sea at the surface may have been as hot as a hot bath, about 40 °C, and for about five million years almost no fish or reptiles lived there. It took eight or nine million years for the living world to fill up again.

There were no dinosaurs in it. The oldest found, in Brazil and Argentina, are about 233 to 231 million years old, nearly twenty million years after this stretch began (*Dinosaurs*). Even then they were not in charge. For a long time the reptiles on the crocodile side of the family were often more common and came in more shapes.

Then, about 201 million years ago, Pangaea began to crack open, volcanoes poured out lava along the cracks, and another dying took away many of those rivals (*Pangaea cracks*, *Triassic ends*). After it, dinosaurs ruled the land.

What is new is the dinosaurs, and the world they lived in: for most of this stretch the Earth was warm, with little or no ice at the poles. Birds appeared (*Archaeopteryx*), and so did flowers (*Flowers*). Small mammals lived through most of it.

A hundred and eighty-six million years is a quarter of the Animals line. It is nearly three times as long as the Mammals line.

Geologists call this stretch the Mesozoic ("mez-o-ZOH-ik"), "middle life". John Phillips, an English geologist, gave it the name in 1840.

It ended about 66 million years ago, when an asteroid about ten kilometres across hit what is now Yucatán, in Mexico, and every dinosaur died except the birds (*The asteroid*).

## Fern spike

**Label:** Fern spike
**Age:** 66.0 Ma · ± 0.05 Ma · dated by the boundary it lies on: the fern layer sits directly on the clay rich in iridium that the impact laid down

**Kind:** life

**Summary (39 words)**
About 66 million years ago. Just after the asteroid, ferns grew back first. In rock from New Mexico to Saskatchewan, and in New Zealand, most of the fossil spores and pollen just above the impact layer come from ferns. *More*

**More (292 words)**

```
About 66 million years ago.
```

In the hills of the Raton Basin, on the border of New Mexico and Colorado, there is a pale layer of clay only a finger or two thick. It is the layer the asteroid laid down when it hit Yucatán (*The asteroid*). It is rich in iridium, a metal that is rare in the Earth's crust and common in asteroids.

Geologists take small pieces of the rock just below the clay and just above it, dissolve the rock in acid, and look at what is left under a microscope: pollen and spores, the tiny grains plants release to make more plants. Pollen and spores are tough, and they last for millions of years.

Below the clay, the grains come from many kinds of plant. Ferns make up perhaps a fifth or a quarter of them.

Just above the clay, most of them are fern spores. In some samples, ninety-nine in every hundred.

A hand's width higher, the other plants come back.

Ferns are good at starting over. Their spores blow everywhere, and many ferns can sprout again from roots and stems buried in the ground. After the impact, when forests had died back across much of the world, ferns were the first to cover the bare ground.

The same layer of fern spores has been traced along more than a thousand kilometres, from New Mexico north to Saskatchewan. On the other side of the world, in New Zealand, the share of fern spores jumps the same way, from about a quarter to about nine in ten.

How long the ferns held on is argued. Some say a few thousand years, some say tens of thousands. In Colorado, by about a hundred thousand years after the impact, palm forests had grown up (*Mammals*).

## Lystrosaurus

**Label:** Lystrosaurus
**Age:** 251.8 Ma · ± 0.5 Ma · from the layers: the earliest Triassic beds of the Karoo, above a boundary bracketed between about 252.24 and 251.7 Ma (Botha 2020); how these beds line up with the sea extinction is argued (Gastaldo 2020)
**Tail:** none — the event is the animal's commonness after the dying, not its first appearance (it lived before the dying too)
**Known from:** bones in old river mud in the Karoo, and in rock of the same age in Antarctica, India, China and Russia
**Place now:** the Karoo Basin, South Africa (about 32.0 S, 25.5 E, for the zone as a whole; a site near Bethulie to choose)
**Kind:** crop or animal (a living thing; the list has no better word)
**Replaces:** none; new

**Summary (37 words)**
About 252 million years ago. Just after the Great Dying, one plant-eater, about a metre long, spread across much of the land. In the Karoo of South Africa, nine in ten bones from that time are its. *More*

**More (290 words)**

```
About 252 million years ago.
```

The Karoo is a wide, dry country in the middle of South Africa, with flat-topped hills and long views. Its rocks are layers of mud and sand laid down by rivers, one on another, for tens of millions of years. Fossil hunters have walked those hillsides for more than a century, and today each bone they find is recorded with the layer it came from.

Low in the layers, the bones come from many kinds of animal. Then comes the Great Dying (*The Great Dying*), and above it, nearly every bone is the same animal.

It is called *Lystrosaurus* ("liss-tro-SAW-rus"), which means "shovel lizard". It was not a lizard, and not a dinosaur. It belonged to the same great branch of animals as mammals. It was stocky, about a metre long, with a beak for cutting plants and two tusks. Of the bones found in the layers just above the Great Dying, more than nine in ten are *Lystrosaurus*.

*Lystrosaurus* had lived before the dying too. What was strange afterwards was that there was almost nothing else. Why it survived when most others did not is argued. It may have been able to eat almost any plant, to dig burrows, and to grow up fast.

It did not stay in the Karoo. Its bones are found in rock of the same age in India, China and Russia, and in Antarctica. In 1969 and 1970 geologists found *Lystrosaurus* in the Transantarctic Mountains, about 650 kilometres from the South Pole. A land animal the size of a dog could not swim there. The find helped convince people that the continents had once been joined (*Pangaea cracks*).

Within a few million years, other animals came back, and *Lystrosaurus* became rare and then disappeared.
