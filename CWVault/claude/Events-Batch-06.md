On the night of 7 January 1610, in Padua, in northern Italy, a professor of mathematics named Galileo Galilei ("gal-ih-LAY-oh gal-ih-LAY-ee") pointed a tube with two glass lenses in it at the planet Jupiter. Beside the planet he saw three small stars in a line. The next night they were in different places. On 13 January he found a fourth, and over the following weeks he watched all four move from one side of Jupiter to the other and back. They were not stars. They were moons, going around Jupiter as our Moon goes around the Earth.

The telescope was new. The first one we have a record of was shown by a Dutch spectacle-maker in the autumn of 1608, and news of telescopes reached Galileo the next year. Working only from the news, he said, he worked out how one must be built, ground his own lenses, and kept improving them until distant things looked about twenty times closer.

Late in 1609 he turned his telescope on the Moon. The line between the Moon's lit half and its dark half was not smooth but ragged, and just beyond it, in the dark, there were bright points. Galileo reasoned that they were mountain tops catching the sunrise while the land below them was still in shadow, as happens on Earth at dawn. From how far the bright points stood from the line, he worked out that some of the Moon's mountains were more than four miles high, in the Italian miles of his day.

He also found that the Milky Way, the pale band across the night sky, is made of stars, too many and too faint to see one by one without a telescope.

In March 1610 he described all of it in a short book called *The Starry Messenger*. Five hundred and fifty copies were printed in Venice, and they were gone in little more than a week.

Galileo named Jupiter's moons after the Medici family, who ruled Florence. That summer the head of the family, the Grand Duke of Tuscany, made Galileo his mathematician.

## 2. Decimal fractions

**Label:** Decimal fractions
**Year:** 1585 · precision: year · kind: text
**Place:** Leiden, the Netherlands (52.16 N, 4.50 E)

**Summary (35 words)**
1585. In Leiden, in the Netherlands, Simon Stevin published a short booklet showing how to write parts of things in tenths, hundredths and thousandths, so that they could be added and multiplied like whole numbers. *More*

**More (349 words)**

```

      or 1585
            or 441 years ago.
```

A merchant in 1585 might need to add three eighths of a guilder, the Dutch money of the day, to seven twentieths of a guilder. How much is that altogether?

To find out, he had to make the two fractions the same kind. Eighths and twentieths both fit into fortieths, so three eighths becomes fifteen fortieths, and seven twentieths becomes fourteen fortieths. Together they make twenty-nine fortieths. That was one sum. Multiplying fractions was harder, and dividing them harder still.

Before about 1600, almost everyone in Europe who worked with parts of things had to do it this way: merchants, surveyors measuring land, people who measured cloth or wine. Astronomers used another system. They split each degree into sixty minutes and each minute into sixty seconds. We still split hours that way.

In 1585 a booklet of about thirty pages appeared in Leiden ("LIE-den"). It was called *De Thiende*, Dutch for "the tenth," and its author was Simon Stevin ("stay-VIN"), a mathematician. He addressed it to stargazers, surveyors, measurers of tapestry and wine casks, the masters of the mint, and all merchants.

His idea was to count parts the way we already count whole things. In 375, the 3 means hundreds, the 7 tens and the 5 ones. Keep going to the right, and the next places can mean tenths, hundredths and thousandths. Three eighths becomes three tenths, seven hundredths and five thousandths, which we write 0.375. Seven twentieths becomes 0.35. Add them the way you add any two numbers, and the answer is 0.725, which is twenty-nine fortieths again.

Stevin did not use a dot. He wrote a small number in a circle after each figure to say which place it held. The dot, or in many countries a comma, came a few decades later.

Stevin also said that coins, weights and measures should be divided into tens, and that it was only a matter of time. It took a long time. The United States made its dollar of a hundred cents in 1792, and France brought in the metre in 1795.

The hour still has sixty minutes.

## 3. Franklin's kite

1752. In Philadelphia, Benjamin Franklin flew a kite under a thunder cloud and drew sparks from a key tied to its string. He wanted to know whether lightning was electricity. *More*

**More (348 words)**

Under a dark summer sky, a man stood in a shed in a field, holding a kite string by a silk ribbon. The kite was made of a silk handkerchief, with a sharp wire sticking up from its top, and a metal key hung on the string just above the ribbon. The loose threads of the string stood out on end, and when the man brought his knuckle near the key, a spark jumped to it. Once rain had soaked the string, the sparks came freely.

The man was Benjamin Franklin, a printer in Philadelphia, and his question was whether lightning is electricity.

In 1752, electricity was something people made for themselves, by rubbing glass tubes or spinning glass globes. It could be stored in a glass jar coated with metal foil, called a Leyden jar, and let go all at once with a bang. A spark and a bang looked like lightning and thunder, only much smaller.

Two years earlier, Franklin had suggested a way to find out: set up a tall, pointed iron rod on a high place and see whether it drew sparks from a passing storm. His letters were printed in London in 1751, and on 10 May 1752, in a garden at Marly, near Paris, a retired soldier named Coiffier did it and drew sparks. Franklin seems not to have heard about it when he flew his kite.

The kite was not struck by lightning. The sharp wire drew electricity from the storm cloud, and the wet string carried it down. The next year, in St Petersburg, Georg Wilhelm Richmann was killed by lightning that came down a rod he had set up to study it.

Franklin's own account, printed that October, does not say when he flew the kite. The date comes from Joseph Priestley, who wrote fifteen years later, probably from what Franklin told him.

Franklin's answer to the danger was the lightning rod: a pointed iron rod on a roof, with a wire down into the ground. By the end of 1752 there were rods on two buildings in Philadelphia.

## 4. Galvani's frogs

**Year:** 1791 · precision: year · kind: craft

**Summary (36 words)**
1791. In Bologna, in Italy, Luigi Galvani published eleven years of experiments on frogs' legs that twitched when touched with metal. He said animals make electricity of their own. Alessandro Volta said the metal made it. *More*

**More (350 words)**

In a room in Bologna, in Italy, a frog had been cut up for study, down to its legs and the nerves that run to them. It lay on a table near a machine that made electricity. One of Luigi Galvani's ("gal-VAH-nee") assistants touched a nerve lightly with the tip of a scalpel, and the frog's legs jerked as if they were alive. Someone else noticed that this happened just when a spark leapt from the machine.

Galvani's notebooks put the first such twitch in the winter of 1780 to 1781. He was a doctor who taught anatomy, the study of how bodies are built, and he spent the next ten years trying to understand what he had seen.

The strangest result came in 1786. Frogs prepared with brass hooks through their spines were hung on an iron railing, and the legs twitched whenever a hook touched the iron. They did it in calm weather as well as in storms, and they did it indoors. There was no machine and no lightning.

So where was the electricity coming from?

Galvani's answer was the frog. In 1791 he published a long report saying that animals make electricity of their own, and that nerves carry it to the muscles and make them move. The metal, he thought, only gave it a path.

Alessandro Volta ("VOL-tuh"), a professor of physics at Pavia, read the report and at first agreed. Then he changed his mind. The hook was brass and the railing was iron, and Volta argued that two different metals touching make the electricity themselves. The frog's leg, he said, was only a very sensitive way of noticing it.

Galvani answered with experiments that used no metal at all. In 1794 he made a frog's muscle twitch by touching it with its own nerve, and in 1797 he made one frog's leg twitch with a nerve from another frog.

In the end, both men were partly right. Nerves do carry electrical signals to the muscles. And two different metals, with something wet between them, do make electricity, as Volta went on to show.

## 5. Laki

**Label:** Laki
**Year:** 1783 · precision: exact (8 June) · kind: earth
**Place:** Lakagígar, the craters of Laki, south Iceland (64.06 N, 18.23 W)
**Replaces:** `laki`

**Summary (36 words)**
1783. In the south of Iceland, a crack in the ground 27 kilometres long poured out lava and poisonous gas for eight months. Most of Iceland's farm animals died, and then about one Icelander in six. *More*

**More (349 words)**

            or 243 years ago.

On 8 June 1783, in the south of Iceland, the ground split open near a mountain called Laki ("LAH-kee"), and lava began pouring out. Within a few weeks, Jón Steingrímsson, the pastor at Prestbakki, near the village of Kirkjubæjarklaustur, began writing down what he saw. His account is one of the main ways we know what happened.

The crack grew to twenty-seven kilometres long, with about a hundred and forty craters along it. For eight months they poured out lava, about fifteen cubic kilometres of it, enough to cover six hundred square kilometres. Icelanders call the eruption *Skaftáreldar*, the Skaftá Fires, after the Skaftá, one of the two rivers whose valleys the lava filled.

The lava was not the worst of it. The craters also gave off gas, and the gas made a haze that hung over the country all summer. It carried fluorine, a poison, which settled on the grass. Sheep, cattle and horses ate the grass and sickened. By 1785 about half of Iceland's cattle had died, about three out of every four horses, and four out of every five sheep.

Icelanders lived on the milk, meat and wool of their animals. With the animals gone, the people went hungry, and many fell ill. In the next two years about eight thousand more Icelanders died than in ordinary years, about one in every six people in the country. The time is still called *Móðuharðindin*, the Haze Famine. People used to think the gas had poisoned the people as well as the animals, but a study of the parish records, published in 2024, found that hunger and disease explain who died, and where, and when.

The haze drifted south as well. That summer a dry fog lay over much of Europe. Benjamin Franklin, who was living just outside Paris, wrote that the sun's rays were so weak that a burning glass, a lens that gathers sunlight to a hot point, would scarcely set brown paper alight. Franklin guessed it might be smoke from Hekla, a famous volcano in Iceland.

He had the right country but the wrong volcano.

Around the end of 1799, near Como, a town on a lake in northern Italy, Alessandro Volta ("VOL-tuh") built a strange stack. At the bottom was a disc of silver or copper, then a disc of zinc, then a disc of card soaked in salt water. Then metal, metal and wet card again, and again, twenty times over. When he touched the top and the bottom with wet hands, he felt a shock, and when he touched them again he felt another. The stack did not run down.

Volta was a professor of physics at Pavia, and for years he had argued with Luigi Galvani, a doctor in Bologna, who died in 1798. Galvani had found that frogs' legs twitch when they touch two different metals, and he said the electricity came from the frog. Volta said it came from the metals. The stack was his proof. It had no frog in it at all.

Until then, electricity came one spark at a time. People made it by rubbing glass and stored it in a jar coated with metal foil, and once the jar had given its spark, it had to be charged again. Volta's stack needed no charging. As long as the card stayed wet, it kept electricity moving, a steady flow that we now call a current. Volta called his stack an artificial electric organ, after the electric fish that can give a shock.

On 20 March 1800 he described it in a letter to Joseph Banks, the president of the Royal Society in London. Word of the letter reached a surgeon, Anthony Carlisle, who built a stack of his own with William Nicholson. They put two wires from it into water, and bubbles of gas rose around the wires: the current was splitting the water into hydrogen and oxygen.

In 1801 Volta showed his stack in Paris, with Napoleon Bonaparte, who then ruled France, in the audience, and he was given a gold medal.

The number printed on a battery, 1.5 on a small one, is counted in volts, a unit named after him.

On 5 April 1815, people on the island of Java heard heavy booms far away. Most of them took it for cannon fire. It was a volcano, far to the east.

The volcano was Tambora ("tam-BOR-uh"), on the island of Sumbawa ("soom-BAH-wuh"), in what is now Indonesia. Five days later, on the evening of 10 April, it blew apart. A column of ash and rock rose more than forty kilometres into the sky and then fell back down the mountain's sides as glowing avalanches of hot gas and ash. The explosions were heard more than 1,500 kilometres away.

When it was over, the top of the mountain was gone. Tambora had been about 4,300 metres tall, by one estimate, and now it was about 2,850, with a crater six or seven kilometres across.

About eleven thousand people were killed by the eruption itself. Many more died afterwards, because ash had buried the fields and food ran out on Sumbawa and on Lombok, the island next to it. Altogether at least seventy-one thousand people died. Two small kingdoms on the mountain's slopes, Tambora and Pekat, disappeared, and so did the language of Tambora, which nobody speaks today. Only a short list of its words survives, written down before the eruption.

In 2004 a team of Indonesian and American volcanologists dug through three metres of ash on the mountain's side and found a house, with bronze bowls, pots and iron tools in it, and two people who had died inside.

The eruption also threw tens of millions of tonnes of sulphur gas high above the weather. There it turned into a fine mist of tiny droplets, which spread around the world and reflected some of the sunlight back into space. For a year or two the Earth was a little colder. In 1816 snow fell in New England in June, and harvests failed across much of Europe, where people remembered it as the year without a summer.

Nobody at the time connected the cold to a volcano on the other side of the world. That came much later.

From an upstairs window of the house at Le Gras, in the countryside of eastern France, you could see a courtyard, a pigeon house, a pear tree, the sloping roof of a barn and the chimney of the bakehouse. Joseph Nicéphore Niépce ("nee-EPS") set a box at that window and left it there for days.

The box was a camera obscura, Latin for "dark room": a closed box with a lens in one side. Light from the view outside comes in through the lens and makes a small picture of it, upside down, on the back of the box. People had used such boxes for centuries to help them draw. Niépce wanted the picture to draw itself.

At the back of the box he put a polished plate of pewter, about twenty centimetres by seventeen. He had coated the plate thinly with bitumen, a kind of natural tar, dissolved in oil of lavender. Bitumen hardens where light falls on it. Where the view was bright, the bitumen went hard, and where it was dark, the bitumen stayed soft. When he took the plate out, he washed it with a mixture that dissolved the soft bitumen and left the hard. What remained was a picture.

The picture is dim and hard to make out. The sun lights both sides of the buildings, because the sun crossed the sky again and again while the plate was in the box. For many years books said it took about eight hours. People who have since tried Niépce's method think it took several days of sunshine. Even the year has moved: books used to say 1826, and the museum that owns it now says 1827.

Niépce called his process heliography, from Greek words meaning "sun writing." He took the plate to England that year, and he left it there. Then it was lost. In 1952 Helmut Gernsheim, a historian of photography, and his wife, Alison, tracked it down. It had been forgotten in a trunk.

Today it is at the Harry Ransom Center in Austin, Texas, sealed in a case with no oxygen in it.

The ring is iron, about fifteen centimetres across and nearly as thick as a thumb. Around one side of it, and separately around the other side, Michael Faraday wound coils of copper wire, wrapped in cotton and twine so that the turns would not touch. Some of the wire had been made for bonnets. The ring is still at the Royal Institution in London, where he made it.

Eleven years earlier, a Danish professor, Hans Christian Ørsted ("UR-sted"), had found that an electric current flowing in a wire makes a compass needle near it swing. So electricity could make magnetism. Faraday wanted to know whether magnetism could make electricity, and he had been trying for years.

On 29 August 1831 he joined one coil to a battery. He joined the other coil to a long wire that ran to a compass needle about a metre away. Ørsted's discovery meant that the needle would swing if any current flowed in the second coil. When Faraday connected the battery, the needle swung, wobbled, and settled back where it had been. When he disconnected the battery, the needle swung again. While the battery stayed connected, nothing happened.

Here is what was going on. The current in the first coil turned the iron ring into a magnet. While the magnet was growing, and again while it was fading, a current flowed in the second coil. A magnet that stayed the same made nothing at all. What made electricity was a change: magnetism growing or fading, or, as he soon found, a wire moving past a magnet.

Within two months Faraday had shown the same thing more simply, by pushing a bar magnet into a coil of wire and pulling it out. Then he set a copper disc spinning between the ends of a huge magnet, and drew a steady current from it for as long as it turned.

Most of the electricity made in the world today comes from machines that do what his magnet and coil did, over and over: they turn magnets and coils of wire past each other.

On a page of a small notebook, someone has written two words, "I think," and then stopped and drawn instead. The drawing starts at a point marked 1. Lines branch out from it, and branch again, and again. Some of the branches end in a short crossing line, and others just stop. Four of the ends are labelled A, B, C and D.

The notebook belonged to Charles Darwin. He was twenty-eight, and nine months earlier he had come home from almost five years on a navy ship, the *Beagle*, which had sailed around the world while he collected animals, plants, fossils and rocks. Now he lived in central London and was working through what he had found. Inside the cover of this notebook he wrote that he had begun it about July 1837.

The drawing is a guess about where kinds of living things, the species, come from. Suppose every species began as a branch of some older species, and that one as a branch of something older still. Then the species alive today are the tips of the branches. Some branches have died out, and the species on them are gone. Where many have died out between two tips, the two living species look very different, like A and B. Where few have, they look alike, like B and C. Under the drawing Darwin wrote, "Thus between A & B. immense gap of relation, C & B. the finest gradation, B & D rather greater distinction."

Darwin was not the first to draw living things as a branching tree.

Twenty-two years later he published the idea, in a book called *On the Origin of Species*. The book had only one picture in it: a branching tree.

In 2001 the notebook was found to be missing from Cambridge University Library, where it was kept. On 9 March 2022 it came back. Someone left it outside the librarian's office in a bright pink gift bag, with a note that said, "Librarian. Happy Easter. X."
