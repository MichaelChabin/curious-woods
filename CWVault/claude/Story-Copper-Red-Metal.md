# Red Metal

*About 3,500 years after the ice, or about 6500 BCE*

[Opening picture: a lump of native copper from the Keweenaw. Magnifier on. Tool word: *Look closer*.]

This lump came out of the ground near the biggest lake in North America. Nobody melted it. Nobody cooked it out of a rock. It came out of the ground the way you see it here, already metal.

Tap *Look closer*.

## Before anything else

I'd like you to try something before I tell you what it's for.

Find a piece of bare copper wire, the kind inside an old lamp cord. Ask before you cut one. Hold it in both hands and bend it back and forth at one spot. Count the bends.

Keep going until it breaks.

Keep the two pieces. We'll come back to them.

## A continent that almost broke

About eleven hundred million years ago, North America began to tear in two. A crack opened in the middle of the land, three thousand kilometres long. Lava poured out of it, flow after flow, for more than twenty million years. In places the lava piled up twenty kilometres deep.

Then the tearing stopped, and the land stayed in one piece. Nobody is sure why.

The biggest lake in North America lies in what is left of that crack. The Ojibwe, who live around it, call it Gichigami, the great sea. On maps it is Lake Superior.

[Tool word: *The copper's road*. The stack opens here.]

## Bubbles

Tap *The copper's road*.

The top of each lava flow was full of gas bubbles, like the holes in a slice of bread. When the lava cooled to stone, the holes stayed.

Millions of years later, hot water crept through the rock, deep underground. As it went, it picked up copper from the lava it passed through, a little at a time. Where the water cooled, it left the copper behind. The copper filled the bubbles. It filled the cracks, and the gaps between pebbles.

Nearly everywhere else on Earth, copper is locked inside rock, mixed with other things, and the only way to get it out is with a fire far hotter than a campfire. Here it came out pure. This is the largest store of pure copper anywhere in the world.

[Tool word: *Cu*.]

Copper has a short name, Cu. Tap *Cu*.

## The ice

Then the copper waited underground for about a thousand million years.

Ice came and went many times. The last sheet of ice covered all of this land, hills and all, and it moved. As it slid south it tore lumps of copper out of the rock and carried them along. It rolled them, scratched them, bent their edges over, and polished them until they shone.

Most lumps travelled only a few kilometres. A few were carried as far as Ohio.

The biggest lump the ice ever moved was found about twenty years ago, near an airport in Michigan. It weighed about 35 tonnes, as much as half a dozen elephants. It was cut up and melted down. Another, of 26 tonnes, still exists.

The ice let go of this land about ten thousand years ago. When it had gone, copper lay on the ground and in the beds of streams, shining.

The lake was not the shape it is now. Freed of the weight of the ice, the land was slowly rising, and for thousands of years the shores kept moving.

## The people

People came to live here. They hunted moose and caribou, trapped beaver, and fished. The forest around them was a forest nobody had ever cut.

On the far side of an ocean nobody here knew was there, other people were planting the first fields. Neither knew the other existed.

The people of the lake learned what the red stones were. In Ojibwe, copper is *miskwaabik*, red metal. Say "misk-WAH-bik."

They picked up the copper the ice had left. When that ran low, they dug for more, breaking it out of the rock with round stones from the beach. Their pits are still there. On one island in the lake there are more than a thousand of them, and some are deeper than a six-storey building is tall.

They made spear points and knives, awls for punching holes in hide, fish hooks and harpoons. The oldest of these tools that anyone has been able to date is about 8,500 years old. That is about four thousand years before the pyramids of Egypt.

## Hammer and fire

They never melted the copper. A campfire can't get anywhere near hot enough; copper melts at 1,085 degrees. They hammered it, cold, with stones.

Here is where your wire comes in.

Hammering copper does what your bending did. Each blow makes the copper a little harder and a little stiffer. Keep going and it cracks. People who work metal call this work hardening.

Copper is made of tiny crystals packed together, far too small to see. This drawing shows them much bigger than they are.

[Tool words: *Hammer*, *Heat*. The drawing appears with this paragraph.]

Tap *Hammer*.

The crystals squash. Tap it again. They stretch. Tap it again, and again.

There is a way back. Put the copper in a fire until it glows dull red, and take it out. New crystals grow, round and small, and the squashed ones are gone. The copper is soft again. This is called annealing. Say "a-NEEL-ing."

Tap *Heat*.

The people of the lake knew this. They hammered and heated, hammered and heated, and so they could draw a lump out into a long, sharp point without its cracking.

How do we know? Cut a tiny slice from an old copper tool, polish it, and look at it under a microscope. Copper that has been heated shows its crystals crossed by straight lines, like reflections in a mirror. Those lines form only when copper is heated. They turn up in old copper tools from all over North America.

[Picture: two micrographs, hammered copper and annealed copper.]

Your wire broke because it was work hardened. If you had held it in a flame until it glowed, it would have bent again.

## Afterwards

About five thousand years ago, the people around the lake began making fewer copper tools. Nobody knows exactly why. When archaeologists made copper knives and stone knives and tried them side by side, the stone ones were sharper. The copper ones lasted longer. Copper went on being made into beads and gifts, and it travelled a long way in trade.

In the 1800s, miners came and dug deep into the same rock. Much of what they dug became wire.

Copper does not wear out. Melt it and make it into something else, and it is still copper, every atom of it. Some of the copper in the walls of houses today came out of the ground by Gichigami.

## More

**The archaeologists' name** for the people of the lake is the Old Copper Complex, from the copper and nothing else. Nobody knows what the people called themselves.

**Ötzi**, a man who died in the Alps about 5,300 years ago, carried an axe of nearly pure copper. His copper had been melted and poured into a mould, something the people of the lake never did.

**Belovode**, in Serbia, is where the oldest certain copper cooked out of green rock was found, about 7,000 years old. Same metal, the opposite way of getting it.

**Mammoths** were gone from the lake long before the copper-workers came. But a small herd lived on an island off Siberia until about 4,000 years ago, so there were still mammoths alive when the pyramids were built.

**A raft of bubbles.** In 1947 two physicists, Lawrence Bragg and John Nye, blew tiny soap bubbles, all the same size, onto soapy water. The bubbles packed together in patches that looked exactly like the crystals in a metal, with crooked borders between them. Squeeze the raft and the borders move. You can make one at a sink, with an adult and an aquarium pump.

**The tool this story calls (new): Hammer / Heat.**
What's on the screen: a patch of copper drawn as a mosaic of irregular crystals (grains), a few dozen, each with faint inner lines; a caption saying it is drawn far bigger than life.
What she does: taps *Hammer* or *Heat*. Nothing else.
*Hammer*: the patch flattens a step; grains squash and lengthen; after about five taps a small crack opens at the edge and further taps widen it. No counter, no score.
*Heat*: the patch glows dull red for a moment; small round new grains appear at the old grain borders and grow until they fill the patch, some crossed by straight twin lines; the crack stays (heat doesn't mend a crack, which is true and worth keeping).
What it shows when: nothing until she taps. It resets when its text moves off only if she hasn't touched it; once touched it keeps her state (Rulings: a picture she works in keeps what she made).
Honesty: hardening is really tangles inside the grains (dislocations), too fine to draw; squashed grains are the visible sign. The caption says "drawn," and the real micrographs follow.
Shelf: the Elements lab (metals) or the Colour/materials shelf; it serves any metal story (iron, bronze, the smith).
