**More (346 words)**

Claudia Rubinstein, of a laboratory in Mendoza, in western Argentina, and her team from Argentina and Belgium dissolved pieces of rock in strong acid. The rock came from the valley of the Río Capillas, in the province of Jujuy ("hoo-HOO-ee"), and it had been laid down at the edge of the sea, where a river ran into it. What the acid could not dissolve was left at the bottom: specks far too small to see without a microscope.

Some of the specks were spores. A spore is a single cell that a plant sends out to start a new plant, and the spores of land plants have a tough outer wall. A cell that floats in water has no need of such a wall. A cell that blows through dry air would shrivel without one. So a spore with that wall is the mark of a plant that lived on land, even when it is found in rock laid down in water, where wind or rivers carried it.

In 2010 the team said that these spores, about 470 million years old, were the oldest evidence of plants on land.

The plants that made them have never been found. Spores like these are much like the spores of liverworts, small flat green plants that still grow on damp rock and soil. The oldest fossil of a whole land plant with stems, *Cooksonia* ("cook-SOH-nee-ah"), a few centimetres tall, with stems that fork, is about 432 million years old, from the Czech Republic.

In 2021 two scientists described spore-like specks about 480 million years old, from rock drilled in Western Australia in 1958. They argued that the specks were halfway between the spores of water plants and the spores of land plants, and that plants moved onto land slowly, over many millions of years. Gene clocks, which estimate when two kinds of living thing split apart by counting the differences in their genes, put the first land plants somewhere between about 515 and 474 million years ago.

The plant that made the oldest spores is still to be found.

## 2. Short, sharp ice

**Label:** Short, sharp ice

**More (337 words)**

In the Tassili n'Ajjer ("ta-SEE-lee nah-JER"), a high plateau of sandstone in the Sahara, in southeast Algeria, there are wide floors of bare rock scored with long straight grooves, all running the same way. Grooves like these are made by ice. When a glacier slides over rock, stones frozen into its base scrape the rock beneath, the way sandpaper scratches wood.

So how did ice reach the Sahara?

It didn't, quite. The Sahara wasn't there. About 445 million years ago the land that is now North Africa lay near the South Pole, part of the great southern continent of Gondwana. An ice sheet spread over it, and the ice ran out across the land toward the sea, scraping as it went. Geologists can still follow which way it flowed.

The cold did not last long, as such things go. The coldest part may have lasted only about two hundred thousand years. But it came with one of the worst dyings in the history of life. In the seas, which held nearly all the animals then, most kinds died out: about 85 kinds in every 100, by one count. It happened in two pulses, one as the ice came and one as it melted. It is the oldest of the five great dyings that geologists count.

What killed them is argued. The old answer is the cold itself: seas cooling and the water locked up as ice, so that the sea fell and the shallow seas where most animals lived dried out. In 2012 one team suggested that the first small plants on land may have pulled enough carbon dioxide from the air to start the cooling. In 2020 another team argued that the dying came from volcanoes, which warmed the seas and took the oxygen from them, not from the ice at all.

Some of the best rocks for the argument are at Dob's Linn, a steep gorge in the south of Scotland, where the line between two stretches of geological time is drawn in dark shale.

In the hills of Xiushan ("shyoh-shahn") County, in Chongqing, in southern China, there is a layer of rock that kept whole fish: heads, fins, scales and all. In 2022 a team led by Zhu Min, of the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology in Beijing, described what they had found there. One of the fish, which they named *Xiushanosteus* ("shyoh-shahn-OSS-tee-us"), is about three centimetres long, with bony plates over its head and the front of its body. And it has jaws.

A jaw is a mouth that can bite. The lower part is hinged, so that it can close hard on something and hold it. For millions of years before this, there were fish without jaws, and some still live: the lamprey, which fastens itself to other fish with a round, sucking mouth, is one.

The rock at Xiushan is about 436 million years old. Before 2022, the oldest whole fish with jaws came from Yunnan, in rock about 425 million years old, so the find moved the oldest jaws back by about eleven million years.

In the same issue of the journal, a team led by Plamen Andreev described teeth from Shiqian ("shir-chyen"), in Guizhou, about 439 million years old, a little older still. Each is a little curl of teeth no more than two and a half millimetres across, a shape known from many ancient sharks and bony fish. The rows of teeth lean left or right, as teeth do on the two sides of a jaw.

So were these the first fish with jaws?

Almost certainly not. By 436 million years ago there were already several different kinds, armoured fish and early relatives of sharks among them, and kinds take time to split apart. In Colorado, in rock about 455 million years old, there are scales that some scientists think came from jawed fish. Others say scales that old are too hard to tell apart to be sure.

The oldest jaw is still to be found.

## 4. Animals ashore

**Label:** Animals ashore
**Age:** 428 Ma · argued, between about 430 and 414 Ma · from spores in the rock (late Wenlock, Wellman 2024) or from zircons in ash below the fossil bed (413.7 ± 4.4 Ma, Suarez 2017); the argument is the event

**Summary (39 words)**

**More (328 words)**

```
About 428 million years ago
      and probably begun by 455 million years ago.
```

On the shore at Cowie, at the north end of Stonehaven, in Aberdeenshire, layers of grey rock run down into the sea. Mike Newman, a bus driver from Aberdeen who hunted fossils in his spare time, split some of that rock and found a small piece of an animal with many legs. "The legs were very clear," he said later.

In 2004 two palaeontologists described it and named it *Pneumodesmus newmani* ("NEW-mo-DEZ-mus NEW-man-eye"), after him. On its body are tiny holes, which they read as spiracles, the openings through which millipedes and insects breathe air. An animal that breathed air lived on land, so *Pneumodesmus* became the oldest known animal to live on land.

So how old is it?

That is the argument. When it was named, its age came from fossil spores in rocks nearby: about 428 million years. In 2017 a team led by Stephanie Suarez dated crystals of zircon from volcanic ash below the fossil bed and got about 414 million years, which would make *Pneumodesmus* about fourteen million years younger. Another fossil, from the island of Kerrera, near Oban, dated by the same group at about 425 million years, would then be the oldest millipede known.

In 2024 a team led by Charles Wellman dated the rock again from its spores, looked again at zircon ages from other studies, and argued for the older age. Two of the 2017 team replied that the new samples came from a separate block of rock, cut off from the fossil bed by a fault. Nobody has settled it.

Older tracks exist. In the Lake District, in rock about 455 million years old, there are trackways of many-legged animals on volcanic ash. Most were made under water, but one trackway crosses a few centimetres of ripple tops that stood out of the water, as if the animal made a short walk on dry ground.

The first animal to live on land, whatever it was, has not been found.

Culver Cliff is a lone cliff of sandstone about thirty metres tall, a kilometre northwest of Minehead, on the coast of Somerset. On one surface of its rock lie at least seventeen fossil tree trunks, five to ten centimetres thick and nearly a metre and a half long. West along the coast, near Porlock Weir, there are six large hollows in the rock, each up to thirty centimetres across and filled with mud. They are where standing trees rotted away.

Geologists had not expected to find important fossil plants in these rocks. In 2024 Neil Davies, of the University of Cambridge, and Christopher Berry and William McMahon described the trunks and hollows and said they were the oldest fossil forest known. "When I first saw pictures of the tree trunks I immediately knew what they were," Berry said, "based on 30 years of studying this type of tree worldwide."

The trees are called *Calamophyton* ("KAL-am-oh-FY-ton"). The largest were two to four metres tall, with hollow trunks, and they had no leaves at all, only hundreds of twiglike branches, which they shed as they grew. There was no grass yet, and almost nothing grew beneath them, but the twigs piled up on the ground. Davies and his colleagues think the trees and their fallen twigs changed the way rivers ran, and helped hold the ground in place.

The rock was laid down by rivers, and the age comes from fossil spores in it: about 390 million years.

Before 2024, the oldest forest known was in an old quarry near Cairo, in New York State, about 386 million years old. Its trees are more like ours. Some belonged to *Archaeopteris* ("ar-kee-OP-ter-iss"), a tree with wood and leaves, whose roots reached out about eleven metres. Nearby, at Gilboa, a flood in 1869 uncovered fossil stumps standing where they grew.

Some of the cliffs along this coast of Somerset can be reached only by boat.

On Ellesmere Island, in Nunavut, the far north of Canada, a team led by Edward Daeschler, Neil Shubin and Farish Jenkins went looking for a particular kind of fossil. They wanted fish that lived just before animals with legs, and they knew about how old that rock should be, and what kind of rock it should be. Ellesmere Island had rock of that age and kind, bare of soil and trees. They began searching in 1999.

In the summer of 2004, Stephen Gatesy, one of the team, noticed the tip of a flat, pointed snout in the rock.

The animal it belonged to is a fish, with scales and gills. Inside each front fin there are bones that match the bones of an arm: an upper arm, a forearm, and parts of a wrist that could bend. The fin still ended in fin rays, not fingers.

So was *Tiktaalik* the ancestor of animals with legs?

Probably not, as it turned out. The fossils of *Tiktaalik* are kept at the Canadian Museum of Nature, in Ottawa, until a place for them is built in Nunavut.

At Joggins, on the Bay of Fundy, in Nova Scotia, the cliffs are layers of rock from an old swamp forest, and some of the fossil trees in them still stand upright, as they grew. They were not trees like ours. Their trunks had a soft inside, and when a tree died the inside rotted first, leaving a hollow stump standing in the swamp.

Small animals got into these stumps, perhaps by falling in, perhaps to live there, and their bones were buried inside. In 1852 the geologists Charles Lyell and William Dawson found bones inside one of them. A few years later Dawson found more bones, in other stumps, from a different animal, about twenty to twenty-five centimetres long, like a small lizard. He named it *Hylonomus* ("hy-LON-oh-mus"), which means "forest dweller."

*Hylonomus* has long been called the oldest reptile yet found, though a study in 2025 argues that it was a close cousin of the reptiles instead. It matters because of the eggs reptiles lay.

A frog's eggs are soft jelly, laid in water, and on dry land they would dry out. Reptiles, birds and mammals belong to a great group of animals whose eggs carry their own water. Inside, a thin skin wraps the growing young in its own small pond, so the egg can be laid on dry land. *Hylonomus*'s bones put it in that group, or very close to it.

But nobody has found its eggs. The oldest fossil eggs of this group yet found are dinosaur eggs about 195 million years old, more than a hundred million years younger. So the egg of *Hylonomus* is worked out from its skeleton, not seen, and whether the first such eggs had hard shells or soft, leathery ones is argued.

The group is older than *Hylonomus*. In 2025 a team described footprints with claw marks in rock about 355 million years old in Victoria, Australia, and argued that animals of the egg-laying kind made them.

At Joggins, the tides of the Bay of Fundy keep wearing the cliffs back, and new fossil trees come into view.

In two coal mines near Danville, in Illinois, geologists from the Illinois State Geological Survey, among them John Nelson and Scott Elrick, looked up at the roof of the tunnels. The miners had taken out a seam of coal, and the rock left above it was full of fossil trees: stumps, fallen trunks, and leaves pressed flat in the stone. Mapped from tunnel to tunnel, the forest on the roof covered about a thousand hectares.

The coal and the forest were the same place. The coal is what the forest's swamp became.

When plants die on dry ground, they rot away. In a swamp they fall into still water with little oxygen in it, and they rot only partly, piling up as a thick brown mat called peat. Buried deep under later layers, squeezed and warmed for millions of years, peat turns into coal. Here a fault in the earth dropped the swamp, the sea came in, and mud buried the forest so quickly that the trees were kept on top of their own coal.

Between about 323 and 299 million years ago, swamps like this one covered great stretches of land near the equator, and they made more coal than almost any other stretch of time.

So why so much?

In 2012 a team that compared the genes of fungi suggested an answer. Wood is tough because of a substance called lignin, and the fungi that can break lignin down may not have evolved until the end of this stretch. Wood, they argued, was harder to rot.

In 2016 another team argued back. Many of the coal-swamp trees had little lignin, and coal kept forming wherever two things came together: a wet climate near the equator, and land that was slowly sinking, so that peat could pile up for a long time without drying out. "To form coal you need two basic conditions," one of them, Kevin Boyce, said: "wet tropics and a hole to bury organic matter in for a long period of time."

**Line:** earth

At Meishan ("may-shahn"), in Changxing ("chahng-shing") County, in Zhejiang, in eastern China, the walls of limestone quarries show layers of rock and clay, one on top of another. Geologists have numbered the layers, and in them are the fossils of hundreds of kinds of sea animal: shellfish, corals, and tiny animals called foraminifera ("for-AM-in-IF-er-a"), each living in a shell, most no bigger than a grain of sand.

At one thin layer of white clay, numbered 25, the fossils begin to vanish, and a few layers higher most of them are gone.

Between the layers are beds of volcanic ash, and the ash holds crystals of zircon, which can be dated. In 2014 Seth Burgess, Samuel Bowring and Shen Shuzhong dated them so closely that they could say when the dying began, about 251.94 million years ago, and how long it took: about sixty thousand years, give or take nearly fifty thousand. In the life of the earth that is very fast.

How many kinds died? For years the figure was nearly 90 or even 96 kinds of sea animal in every 100. In 2016 a new count put it at about 81. Plants and animals on land suffered badly too. It is the most severe dying known.

What caused it is now argued less than it was. At the same time, in what is now Siberia, volcanoes poured out lava, layer on layer, over millions of square kilometres, for close to a million years. Melted rock also spread underground in sheets, and many think its heat baked buried rock that gave off huge amounts of carbon dioxide and other gases. The world warmed. One team, with a computer model of the oceans, worked out that the tropical seas warmed by about eleven degrees Celsius and lost much of their oxygen, and that heat and too little oxygen together could explain more than half of the dying, and where it was worst.

Geologists everywhere now draw the line between two great stretches of the earth's time at one point in the rock at Meishan, just above layer 25.

Ischigualasto ("ees-chee-gwah-LAHS-toh"), in the province of San Juan, in western Argentina, is a valley of bare, worn hills where little grows but bushes and cactus. People there call it the Valle de la Luna, the Valley of the Moon. Its rocks were laid down by rivers about 231 million years ago, and they hold some of the oldest dinosaurs on earth.

In 1991 Ricardo Martínez, of the National University of San Juan, was in the valley with a team that included Paul Sereno, from Chicago, when he found a skeleton almost whole. It was a little over a metre long, and it walked on its back legs. Sereno named it *Eoraptor* ("EE-oh-RAP-tor"), "dawn plunderer."

Not every early dinosaur was small. In 1959 Victorino Herrera, a goatherd, noticed bones in the same rocks, and they turned out to belong to *Herrerasaurus*, a hunter that could grow to about six metres.

But dinosaurs were few in the valley. Most of the fossils there are of other animals: rhynchosaurs ("RING-ko-sores"), reptiles with beaks that ate plants, and cynodonts ("SY-no-donts"), relatives of mammals.

Near the city of Santa Maria, in the south of Brazil, dinosaurs have been found in rock about 233 million years old, older than Ischigualasto's. And in Zimbabwe, in 2022, a team described *Mbiresaurus* ("um-BEER-eh-SORE-us"), from rock about 230 million years old.

How did so few become so many? Some scientists argue that a long spell of heavy rain, which began about 234 million years ago and lasted a million years or more, changed the plants and the climate, and that dinosaurs spread in the world that came after.

And how much older are dinosaurs than the oldest found? *Nyasasaurus*, from Tanzania, was once put at 243 million years and called perhaps the oldest dinosaur. Since then some have argued that its rock is younger, and some doubt that it was a dinosaur at all.

## 11. Pangaea cracks

Along the west bank of the Hudson River, across from Manhattan, a wall of dark rock rises from the water, in places more than a hundred metres high. It is called the Palisades. A palisade is a fence of tall stakes, and the rock stands in tall columns, like one.

The rock of the Palisades was once melted. It pushed its way up between layers of older rock, spread out underground as a sheet about three hundred metres thick, and cooled there slowly. Melted rock from the same source, many geologists think, reached the surface nearby and poured out as lava.

At that time there was no Atlantic Ocean. Nearly all the land on earth was joined in one continent, which geologists call Pangaea ("pan-JEE-ah"), and North America, Africa and South America lay side by side. Then the land between them began to stretch and crack, and melted rock came up through the cracks.

It came up in a great many places at once. Rock of the same kind and the same age is found in Nova Scotia, in Morocco, in Brazil, in Portugal, and in West Africa, now on four continents and on both sides of an ocean. Geologists call it all one province, and it spread over perhaps eleven million square kilometres, wider than any other such outpouring known.

In 2013 a team led by Terrence Blackburn dated crystals of zircon from these rocks. The earliest lava came about 201.6 million years ago, in four pulses over about six hundred thousand years, and the first pulse came at the same time as one of the five great dyings, in the sea and on land.

The cracks went on widening, and some time later, perhaps about 190 million years ago, new ocean floor began to form between them. That floor is the floor of the Atlantic, and it is still being made, down its middle, today.

Nearly all ocean floor older than about 200 million years has since sunk back into the earth. So from here on, the floor of the sea helps geologists draw the map.
