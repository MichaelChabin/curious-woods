## 1. Anaxagoras

**Year:** about −450 · precision: decade · kind: person

**More (350 words)**

Around 467 BCE, a stone fell out of the sky at a place called Aegospotami ("ee-gos-POT-uh-my"), on the narrow strait between Europe and Asia that is now called the Dardanelles. Plutarch, a Greek writer who lived more than five hundred years later, says people believed the stone was huge, and that it was still being shown in his own day.

Stones were not supposed to be up there. But a man named Anaxagoras ("an-ak-SAG-er-us") thought they were. He came from Clazomenae, a Greek city on the coast of what is now Turkey, and he moved to Athens and taught there for many years. Nobody is sure when he arrived: scholars' guesses run from about 480 to about 456 BCE.

To many Greeks the Sun was a god. Anaxagoras said it was a mass of red-hot stone, or perhaps red-hot metal, larger than the Peloponnese ("PEL-uh-puh-neez"), the great peninsula that makes up the south of Greece. He said the Moon was made of earth, with hills and valleys, and that it had no light of its own: it shone with light from the Sun.

That one idea explains eclipses. When the Moon passes between us and the Sun, it blocks the Sun. When the Earth comes between the Sun and the Moon, the Moon goes dark. Anaxagoras is the first Greek known to have explained eclipses this way. And if the Sun was a stone, then a stone could fall out of the sky.

Athens grew nervous. Plutarch says a law was proposed against anyone who taught about the heavens and did not believe in the gods, and that Pericles, the leading man of the city and a friend of Anaxagoras, sent the teacher away to keep him safe. Other writers, centuries later, tell four different stories of a trial. Nobody knows which, if any, is true.

Anaxagoras spent his last years at Lampsacus, across the strait from where the stone had fallen. When he was dying, the town asked how it should honour him. He asked that every year, in the month he died, the children be given a holiday.

At a festival in Athens in 423 BCE, the audience watched an actor playing a real, living man, hung up in the air in a basket. The man was Socrates ("SOK-ruh-teez"), and the play was *Clouds*, a comedy by Aristophanes ("air-is-TOF-uh-neez"). It showed Socrates as a crank who gazed at the sky and taught young men tricks for winning arguments they ought to lose. The play came third in the contest.

The real Socrates spent his days in the streets and the marketplace of Athens, asking people questions. What is courage? What is justice? What is a good life? He kept asking until it was plain that the person who had been so sure did not know the answer, and often that Socrates did not know either. He was famously ugly, with a flat, turned-up nose and bulging eyes, and a friend compared him to a satyr, a wild creature from Greek stories who was part man and part horse.

He wrote nothing. Almost everything we know about him comes from three people who knew him, and they disagree. Aristophanes made fun of him. Xenophon ("ZEN-uh-fun"), a soldier, remembered a sensible man full of practical advice. Plato, his pupil, wrote conversations in which Socrates questions people, but Plato often seems to have put his own ideas into his teacher's mouth.

Which is the real Socrates? Nobody can be sure.

In 399 BCE, when he was about seventy, three citizens accused him of not honouring the gods of the city, of bringing in new gods, and of corrupting the young. A jury of about five hundred citizens heard the case. In Plato's account, Socrates says that if thirty votes had gone the other way, he would have been set free. He was sentenced to death, and some weeks later, in prison, he drank a poison. Plato never names the poison; later writers call it hemlock.

Plato describes the death in detail: Socrates calm, talking with his friends to the end. But the friend who tells the story in Plato's book says that Plato was not there that day. He was ill.

## 3. Paper

**Label:** Paper
**Year:** 105 · precision: year · kind: craft or invention

In 1986, near the city of Tianshui in northwest China, archaeologists opened a tomb more than two thousand years old. Inside lay a scrap of paper about the size of two postage stamps, with fine black ink lines that seem to show mountains, rivers and roads. It is probably part of a map. The tomb was made between about 179 and 141 BCE.

That is a puzzle, because the history books say paper was invented more than two hundred years later.

Their source is the *Book of the Later Han*, a history put together in the 400s CE. It says that in 105 CE Cai Lun ("tsy LWUN"), an official at the emperor's court, showed the emperor a new writing material made from tree bark, the ends of hemp plants, worn-out cloth and old fishing nets, and that afterwards people everywhere called it Lord Cai's paper.

Read the history closely, though, and it does not say Cai Lun was the first. It says that books had mostly been written on strips of bamboo, which were heavy, or on silk, which was expensive, and that the silk was called *zhi* ("jr"), the same word that was later used for paper. Cai Lun had the idea of making *zhi* from cheap materials.

Paper is made by beating plant fibres in water until they come apart into a soupy pulp. The pulp is spread thin on a fine screen and the water drains away, leaving the fibres tangled into a thin mat, which is pressed and dried into a sheet.

The older paper keeps turning up. At Xuanquan ("shwen-chwen"), a Han postal station near Dunhuang, archaeologists found more than four hundred and sixty pieces of paper, about ten with writing, and seven of those seem to be older than 105 CE. Some historians of paper are not convinced these dates are right.

So did Cai Lun invent paper? Perhaps he found a better, cheaper way to make it. Perhaps he simply brought it to court, so his name went into the history book.

Who made the first sheet, and when, nobody yet knows.

## 4. Teotihuacan

**Label:** Teotihuacan
**Year:** about 400 · precision: century · kind: place
**Place:** Teotihuacan, State of Mexico, Mexico (19.69 N, 98.84 W)

**Summary (36 words)**
From about 200 to 550 CE, Teotihuacan, in the Valley of Mexico, was one of the largest cities in the Americas, with perhaps 100,000 people. Nobody knows what they called it, or what language they spoke. *More*

**More (330 words)**

```
About 10,400 years after the ice

            or about 1,625 years ago.
```

A wide, straight avenue runs for kilometres through the ruins of an old city in the Valley of Mexico, less than fifty kilometres northeast of Mexico City. Beside it stands a pyramid sixty-six metres high, as tall as a twenty-storey building, on a base more than two hundred metres along each side.

Every one of those things has a name that someone else gave it. The avenue is the Avenue of the Dead, the pyramid is the Pyramid of the Sun, and the city is Teotihuacan ("tay-oh-tee-wah-KAHN"). All three names are in Nahuatl ("NAH-wah-tl"), the language of the Aztecs, who came to the ruins hundreds of years after the city had emptied. Teotihuacan is usually translated as "the place where the gods were made," though translators disagree.

The city was at its biggest between about 200 and 550 CE. Archaeologists working out how many people lived there start from the large walled apartment buildings, made of stone, where families lived together. In 2019 the archaeologist Michael E. Smith and his colleagues worked from the sizes of those buildings and arrived at about 100,000 people. Older guesses were larger, up to 200,000 in one well-known estimate.

What did those people call their city? What language did they speak? Who ruled them? Nobody knows. They painted signs on walls and pots, but only short groups of signs, never long texts, and nobody can read them for certain. Linguists have suggested several languages. In 2025, two of them, Magnus Pharao Hansen and Christophe Helmke, argued that the signs record an early language from which Nahuatl later grew. That idea is new, and it is too soon to know whether other scholars agree.

About 550 CE, the great buildings along the avenue were burned. Nobody knows who set the fires, whether people of the city or enemies from outside, or why.

The Aztecs believed that the gods had met at Teotihuacan to make the Sun. What the people who built it believed, nobody knows.

## 5. Zero

**Label:** Zero
**Year:** 628 · precision: year · kind: text
**Place:** Bhinmal (ancient Bhillamala), Rajasthan, India (25.00 N, 72.27 E)
**Replaces:** `zero`

**Summary (36 words)**
628 CE. In northwest India, an astronomer named Brahmagupta wrote rules for calculating with nothing, treating it as a number you could add, subtract and multiply. Dividing a number by it, he left as a puzzle. *More*

**More (349 words)**

```
10,628 years after the ice
      or 628 CE
            or 1,398 years ago.
```

Take a debt of five and add nothing to it, and you still owe five. Take nothing away from nothing, and you have nothing. Multiply any number by nothing, and the answer is nothing.

Rules like these seem too plain to need writing down, but someone had to write them down first. In 628 an astronomer named Brahmagupta ("BRAH-muh-GOOP-tuh") did. He was thirty, he tells us, and he lived at Bhillamala, now the town of Bhinmal ("BHEEN-mahl"), in the northwest of India.

Most of his book is about the Sun, Moon and planets, but one chapter sets out rules for calculating, and in it he treats nothing as a number like any other. He gives rules, too, for two other kinds of number, which he calls fortunes and debts, and which we call positive and negative numbers. A debt taken away from nothing becomes a fortune. Two debts multiplied together make a fortune.

People had used a mark for an empty place long before him. By about 400 BCE, Babylonian scribes, in what is now Iraq, put a sign into a number where a place was empty, the way the 0 in 305 shows that there are no tens.

Then he came to dividing. Nothing divided by nothing, he said, is nothing. And a number divided by nothing? He said only that it makes a fraction with nothing underneath, and not what that fraction is worth.

Try it. Twelve divided by three asks how many threes make twelve, and the answer is four. Twelve divided by nothing asks how many nothings make twelve. However many nothings you pile up, you never get there.

Later mathematicians in India tried other answers. Mahavira, in the 800s, said that a number divided by nothing stays as it was. Bhaskara, in the 1100s, said the answer is a quantity without end. Today mathematicians say that dividing by zero cannot be done at all.

The oldest dated zero in India that everyone agrees is genuine is carved on a stone at Gwalior, in 876: a small round 0, in the numbers 270 and 50.

What square, when ten of its roots are added to it, makes thirty-nine?

That is one of the problems in a book written in Baghdad, in what is now Iraq, about twelve hundred years ago. A square is a number multiplied by itself, and its root is the number you started with: 9 is the square of 3, and 3 is the root of 9. So the problem asks for a number which, multiplied by itself and added to ten times itself, makes 39. The book's answer is the square 9, whose root is 3, because nine and ten threes make thirty-nine.

There is not a single symbol in that problem, or in the whole book. Everything is written in words, even the numbers. Its author was Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi ("al-KHWAH-riz-mee"), and he wrote it for al-Ma'mun ("al-mah-MOON"), the caliph, or ruler, in Baghdad from 813 to 833. Nobody knows the exact year.

The title promises calculation by *al-jabr* and *al-muqabala*. *Al-jabr* ("al-JAB-r") means restoring. If one side of a problem has something taken away, you add it back to both sides, and the problem stays true: "a number less four is ten" becomes "the number is fourteen." *Al-muqabala* ("al-moo-KAH-buh-luh") means balancing. If the same thing appears on both sides, you take it away from both.

Al-Khwarizmi says he wrote the book for the reckoning people need every day: dividing what someone left when they died among their family, trade, measuring land, digging canals. The longest part is about inheritances.

When the book was put into Latin in the 1100s, *al-jabr* became *algebra*.

He wrote a second book, on how to calculate with the figures used in India, the ancestors of 1, 2, 3 and 0. In Latin his name was spelled Algoritmi or Algorizmi, and calculating with those figures came to be called algorism. Over the centuries the word bent into algorithm, which now means a list of steps that solves a problem.

The Arabic original of that second book is lost. It survives only in Latin, and one of only two copies is in Cambridge, in England.

In the 1930s, someone picked up a stone adze, a cutting tool like an axe, on a small coral island in the Tuamotus, in the middle of the South Pacific. Someone had carried that stone south. People did not only reach Hawaiʻi; they sailed away from it, and back.

The islands that Hawaiʻi's first people probably came from, the Marquesas and the Society Islands, lie about 3,500 and 4,100 kilometres from the island of Hawaiʻi, across open ocean. The navigators had no compass and no charts. They held the course in their heads. Near the end of a voyage, birds and clouds helped: a white tern fishes up to about two hundred kilometres out to sea and flies home to land at night, and clouds pile up over islands.

Could it really be done that way? In 1976, a double-hulled voyaging canoe called Hōkūleʻa ("hoh-KOO-leh-ah") sailed from Maui to Tahiti in thirty-four days with no instruments at all. About seventeen thousand people came to meet the canoe in Tahiti.

A study in 2014 said between about 940 and 1130. Another, in 2011, said that people reached the island of Hawaiʻi itself between about 1220 and 1260.

Nobody knows yet whose answer is right. The stone from Kahoʻolawe has no date on it at all.

Set several candles in a row outside a dark room that has a small hole in its wall. Inside, on the wall facing the hole, a spot of light appears for each candle, but in the opposite order: the candle on the left makes the spot on the right. Cover one candle, and only its spot goes out.

Ibn al-Haytham ("IB-un al-HAY-tham") described this in his *Book of Optics*, which he wrote in Cairo, in Egypt, probably in the 1020s or 1030s. He had been born about 965 in Basra, in what is now Iraq.

The candles show that light travels in straight lines, and that the light from each candle passes through the hole without mixing with the light from the others. The lines cross at the hole, which is why the order turns around.

For about fourteen hundred years before him, since the time of the ancient Greeks, people had argued about how seeing works. Some, like the Greek mathematicians Euclid and Ptolemy, thought that the eye sends out rays that touch what we see. Others, like the philosopher Aristotle, thought that the thing we see acts on the eye through the air between. Ibn al-Haytham took the second side, and gave reasons anyone can check. A very bright light hurts the eyes. And after looking at something bright, you can still see it for a moment once you look away. Something has come in.

He did not trust a claim just because a famous name was attached to it. In another book, a list of his doubts about Ptolemy, he wrote that a person who studies the writings of scientists, if learning the truth is the goal, should

> make himself an enemy of all that he reads.

*Ibn al-Haytham, Aporias against Ptolemy, translated by A. I. Sabra*

His *Book of Optics* filled seven books. Around 1200 it was put into Latin, and in Latin his name became Alhazen. In 1572 it was printed, and Johannes Kepler, a German astronomer who worked out how the eye makes its picture, read it there.

Rub a needle on a lodestone, a kind of rock that is naturally magnetic, and the needle becomes a magnet too. Hang it so that it can turn freely, and one end swings round to point south, and the other north.

Shen Kuo ("shun KWAW") described this in a book of notes he wrote near the end of his life, at a garden he called Dream Brook, in eastern China. He had served the Song emperors as an astronomer, an envoy and a military commander, and he retired about 1088.

He wrote that people who practised the magical arts made needles point south this way. He described four ways of holding one. Floating on water, the needle wobbled. Balanced on a fingernail or on the rim of a bowl, it turned easily, but it kept slipping off. Best of all was to hang it from a single strand of silk, stuck on with a tiny bit of wax.

He also noted something odd. The needle did not point due south. It always pointed a little to the east of south.

Why? The Earth itself acts like a giant magnet, and a compass needle lines up with it. But the Earth's magnetic poles are not at the North and South Poles, the two points it spins around, and they slowly wander. So in most places a compass points a little to one side of true north and south. Shen Kuo did not know why. He recorded it.

He was not the first to make iron point south: a military handbook of about 1044 describes an iron fish, made magnetic by heating, that floats and points south. Nor was he writing about ships. Sailors appear soon after: a book of about 1115, describing Guangzhou in the 1090s, says pilots there watched the needle in dark weather. In Europe, a compass is first mentioned about 1190. Whether the idea travelled there from China, or was found again, nobody knows.

The difference Shen Kuo noticed is still printed on many walking maps, so that people with a compass can allow for it.

On the night of 10 June 1194, a fire burned most of the cathedral. The Belle Verrière came through it, and so did three tall windows over the west doors, made some decades earlier. The cathedral was rebuilt, and about 1225 glaziers set the old picture of Mary into a new window, with new glass all around her.

What makes the blue? A tiny amount of a metal called cobalt, melted into the glass. In medieval blue glass from Reims, another French cathedral, cobalt makes up only about one or two parts in a thousand.

Nobody recognised cobalt as a metal of its own until about 1735, when a Swedish chemist, Georg Brandt, did. Its name comes from *Kobold*, a German word for a goblin.

So where did the glaziers get it?

Nobody is sure. Some of it may not have been new at all. A monk who called himself Theophilus, who wrote down how windows were made, early in the 1100s, says that the French were especially good at collecting pieces of old blue glass from ancient ruins and melting them down for windows. When chemists analyse medieval blue glass, they sometimes find the chemical traces of the cobalt used in Roman times, many centuries earlier, which fits what he says. From the late 1100s, a new kind of cobalt appears in the glass, perhaps from mines in Europe, perhaps from farther away.

So some of the blue in a twelfth-century window may be Roman glass, melted and used again. Whether any of the oldest blue at Chartres is, nobody yet knows.

The Belle Verrière still hangs in the cathedral, with the glass from before the fire in the middle and the glass from after it all around.

A book written in Italy in 1202 begins by setting out nine figures, 9 8 7 6 5 4 3 2 1, which it calls the figures of the Indians. Then it adds a tenth sign, 0, which it says the Arabs call *zephirum*. With these ten signs, it says, any number at all can be written.

The book is *Liber Abaci* ("LEE-ber AH-bah-chee"), the Book of Calculation, and its author was a man from Pisa, in Italy, who called himself Leonardo of Pisa. The name we know him by, Fibonacci ("fee-boh-NAH-chee"), comes from the Latin *filius Bonacci*, which may mean "son of Bonacci" or "of the Bonacci family," and it was given to him long after he died.

Leonardo learned the figures as a boy in Bugia, now Béjaïa, in Algeria, a port on the coast of North Africa, where his father worked for the merchants of Pisa.

What made them better than Roman numerals? In the Indian figures, a figure's place tells you what it is worth. In 305, the 3 means three hundreds, the 0 holds the tens' place empty, and the 5 means five ones. So a sum can be done on paper, column by column. Roman numerals don't work that way. Try multiplying XXIV by XIII without first turning them into ordinary numbers.

*Liber Abaci* is full of problems a merchant would meet: changing money, weights and measures, sharing profits among partners. One puzzle asks how many pairs of rabbits a single pair would produce in a year, and its answers make a list of numbers, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, each the sum of the two before, that is now named after him, though it was known in India long before.

No copy of 1202 survives; we read the version Leonardo revised in 1228.

The new figures spread slowly. In 1299, almost a hundred years after the book, the money-changers of Florence told their members to write the sums in their account books openly and in full, in letters, not in the figures of the abacus. Nobody knows why.

In 1939, near the mouth of the Wairau River, at the top of Te Waipounamu ("teh wai-poh-NAH-moo"), the South Island of New Zealand, a boy named Jim Eyles found a moa egg. A moa was a huge bird that could not fly; the tallest stood higher than a person.

The place is a long strip of shingle called Te Pokohiwi ("teh poh-koh-HEE-wee"), the Wairau Bar. Digging there over the next twenty-odd years uncovered the graves of forty-four people and more than three thousand things they had made or used, among them a necklace of more than a hundred dolphin teeth. Te Pokohiwi is one of the oldest places where people are known to have lived in Aotearoa ("ah-oh-teh-ah-ROH-ah").

Some of the people buried there may have been born far away. In 2013 scientists studied chemical traces in their bones, which record what a person ate in the years before death, and in their teeth, which record where a person grew up. One group had eaten alike and grown up in similar places; the scientists suggested they were perhaps among the first arrivals.

When did people first arrive? The kiore, the Pacific rat, could only have reached Aotearoa in a canoe, with people, so the oldest rats date the first people. Some scientists once thought that was two thousand years ago. In 2008 Janet Wilmshurst and her colleagues dated rat bones again, along with seeds that rats had gnawed. Nothing was older than about 1280.

There is a second clock. About 1314, the volcano Tarawera erupted and spread a layer of ash across the North Island. The first signs of people clearing forest lie just below that ash.

So people reached Aotearoa a little before 1300, probably from islands far to the northeast. Māori tradition calls the homeland Hawaiki ("hah-WAI-kee"). It was one of the last large lands on Earth that people found.

In 2009, after up to seventy years in a museum, the people of Te Pokohiwi were brought home. Elders of Rangitāne o Wairau ("rah-ngee-TAH-neh oh WAI-rau") buried them again where they had been found.

In March 1455, a churchman named Enea Silvio Piccolomini ("pee-koh-LOH-mee-nee"), who later became pope, wrote to a cardinal about something he had seen at Frankfurt, in Germany. It was a Bible, or parts of one: loose bundles of pages, printed, not written by hand. He had been told, he said, that 158 copies were finished, though others said 180.

The Bible had been printed in Mainz, not far from Frankfurt, by Johannes Gutenberg ("GOO-ten-bairk"). He was born about 1400, though nobody knows the year.

Printing was not new. In China, books had been printed from carved wooden blocks for hundreds of years, and in the 1040s a craftsman named Bi Sheng made letters of baked clay that could be moved and used again. In Korea, in 1377, a book was printed with movable letters made of metal, and one volume of it still survives in Paris.

What Gutenberg did was to cast letters in metal, many copies of each, so quickly and so exactly that a whole page could be built from thousands of small letters, inked, pressed onto paper or vellum with a screw press, and then taken apart so the letters could be used for the next page. He made an ink with oil in it, which would stick to metal.

The work cost a fortune. A businessman named Johann Fust lent Gutenberg 800 guilders, and then another 800. In 1455 Fust took him to court to get the money back with interest, and in November the court gave Fust the workshop where the Bible had been printed. So in the very year the Bible appeared, Gutenberg seems to have lost much of what he had built.

The idea spread anyway. By 1500 there were printers all over Europe. Two economists who tried to count reckon they had made about thirteen million books, more than had been copied by hand in western Europe in all the centuries before. Others put the total nearer ten million. All are estimates.

Of the roughly 180 copies of Gutenberg's Bible, about forty-nine survive. Gutenberg's name is not printed in any of them.
