# The Elements Lab

**Pillar:** Labs. **Intuitions served:** #3 (the same thing in several skins: name, symbol, the metal in your hand, its place in four different orderings), #5 (order of magnitude: weights, temperatures and ages across many powers of ten on common rulers), #13 (every element has a discovery date and a birth in some star), #16 (Davy, Scheele, Berzelius, the Curies), and it underwrites the boundary conditions in Laws-Macroscopic.md: mass and charge are conserved, and elements are what you count when you check.

## What it is

One canonical entry per element, so lead is the same lead in every window, pipe and Roman story. Any story that touches an element calls its tile; the lab is where the tile lives. A child can also walk in with no story behind her and play. She sees every tile, guests included (ruled 24 Oct: labs show everything; the Time Machine will too).

**Ruled 24 Oct: this lab is the reference.** For any element, and for the common mixtures too (bronze, brass, steel, solder, pewter, and glass itself), because there has to be one obvious place to get a solid answer. Glass Geometry is geometry whose areas can carry colour and weight; when it needs to know what lead weighs or what colour cobalt gives, it looks it up here. The earlier question of whether the six metals "belong" to Glass is closed: their entries live here, and Glass links to them.

**Ruled 24 Oct: the front door is the Time Machine's.** A short explanation of what a lab is and what this one does, and a Next button. No single introductory story for everyone; the lab fuels stories and any of them can call it. Eileen's talk (below) is a story that uses the lab, not its doorway.

## The tiles

**The core seventeen.** What the world is made of.

- Hydrogen, helium: nearly the whole universe; the Sun burns one into the other.
- Carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, sulfur: with hydrogen, these are life.
- Silicon, aluminium, iron, magnesium, calcium: with oxygen, these are rock.
- Sodium, chlorine, potassium: salt, the sea, and the signal in a nerve.
- Uranium, nickel: the deep-time clock and the core. (Michael, 23 Oct: deep time matters, so uranium is in; the geophysics variant of the Time Machine touches zircons and the nickel-iron core.)

Between them, about 99 percent of the crust, the ocean, the air and the child.

**The second shelf, the six metals.** What people made things from before anyone knew what an element was: copper, tin, lead, gold, silver, zinc. Their symbols are Latin because they are older than chemistry. They are also the stained glass windows: lead came, tin in the solder, copper foil, zinc came, and the colours in the glass (cobalt, copper, gold, manganese, silver).

**Mixtures.** A short shelf of the alloys and compounds stories keep meeting: bronze (copper and tin), brass (copper and zinc), steel (iron and a little carbon), solder (tin and lead), glass (silicon and oxygen, with potassium or sodium to make it melt). A mixture's entry is shorter than an element's: what it is made of, in what proportion, why anyone bothered, and the stories that met it.

**Guests.** The lab grows only because a story met something. Zirconium arrives with the zircon clock, helium's story is a line in the Sun's spectrum, radium comes from the Curies' shed, argon from Rayleigh's too-heavy nitrogen. A guest tile carries a small mark saying which story brought it.

## The sorts

The tiles never change; only where they stand. The child picks a sort and watches the tiles move. The animation is the lab, not a decoration on it.

- **How much of it is here**: by abundance on Earth. Oxygen and silicon stride to the front; gold hides.
- **When it was made**: hydrogen and helium alone at the start; then everything up to iron, from ordinary stars; then the heavy ones, from explosions and collisions.
- **What kind of star it took**: see *Birth* below.
- **How it behaves**: the tiles fall into Mendeleev's rows and columns. The periodic table turns out to be one ordering among several.
- **When people found it**: puts the lab on the Time Machine.

## The entry

Every tile opens to the same shape, so no two entries can disagree about what matters. The record is specified in Schema-Elements.md, with carbon worked.

1. **How much.** In the universe, in the Earth, in you. Three bars. The universe bar is measured at the Sun's surface, the best sample there is; the lab says so once, where the bars are explained.
2. **Birth.** Which furnace made it, and roughly what it took (below).
3. **Who first held it as itself, and when.** Aluminium: the most common metal in the crust, and in the 1850s priced like silver.
4. **Its rulers.** The common comparisons, below.
5. **Its spectrum.** The lines it shows when it glows.
6. **Stories that met it.**

## Common rulers

Michael's aim (24 Oct): an ocean of facts becomes usable if every element is measured with the same few rulers, so the child compares rather than memorises.

**Weight: the 10 cm cube.** One litre of each. Water is a kilogram, so everything is "times water." Hydrogen gas, a tenth of a gram. Lithium floats. Aluminium about 2.7 kg, iron about 7.9, lead about 11, gold about 19, osmium (if it ever comes as a guest) about 22, the heaviest thing there is. The cube is the Glass unit made solid.

**Temperature: one ruler, three readings.** The range is enormous and a child has no feel for any of it past a kettle. Three ways to read the same ruler:

- *How fast its atoms move as a gas.* Air at room temperature: about 500 metres a second, faster than sound, because sound is those atoms passing the word along. Hotter, faster; heavier, slower, for the same temperature. This makes temperature a speed, which is a thing a child can picture.
- *What colour it glows.* Anything hot enough glows, and the colour depends only on the temperature, not the stuff: dull red near 800 K, orange, yellow, white at the Sun's 5800 K, blue-white beyond. A potter judges a kiln through the peephole by this. **This is the same physics as the spectrum**, so the two sections of the entry are one idea seen twice.
- *The open door.* Michael's proposal: a perfectly insulated chamber at that temperature, a 10 cm square door opened, and what you would feel at a given distance. It is a kiln's peephole, and the heat coming through goes as the fourth power of the temperature, so it climbs ferociously. Below body temperature you feel cold (heat leaves you for the door). Around a candle or a kiln it is the familiar face-on-a-stove feeling. At the Sun's surface temperature a 10 cm door is unbearable across a room. For the Sun's core (about 15.7 million K) the door lets out about 3.5 × 10¹⁹ watts, eight gigatons of TNT a second from a hole the size of a saucer, and still only a ten-millionth of the whole Sun; it feels like a sunny afternoon at about 105,000 km, a quarter of the way to the Moon. The child can check: the surface is about 2,700 times cooler than the core, and 2,700 to the fourth is about fifty trillion. Caveats for the entry: radiant heat only, the air ignored; and at the core's temperature the glow is hard X-rays, so "you'd feel the same warmth, but you would not want to stand in the beam." Michael, 24 Oct: keep that; it is the chance to talk about photons, and the Sun's core is not the hottest thing we will describe.

The ladder, then: candle, kiln, Sun's surface across a room, Sun's core from a quarter of the way to the Moon, and whatever comes next (a supernova's core, the first minutes of the universe) handed over to colour and speed.

Melting and boiling points sit on this ruler too. Where a child's hand stops (about 320 K) is marked; where paper burns, where lead runs, where iron runs, where everything is gas.

**Age: how old is this atom?** With the obvious caveats, which are the point. Hydrogen atoms are as old as the universe. Nearly every other atom in a piece of zinc was made in a star that died before the Sun was born, which makes the zinc older than the Sun, and the same is true of the child. We cannot date a single atom, but we can say which generation of stars most of them came from, and for uranium we can even say roughly when, because its two kinds decay at different rates and the mix today remembers the starting mix. The honest answer is a range and a story, not a number.

*Eileen's talk.* Eileen once gave an extended popular talk, "How Old Are You Really," on the chemical make-up of a human and the age of each element in it. People liked it; Michael thinks kids would love it (24 Oct). As a story it reads the "in you" bar of every core entry in one sitting and ends with most of the child being older than the Sun. Credit Eileen; ask her for the notes.

**Birth: what kind of star.** Rough but true:

- Hydrogen, helium, a little lithium: the Big Bang itself.
- Carbon and nitrogen: stars of about one to eight suns, late in life, which then shed their outer layers and give the stuff away (about half the carbon, about three quarters of the nitrogen; Kobayashi 2020). Oxygen comes almost all from the bigger stars.
- Everything up to iron: stars of more than about eight suns, which build the elements in shells and then explode; about half the iron instead comes from exploding white dwarfs, which the lab files under the last bin.
- Most of the heavy ones, gold and uranium among them: the explosions themselves, and, we now think, the collision of two dead stars' cores. Gold is mostly from the collisions. The `collisions` bin also holds exploding white dwarfs (Type Ia supernovae), which make about half of all iron.

So the "stellar type" sort is really three or four bins, and the tile for gold should say that most of the gold on Earth was made in an event a child can picture: two neutron stars spiralling together.

## Spectra, and how to put them in front of a child

Every element glows in its own set of colours, and nothing else glows in exactly that set. That is the whole idea; the fingerprint metaphor is fair. The lab shows each element's lines as a strip: black with coloured lines for a glowing gas, or the rainbow with dark gaps for light that has passed through the gas. Hydrogen's four visible lines are the first strip a child should see, because there are only four and they are pretty.

The story that opens spectra is helium: a yellow line in the Sun's light in 1868 that matched nothing on Earth, named for the Sun, and found on Earth twenty-seven years later. A child who has met that story knows what a spectrum is for without being told. The colour lab has the prism and the three lamps; this is where they pay off.

## Interactives

- **The sorts** (above). The first interactive, and possibly enough on its own for the first release.
- **The rulers as instruments.** The cube with a scale under it; the temperature ruler with the three readings changing as you slide; the age range with its caveats.
- **The glow.** Slide the temperature and watch the colour of a block change, with its spectrum alongside. One slider, two readouts, and the child has the link between heat and light.
- **The builder.** Below.

## The builder

Two dials and a tile. One dial adds or removes protons; the other adds or removes neutrons. Electrons are not a dial: charge is conserved, so they follow the protons on their own, and the child never has to think about them. (This is the boundary condition from Laws-Macroscopic.md doing real work.)

Around the tile, the readouts that every entry already has: the name and symbol, the weight of the 10 cm cube, the spectrum strip, the tile's place in the "how it behaves" sort, and a small light that says whether this thing lasts.

What the child discovers by turning the dials, in the order she is likely to discover it:

- **Protons change everything you can see.** One click and the name changes, the symbol changes, the spectrum is a completely different set of lines, the tile jumps to a new column, the cube weighs something else. Carbon to nitrogen to oxygen is the first walk to take. The elements are a counting sequence; that is the quiet point of the whole lab, and here she counts it.
- **Neutrons change almost nothing you can see.** Same name, same symbol, same spectrum (to the eye), same column, same chemistry. Only the weight moves, and only a little. This is the surprise, and it is the real content: what a thing is depends on one number, and the other number is just ballast.
- **Until it isn't.** Keep adding neutrons and the light goes amber, then red: the nucleus will not hold, and the lab shows it falling apart and into what. Carbon with six neutrons lasts forever; with eight it turns, over a few thousand years, into nitrogen. Potassium-40 turns into argon over a billion years. Uranium walks down a long staircase and ends as lead. These three are the clocks, and they should be the three worked examples, because a child will meet all three in the Time Machine.
- **Too few neutrons, same trouble.** The stable band is narrow and bends upward (heavy nuclei need more neutrons than protons), which is why uranium has 146 neutrons to its 92 protons. A child who has walked the dials sees the band without being shown a chart of it.
- **Half-lives.** Needed for the clocks, and they are a probability idea: not "this atom will decay in 5,730 years" but "half of a heap will be gone by then." The builder should show a heap, not an atom, when it shows decay. Links to the probability lab if it exists; the random walk is the same mathematics.

Mass, for the readout, is the proton count plus the neutron count, near enough; the cube's weight follows from that and from how tightly the atoms pack, which the lab can look up. The builder does not need to be exact about binding energy; it needs to be honest that the weight of a nucleus is a touch less than its parts, and that the missing bit is what the Sun shines on. One sentence, no number.

This drags the lab below the macroscopic line that Laws-Macroscopic.md draws. Michael's ruling, 24 Oct: yes, the builder shows decay. The clocks need it, and the lab is where the microscopic edge belongs if it belongs anywhere.

**Why it is the deep end** (Michael asked, 24 Oct). Not the physics, which is a table lookup, and not the dials. Three things. *Reach:* the dials let a child click past the lab's tiles; one proton past iron is cobalt, which has no entry, and she can keep going to 92 and then 118, all real. So the builder needs a thin entry (name, symbol, weight, lasts or not) for every element, a second and larger data file, and a way of showing "this exists but we have not been here." *The neutron table:* every element has a handful of stable kinds and dozens of unstable ones, each with its own lifetime and way of falling apart, sometimes two ways; uranium's staircase to lead has fourteen steps and branches. The data is free, but curating it down to what a child can follow is judgement, and someone must decide where simplifying becomes wrong. *Probability:* half-lives need a heap and a clock, and a child who has not met randomness elsewhere in CW meets it here by accident. Better if the probability lab opens that door on purpose. None of it is hard to code; it is hard to scope.

**The shallow version.** The dials wander only across tiles the lab already has; everything else greys out with a name and nothing more; decay is shown for the three clocks only. That delivers the real surprise (protons change everything, neutrons almost nothing) for a day's work rather than a season's, and leaves the full builder as a later session.

## Toward a build prompt

Michael asked (24 Oct) how far we are from a prompt that would build this. Honest answer: close for version one, if version one is tiles, entries, the five sorts and the glow slider. Following the pool pattern, the work splits into pieces that different chats can do in parallel:

- **The data file and its schema** (Cowork): Schema-Elements.md, with carbon worked. Done as a draft.
- **The entries' words** (batch chats, like the events batches, ten tiles a batch): the record for each tile, checked the way the pool checks, in the CW voice. Three batches: (1) hydrogen, helium, carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus, sulfur, silicon, aluminium, iron; (2) magnesium, calcium, sodium, chlorine, potassium, nickel, uranium, copper, tin, lead; (3) gold, silver, zinc, and the mixtures bronze, brass, steel, solder, glass. Copper's entry must agree with the copper story chat already running; the batch takes that chat's research as a source.
- **Event-style stories** (a story chat with the cw-story skill): the candidates below, one at a time, each naming its tiles and its laws in *Notes for us*.
- **Pictures** (Claude Code, since Wikimedia Commons cannot be reached from the chats): found, not generated, for the samples themselves; many good public-domain and CC-BY photographs of element samples exist on Commons, and the pool's picture insert already has the rules. Generated or drawn only for diagrams, in the house drawing style (Life-Drawing-Style.md), if we need any. Spectrum strips are drawn from public line lists (NIST's atomic spectra database is the standard source), not photographed.
- **The build** (Claude Code): vanilla JavaScript and canvas, as everything else; the front door as the Time Machine's (explanation and Next); the sorts as an animated rearrangement; the glow slider from the blackbody formula; tiles over the data file; the lab reachable on its own and each tile reachable by link from a story. Point the prompt at this file, the schema and the data file; name the open items and tell it not to invent them; stop and report before any commit.

Later sessions, each its own prompt: the cube and temperature rulers as instruments; the shallow builder; the full builder; the mixtures shelf if it is not in version one.
