**More (343 words)**

```
About 2.46 billion years ago.
```

In Karijini National Park, in the Pilbara of Western Australia, Joffre Gorge cuts down through rock striped red, grey and black. The rock is banded iron: thin layers rich in iron and layers richer in clay, laid down one on another on a seafloor about 2.46 billion years ago.

The stripes are not all alike. Thin layers gather into bundles, and the bundles come round again and again, like a beat.

So what kept the beat?

The Earth spins like a top, and like a top it wobbles, so that its axis slowly traces a circle. Together with slow changes in the Earth's path around the Sun, the wobble changes how sunlight falls on the Earth through the year, and so the climate changes in a slow rhythm too. Today the rhythm takes about 21,000 years to come round. The climate changed what settled on the seafloor, and the rock kept the beat.

How fast the Earth wobbles depends on how fast it spins and on how close the Moon is. So if you can measure the beat in very old rock, you can work out how long a day was then.

In 2022 Margriet Lantink, a geologist at Utrecht University, and her colleagues did that with the layers at Joffre Gorge, which they dated by tiny zircon crystals in a thin layer of shale. Their answer was a day of about 16.9 hours, with the Moon about 321,800 kilometres away, a little more than four-fifths as far as it is now.

The day has been growing longer ever since, and the Moon has been moving away. The Moon's pull raises tides in the sea, and the tides drag on the spinning Earth like a hand on a turning wheel, slowing it. As the Earth slows, the Moon is pushed outward, and today it moves away by about four centimetres a year.

Before 2022, the oldest measurement of the day made this way came from rock in northern China about 1.4 billion years old, and it gave a day of nearly 19 hours.

Near Gowganda, in northern Ontario, there is grey rock packed with stones of every size, from grains of sand to boulders, jumbled together. Rivers and waves sort stones by size. Ice does not. A glacier picks up whatever it slides over and drops the lot in a heap when it melts.

The rock at Gowganda is glacier rubble that turned to stone, about 2.3 billion years old. It is the youngest of three such layers north of Lake Huron, one above another; the oldest, called the Ramsay Lake, was laid down about 2.45 billion years ago. Rocks in South Africa hold others. Dated by the ash and lava between the layers, the rocks of the two countries say that ice came and went at least four times between about 2.45 and 2.22 billion years ago.

How far did the ice reach?

In South Africa, in the Northern Cape, a layer of glacier rubble called the Makganyene lies under old lava. When lava cools, tiny grains of iron in it line up with the Earth's magnetism and stay that way, and how steeply they point says how far from the equator the lava was. In 1997 David Evans, Nicolas Beukes and Joseph Kirschvink measured the lava above the Makganyene and found it had formed about 11 degrees from the equator. Kirschvink and his colleagues argued that the whole Earth had frozen. Not everyone is convinced.

Why then?

The ice came at about the same time as oxygen first reached the air. Before that, the air probably held methane, a gas made by tiny living things, which traps the Sun's heat even better than carbon dioxide does. Oxygen destroys methane. One idea is that when oxygen arrived, the methane went, and the Earth cooled until it froze. Many geologists think the timing is no coincidence. One study argues that the oxygen came first, but the question is not settled.

This was not the Earth's first ice age. Rubble in South Africa shows that glaciers were already at work about 2.9 billion years ago, but nobody knows how far that ice reached.

**More (345 words)**

Long sticks of rock drilled from the ground in South Africa hold grey shale, mud that settled on a seafloor between about 2.35 and 2.2 billion years ago. Inside the shale are tiny grains of pyrite, a mineral made of iron and sulphur, and the sulphur in them tells whether the air above that sea held oxygen.

How can sulphur tell?

Sulphur atoms come in a few weights, some slightly heavier than others. Volcanoes breathe out sulphur gas, and high in the air the Sun's ultraviolet light breaks the gas apart. With no oxygen in the air, the ultraviolet reaches all the way down, and the breaking sorts the sulphur atoms by weight in an odd way that nothing else on Earth does. Rain carries the sulphur into the sea, and the rocks keep the odd mix. But even a trace of oxygen, about a hundred-thousandth of what the air holds now, is enough to end it.

In 2000 James Farquhar and his colleagues measured sulphur in rocks of many ages and found that the odd mix vanishes between about 2.45 and 2.09 billion years ago.

Other rocks show oxygen first reaching the air about 2.43 billion years ago. In 2021 Simon Poulton, a geochemist at the University of Leeds, and his colleagues looked closely at younger rocks from the Transvaal of South Africa. They found the odd mix coming back after oxygen had arrived, then vanishing again, more than once. Only about 2.22 billion years ago did oxygen stay for good.

Why did it rise then, and not before? The tiny living things that make oxygen were probably at work hundreds of millions of years earlier. Geologists have several ideas: that more volcanoes were on land instead of under the sea, that hydrogen leaking away into space slowly cleared the way, or that the living things that made methane ran short of the nickel they need. None has won.

Since then, oxygen has never left the air, though for more than another billion years there was far less of it than there is now.

Around the town of Cobalt, in northern Ontario near the Quebec border, there is a sandstone called the Lorrain. Look at it under a microscope and its grains of quartz, clear as glass on their own, are coated with a thin skin of red dust. The dust is hematite, a kind of rust.

In 2024 a team of geologists in Canada found that the red skin formed soon after the sand was laid down on land, as rainwater soaked through it, a little more than 2.31 billion years ago. They say it is the oldest red rock on land that anyone has found.

Rust is iron joined with oxygen. Iron on land rusts only if there is oxygen in the air.

So what did the land look like before?

At Blind River, also in Ontario, and in South Africa, there are older gravels, laid down by rivers, that hold grains of pyrite, a shiny yellow mineral of iron and sulphur often called fool's gold. Oxygen destroys pyrite. Those grains were tumbled down rivers and survived, so the air they tumbled through had almost no oxygen in it.

Rust is a mineral, and once there was oxygen, the Earth could make many minerals it had never made before. Robert Hazen, a mineralogist in Washington, has spent years counting. In 2008 he and his colleagues worked out that the dust the solar system formed from held only about a dozen kinds of mineral, and that by the time the first continents formed there may have been about 1,500. Of the 4,300 kinds then known, they argued, most exist only because of living things, chiefly because of the oxygen that living things made.

Mineralogists keep finding new kinds. In September 2026 the official list held 6,239.

## 5. Banded iron

**Label:** Banded iron
**Age:** 1878 Ma · ± 2 Ma · radiometric (uranium–lead) on zircons in volcanic ash in the Gunflint iron formation, which matches the Mesabi's
**Tail:** none — banded iron is far older than this; this is its last great pulse
**Known from:** iron formations around Lake Superior, dated by ash beds in them and by a layer of debris from the Sudbury impact above them
**Place now:** the Hull–Rust–Mahoning mine, Hibbing, Minnesota, USA (47.46 N, 92.95 W)
**Kind:** earth event
**Replaces:** sketch *Banded iron*

**Summary (36 words)**
About 1.88 billion years ago. For the last time on such a scale, the iron dissolved in the sea fell as rust, in layers. The layers are now the iron ore of Minnesota, Michigan and Ontario. *More*

**More (322 words)**

```
About 1.88 billion years ago.
```

At Hibbing, in Minnesota, there is a hole in the ground about five kilometres long, three wide and more than 160 metres deep. It is the Hull–Rust–Mahoning mine, and people have been digging iron ore out of it since 1895. The mine lies along the Mesabi Range, a name that comes from the Ojibwe *Misaabe Wajiw*, "Big Man Mountain."

The ore is banded iron, rock in thin layers, some dark and rich in iron and some paler and made of silica, the stuff of quartz and flint. The layers settled on a seafloor about 1.88 billion years ago.

How does a sea make iron ore?

Iron dissolves in seawater only when there is little or no oxygen in it. Join that iron with oxygen and it becomes rust, which cannot stay dissolved, so it sinks to the bottom. Layer after layer of rust, with layers of silica between, became banded iron. What did the joining is argued: oxygen made by tiny living things, other tiny living things that use iron directly, or sunlight.

Banded iron is old. The oldest, in Greenland, is more than 3.7 billion years old, and the greatest of all was laid down in Western Australia about 2.47 to 2.45 billion years ago. The iron around Lake Superior, in Minnesota, Michigan and Ontario, was one last great pulse. Then the seas almost stopped making banded iron for more than a billion years.

Why did it stop? Some geologists think the deep sea gained oxygen, so iron could no longer travel through it dissolved. Others think the deep sea filled instead with sulphur, which locked the iron away as pyrite. Others point to a huge asteroid that hit near what is now Sudbury, Ontario, about 1.85 billion years ago, and stirred the oceans.

Most of the iron ore mined in the world today is banded iron, and most of the iron ore mined in the United States comes from Minnesota.

Under the town of Flin Flon, and across a wide belt of northern Manitoba and Saskatchewan, lie the roots of mountains. Geologists call the belt the Trans-Hudson. About 1.8 billion years ago, two old pieces of what is now North America pushed into each other here, the way India is pushing into Asia today, and the edges crumpled up into mountains. The mountains wore away long ago; the roots remain.

Belts like this one, from about 2.1 to 1.8 billion years old, are found on nearly every continent. In 2002 two groups of geologists argued that they were the seams of one supercontinent, made when most of the land on Earth came together.

They gave it different names. John Rogers and M. Santosh called it Columbia, after the Columbia region of North America, which in their map lay next to India. But Paul Hoffman had already, in 1997, called the joined core of North America and northern and eastern Europe Nuna, from an Inuit word for the lands bordering the northern oceans, and many geologists now use his name.

How do you fit together a continent nobody has seen?

One way is to match the seams, like the torn edges of a map. Another is the magnetism of rocks. When rock forms, tiny grains of iron in it line up with the Earth's magnetism and stay that way, and how steeply they point says how far the rock was from the equator. But the grains cannot say how far east or west the rock was, so a piece of continent can slide sideways on the map without breaking any evidence.

So the shape is argued. Siberia may have been part of Nuna, but nobody has proved it, and for parts of South America and Africa there is no strong evidence that they were part of it. Many geologists think Nuna was together by about 1.8 billion years ago, others say somewhat later, and it seems to have held together until somewhere around 1.5 to 1.4 billion.

Nobody has drawn a map of Nuna that everyone accepts.

Every animal, every plant, every mushroom and every seaweed is made of cells of one kind. Each has a nucleus, a small bag that holds the cell's genes. Each also holds tiny parts called mitochondria ("my-toe-KON-dree-uh"), which use oxygen to make the energy the cell runs on. Bacteria have neither.

The mitochondria were once bacteria themselves. Long ago a cell swallowed a bacterium and, instead of digesting it, kept it, and the two lived on as one. Lynn Margulis, an American biologist, argued this in 1967, and many journals turned her paper down before one printed it. It is now accepted.

But when did it happen?

In the Yanshan mountains of North China there are rocks called the Chuanlinggou, made of mud that settled about 1.64 billion years ago. In them, under the microscope, are fossils of single cells with walls patterned and built in ways that bacteria are not known to build. In 2024 a team of Chinese and American palaeontologists described something more from the same rocks: threads of a few cells to more than twenty, with what may be spores inside. And on the other side of the world, in the Northern Territory of Australia, rock of about the same age holds molecules from the skins of cells, of a kind made almost only by cells like these.

Do they belong to the family of cells alive today? Some palaeontologists think so. Others think they were relatives that died out, and that the cells alive today share an ancestor that lived much later.

Older claims are harder to believe. Coiled ribbons in rock in Michigan, about 1.87 billion years old, might be the remains of a cell like these or of a colony of bacteria. The clocks in living things' genes give dates from about 2.1 billion to about 1 billion years ago.

By about 1.05 billion years ago there is a fossil nobody argues over: a red seaweed, with a nucleus in every cell.

When the Earth's crust is pulled, it cracks, and melted rock rises from below and fills the cracks. The melt cools into walls of dark rock, called dykes, that cut straight down through the older rock around them. Across South Australia, from around Lake Gairdner, runs a swarm of them about a thousand kilometres long, mostly buried under younger ground.

In 1998 Michael Wingate and his colleagues dated tiny crystals from the Gairdner dykes. The dykes had set about 827 million years ago, and they were among the first cracks in a continent that held most of the land on Earth.

That continent is called Rodinia. It came together between about 1.3 billion and 900 million years ago, and in 1990 Mark and Dianna McMenamin named it from *rodina*, the Russian word for motherland.

Nobody is sure of Rodinia's shape. Some maps put Antarctica beside western North America, some put Australia there, and some put a piece of China between them. Every map has to fit the same evidence: cracks, and the lava that filled them, of the same age, on coasts that are now oceans apart.

The cracking went on for about a hundred million years. About 780 million years ago dykes filled cracks across more than 2,400 kilometres of western North America. By about 750 million years ago the land along that coast had begun to split away. About 720 million years ago lava poured out over millions of square kilometres of what is now Arctic Canada, on land that then lay near the equator.

Where Australia began to crack, a long trough opened and slowly filled with sand, mud and lime. That trough is now the rock of the Flinders Ranges and the hills around Adelaide, where the rubble of the next great ice ages was found.

A million or two years after the last of the Arctic lavas cooled, the ice began to spread.

**More (320 words)**

```
About 717 million years ago.
```

On the southern edge of Adelaide, the Sturt River has cut a gorge through grey rock full of jumbled stones, from pebbles to boulders. More than a hundred years ago, Adelaide geologists argued that the jumble had been dropped by glaciers, and in 1950 Douglas Mawson and Reg Sprigg named this time of ice the Sturtian, after the gorge.

Rubble like it turns up on nearly every continent, of nearly the same age. That was strange enough. Stranger still, some of it seemed to have formed near the equator.

When rock forms, tiny grains of iron in it line up with the Earth's magnetism, and how steeply they point says how far from the equator the rock was. In 1964 Brian Harland, a geologist at Cambridge, argued from this that there had been ice in the tropics. In the 1960s a Soviet scientist, Mikhail Budyko, worked out that if ice ever covered the whole Earth, it would reflect so much sunlight that it could never melt, so it could never have happened. And yet the Earth is not frozen.

In 1992 Joseph Kirschvink, a geologist in California, gave the idea its name, the snowball Earth, and suggested how the ice could end. In 1998 Paul Hoffman and his colleagues published evidence from Namibia, and many more geologists began to take it seriously.

In 2010 Francis Macdonald and his colleagues dated ash among the glacial rocks of the Yukon at a little under 717 million years, and showed that the Yukon then lay within ten degrees of the equator, with ice reaching the sea.

How cold was it? Models say the equator may have been as cold as Antarctica is now. Some geologists think a strip of open water stayed near the equator. Others think the ice covered nearly everything.

The ice lasted about 57 million years, nearly as long as the time from the end of the dinosaurs to now.

**More (335 words)**

In the hills of northern Namibia there is rubble that glaciers dropped, turned to stone, called the Ghaub. Here and there in it are thin layers of volcanic ash.

Volcanic ash holds tiny crystals of zircon, and a zircon starts a clock when it forms. Some of the uranium in it slowly turns into lead, at a rate that is known, so the more lead there is, the older the crystal. Ash that falls into rubble as it piles up dates the rubble.

In 2016 Anthony Prave and his colleagues dated two ash beds in the Ghaub. One, already deep in the rubble, was about 639 million years old. The other, near the top, was about 635 million, so the ice had lasted at least four million years. In 2025 Adrian Tasistro-Hart and his colleagues dated ash in the same hills that fell just before the ice arrived, also about 639 million years ago, and came to the same answer: this second snowball lasted about four million years. The first had lasted about 57 million.

Geologists call this second snowball the Marinoan, after Marino, a suburb of Adelaide. Douglas Mawson and Reg Sprigg named it in 1950, along with the Sturtian.

Was it really at the equator? In the Flinders Ranges of South Australia there is rock of the same ice age, the Elatina, laid down in a sea at the edge of the ice. In 1999 Linda Sohl, Nicholas Christie-Blick and Dennis Kent measured its magnetism and found that it formed about eight degrees from the equator. They also found that the Earth's magnetism had flipped, north for south, at least twice while it was being laid down. The magnetism flips only every so often, so the ice at the equator lasted at least hundreds of thousands of years.

About 580 million years ago ice came again, in what is now Newfoundland, near Gaskiers. That ice came and went quickly, perhaps within a few hundred thousand years, and nobody thinks it was a snowball.

## 12. The thaw

**Label:** The thaw
**Age:** 635 Ma · ± 1 Ma · radiometric (uranium–lead) on ash at the top of the glacial rubble in Namibia (635.21 ± 0.59 Ma)
**Tail:** none — a dated moment
**Known from:** pale limestone lying straight on top of the glacial rubble, all over the world

**Summary (39 words)**
About 635 million years ago. The second snowball ended. Carbon dioxide from volcanoes, and perhaps dust darkening the ice, warmed the Earth enough to melt it, perhaps in a few thousand years. Pale limestone settled straight onto the rubble. *More*

**More (347 words)**

```
About 635 million years ago.
```

In Brachina Gorge, in the Flinders Ranges of South Australia, a creek has cut through layers of rock tilted on their sides. One layer is the rubble of the second snowball. Lying straight on top of it, with nothing in between, is a pale, fine rock, a kind of limestone, a few metres thick.

The same thing is found all over the world. Wherever there is rubble from this second snowball, there is pale limestone on top of it. Geologists call it the cap.

So how did a snowball end?

On an Earth without ice, rain takes carbon dioxide out of the air. The rain wears away rock, carries what it has dissolved to the sea, and the sea turns it into limestone. Volcanoes put the carbon dioxide back, and the two stay in rough balance.

Under the ice, there was almost no rain on bare rock, so the wearing away nearly stopped. But the volcanoes went on breathing out carbon dioxide for millions of years. It piled up in the air, perhaps to a hundred times as much as now, or more. Carbon dioxide traps the Sun's heat, and at last there was enough to start melting the ice at the equator. One recent study argues that dark volcanic dust on the ice helped, by soaking up sunlight.

Then the melting fed itself. Open sea is dark and soaks up sunlight that white ice would reflect, so the more sea opened, the warmer it got. Models say the ice could have retreated toward the poles in a few thousand years.

The air was still full of carbon dioxide. Hot, heavy rain fell on rock the ice had ground to powder, and the rivers carried what they dissolved to the sea, where it settled as the pale cap. How long that took is argued: some say thousands of years, some say hundreds of thousands.

Geologists chose the bottom of the cap at Enorama Creek as the start of a new stretch of the Earth's history, the Ediacaran, and in 2004 they marked the spot with a plaque.
