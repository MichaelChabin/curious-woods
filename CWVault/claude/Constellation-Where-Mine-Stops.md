# Where Mine Stops

## The core idea

A child looks at a map and sees countries: coloured shapes with hard edges, each one ending exactly where the next begins. That picture is about two hundred years old. Before it, people answered the question *where does mine stop* in other ways, and the other ways were not vaguer or stupider — some of them were more exact. They were answers to a different question.

These stories are about those other answers. A god living inside a stone. Six hundred judges sent from another city to settle an argument about a mountain. A map good enough to draw a line eight hundred years before anyone thought a country ended at one. A village split by who owed rent to whom in 1198, and still split that way, with a rule about front doors.

**A correction to an earlier version of this idea.** I first framed the spine as *people knew exactly where a field ended and only roughly where a country ended*. The evidence does not support it. Greek states drew political boundaries precisely when it mattered — a treaty of the 160s BC requires the line to run κατ' εὐθύ, "in a straight line," and appoints officers to go and set the stones; twelve markers survive along 28 kilometres of ridge on Taygetos, some cut into the living rock. They were not bad at lines.

The true spine is better. **A boundary used to be a different kind of object.** It was a bundle of rights — who could tax, hunt, judge, take a toll, appoint a priest — and those rights attached to people, revenues, offices and named places, not to a continuous surface. Several lords could hold different rights over the same village. You could not draw that on a map, because it was not that sort of thing. The line on the ground came later, and when it came it replaced something, and what it replaced is what these stories are about.

## The overlay

The map. Every story in this constellation uses it, and in two of them the map carries the argument rather than illustrating it. Full spec: `Spec-Maps.md`.

Two things the map already does that this constellation needs. A **region** is a wash and not an outline, because an outline claims a precision nobody has — which is the constellation's whole subject, arrived at independently. And a **window** map opens when she taps a name, which is how a story mentions the Euphrates or Taygetos without stopping.

One thing it does not yet do. Several of these stories want to show a boundary that is *not* a line — a set of scattered parcels, an enclave inside an enclave, a zone. The wash covers most of it. Baarle may need something new; that story will say so when it is written, rather than inventing it now.

## Intuitions served

- **#14 — Awareness that civilizations think differently.** The central one. Each story is a coherent worldview seen from inside, not compared from outside.
- **#13 — Sense of historical sweep.** Rome, Greece, Song China and 18th-century Europe answering the same question, and the child placing them against each other.
- **#15 — Holding a worldview without judging it (yet).** The Roman answer — put a god in the stone — is not superstition dressed up. It is a solution to the enforcement problem, and it worked for centuries.
- **#9 — "Why is this true."** Three of the five turn on sources disagreeing, and say so.

## Stories in this constellation

### Drafted

- **[[Story-The-God-Who-Would-Not-Move]]** — Rome put a god inside its boundary stones so that nobody could move them, and the boundaries moved anyway. The Terminalia rite, the law that made a stone-mover killable, the layer of ash a surveyor digs for, the temple built around a god who would not shift, and Augustine four hundred years later pointing out that Hadrian gave the land back regardless.

### Sketched

- **[[Story-The-Straight-Lines-Are-Still-There]]** — Roman surveyors laid a grid of squares 710 metres on a side across the land they took, and northeast of Padua those squares are still the working drainage ditches. A 2024 survey counted 197 kilometres of ditch across 31 squares, and measured them disappearing at about two kilometres a year. The beat: the oldest thing in that landscape is the geometry, and it is going. Carries the *groma*, the surveyors' own manuals, and the Codex Arcerianus — a sixth-century picture-book of land, one of the very few illuminated manuscripts to survive from late antiquity that is neither literature nor scripture.

- **[[Story-Three-Hundred-Years-About-One-Mountain]]** — Sparta and Messene argued over one district on Taygetos for about 350 years. It changed hands at least eight times, awarded each time by whoever was currently strongest — Philip, then Antigonos, then six hundred judges sent from Miletos, then Caesar, then Octavian, then finally the Roman Senate in 25 AD, with both sides arguing from myths and from monuments in a sanctuary. The beat: the line was never the problem. Who had the right to draw it was. Carries the Athenian stone that says, in the first person, *I am the boundary of the agora*, and was found still standing where it was set.

- **[[Story-The-Map-Came-First]]** — In 1136 someone carved a map of China into a stone slab, on a grid of squares a hundred *li* to a side, with a coastline close to what a satellite shows. Europe at that moment was drawing Jerusalem in the middle and the rest as decoration. The beat: China could draw the line eight hundred years before anyone had the idea that a country ends at one — and when that idea did arrive, it arrived in China and in Europe at roughly the same time. Carries the Warring States walls, and the Treaty of Nerchinsk in 1689, whose authoritative text was in Latin and whose boundary stones were cut in four languages.

- **[[Story-The-Front-Door-Rule]]** — Baarle is one town in two countries, with sixteen Belgian pieces inside the Netherlands and seven Dutch pieces inside those. It is not a curiosity; it is a fossil. The division goes back to 1198, when a duke handed over land and kept the parcels that paid him rent — so the border followed who owed what to whom, plot by plot. Where a house sits on the line, the address is decided by which country the front door opens into. The beat: this is what a boundary used to be everywhere. Carries Llívia (Spanish to this day because it was a town and not a village, and the treaty said villages), and Pheasant Island, shared by France and Spain six months each.

### Just a title

- **[[Story-After-the-Flood]]** — Egypt, the annual flood erasing field boundaries, and the rope-stretchers who laid them out again. Herodotus claims this is where geometry came from. Wants its own research pass before anything is written; the Herodotus claim in particular is the kind that gets repeated without checking. Would link the constellation to Glass Geometry and to the Ahmose strand.

## What the research changed

Worth recording, so no later session re-imports the errors.

**Westphalia (1648) did not invent the border**, and the claim that it did is a twentieth-century back-projection, codified for international relations by Leo Gross in 1948 and dismantled by Andreas Osiander in 2001. The treaties are silent on sovereignty. What they are *not* silent on is territory — they transfer a great deal of it. But what they transfer is rights, fiefs, jurisdictions, advocacies and titles, which is a different kind of object from a border. That distinction is the whole constellation in one sentence, and it is stronger than the myth it replaces.

**Europe's borders became surveyed lines in the nineteenth century, not the seventeenth.** The Treaty of the Pyrenees is signed in 1659; the 602 numbered cairns that mark that border were set by the Treaties of Bayonne in 1866–68. Two hundred and nine years between the treaty everyone names and the line on the ground. No single date works, and the absence of a single date is itself the finding.

**Do not use *eschatia* to mean the borderland between two city-states.** It means marginal land at the edge of cultivation — and it was productive land, not waste. A quarter of recorded Athenian properties are called that, far too many to be border strips.

**Pei Xiu did not invent the cartographic grid.** That is Needham's reading and the standard history of cartography rejects it. *Fen lü* means scale, not squares. The grid's first surviving appearance is the 1136 stone.

**The Great Wall as one ancient continuous object is largely myth.** The structure in photographs is late Ming. Waldron's book is the source.
