# The Cube That Won't Hold Still

There's a cube on the left. Look at it for a moment, and decide which way it's facing.

That sounds easy, and it isn't. Let your eyes rest on the center of the image and relax. If you do, sooner or later the cube will change. It seems to rotate, although the image doesn't change. Nothing moves. Still, somehow it seems to be facing in a different direction.

Wait a little longer and it will seem to shift back.

Nothing moved. The cube is made of twelve straight lines. Each stays exactly where it started.

Still the cube seems to move, and it will keep that up as long as you watch.

## Why it turns

The cube turns because the drawing doesn't provide enough information. It looks like a three dimensional cube, so that is how your brain thinks of it. But it is missing something.

The cube on the left now shows one face filled in. When that happens your brain has no trouble understanding what it sees.

Tap it, and the other face fills in. The cube seems to rotate, and again your brain knows exactly what it is looking at. Tap again and both faces disappear, and if you watch, the cube will begin to shift again.

The remarkable thing is that all three versions of the cube are the same, except that two of them have a face filled in. That is enough to tell us exactly which direction they face. Without that information, your brain shows you two views, one after the other, and lets you decide which one matters.

This makes sense. Your brain's job is to show you the best possible interpretation of the world you live in. If there isn't enough information to decide, it shows you one and then the other.

You can't really ask for more.

## Telling it which way

Maybe the most interesting thing about all this is that you can learn to control how you see the cube.

The cube on the left has a copper dot at each of the two corners that matter.

Before you begin, take a moment to relax. This isn't something you *make happen*. It is something you *allow to happen*.

You do it by resting your eyes on the copper dot that seems to be in the back corner of the cube. Don't try. You don't even have to focus. Just relax and watch the dot.

In a few seconds the cube will turn, and the corner you're looking at will be at the front.

When it turns, you may notice something: a small, quiet *there* — the feeling of a thing clicking into place. It's the same feeling you get a half-second after you understand a joke.

You should feel pleased when that happens. Once it has, let your eyes rest on the dot for a moment, then let them move to the other dot and wait again.

Do that a few times and something will change. The turns come sooner. You wait less. You are not doing anything more than you were doing at the start, and it works better.

You are learning to tell your brain how to see the cube.

## The small pleasure

Nobody knows exactly what happens in your head when the cube turns and you feel that little *there*. What follows is what is known, and where the knowing stops.

Tap the brain on the left. Two places are lit.

The small one, deep in the middle and low down, makes a chemical called dopamine (say "DOPE-uh-meen") and sends it to other parts of the brain. It is small, about the size of a pea, and its name is so long that neuroscientists call it the VTA.

The VTA produces dopamine when something turns out well, or better than you expected.

The larger one is the part of the brain that learns how to do things. It is called the hippocampus, because on its own, away from the rest of the brain, it looks a little like a seahorse. If you read *The Man Who Learned Without Knowing*, you've run into it before.

Here is how the VTA and the hippocampus work. They are connected together, and they work as a loop: when the hippocampus notices something new, the VTA answers with dopamine, and the dopamine makes it much easier for the hippocampus to hold on to whatever it was working on at that moment.

This is something you feel every day.

That much has been measured, mostly in animals, because measurements like this are almost impossible in humans. But even animal studies are enough to convince neuroscientists that this is how learning takes place in people.

So the chain — try something, feel the pleasure caused by the dopamine, then learn — is what neuroscientists expect from what they know.

Here is the part you can check yourself: the pleasure is real, you can feel it, and the cube gets easier.

## Learning

So far as anyone knows, being able to control which way this cube faces is not an essential skill. For one thing, it is almost impossible to test. Only you know what you see.

The reason it matters is that this is a good example of pure learning, on its own, with nothing else mixed in. And learning is an essential skill.

That means the more you know about how you learn, the better off you will be. If you practice with this cube from time to time, you'll begin to get a feeling for how you learn best.

With the cube, the best way seems to be this: relax and watch it; when your brain does what you want, let yourself feel the pleasure; then try again. Two or three minutes at a time, with a good night's sleep, is all you need. After a few days it will probably be automatic.

## The man who watched his crystals move

In 1832, a Swiss crystallographer named Louis Albert Necker was in Geneva, drawing crystals.

A crystallographer studies the shapes crystals grow into, which are hard, flat-sided shapes with corners, and to study them you draw them. Necker had drawn a great many. And while he was looking at his own drawings, they kept turning inside out — a corner he had drawn at the front would suddenly be at the back, without his having done anything about it.

This annoyed him, and it also interested him. He wrote a letter about it to David Brewster, a Scottish physicist he knew, and the letter was printed in a science magazine that year. In it he described the flipping, said it happened whether he wanted it to or not, and worked out that it had to do with where his eye happened to be resting on the drawing. He was right about that, and it took another hundred and seventy years for anyone to show in detail how much of it a person can control on purpose.

He was writing about a picture of a crystal. The shape people draw now is a plain cube, and it carries his name.

## Nobody needs this

You cannot do anything with a cube that turns when you ask it to. It isn't a skill anyone tests, and there's no reason to have it.

You have it anyway. A little while ago the cube changed whenever it felt like it. Now you can tell it which way to face, most of the time, and it took no effort and no trying and nobody taught you.

The twelve lines on the screen have not moved once.

*More:*

Other drawings do this too. There's a staircase that runs along the ceiling when you look at it the second way, drawn by a German scientist named Schröder in 1858, and a drawing that is a duck or a rabbit depending on the moment, and a vase that turns into two faces looking at each other. Any picture that two different solid things could have cast will do it.

Necker's own figure was not the cube everyone draws now. It was a rhomboid — a box pushed sideways, so the faces are diamonds rather than squares — because that is the shape the crystals he studied actually grow into.

Necker spent the end of his life on the Isle of Skye, off the west coast of Scotland, a long way from Geneva and from crystal drawing. He lived at Portree and died there in 1861.

If the cube goes on flipping for you no matter what you do, that's worth knowing rather than worth fixing: how much control people have over it varies from person to person, and always has. It isn't a measure of anything.

**Honesty checks.**
*Two interpretations, never both at once.* Standard account of bistable perception. That the brain never shows her the flat drawing is the phenomenology everyone reports, not a measurement.
*A filled face removes the ambiguity.* True, and it is the standard demonstration. Worth saying at the bench: the fill must read as an opaque face, not a tint, or the cube stays ambiguous and the section's claim fails.
*Resting the eye on a corner biases which way it faces.* Necker noticed it; Toppino 2003 is the modern demonstration, separating eye position from other kinds of intentional control.
*"The turns come sooner. You wait less."* Practice effects on voluntary control are real but modest and vary between people. Stated flatly in the body; hedged at the end of *More*, on purpose, rather than in the moment. Michael's call, made twice now.
*"After a few days it will probably be automatic."* "Probably" is carrying the weight. Nobody has run a several-day practice study on children and this figure, so this is Michael's experience and expectation, not a result. If he wants it to stand as testimony it should be third person by name; as written it reads as a general prediction.
*The VTA, about the size of a pea.* The ventral tegmental area is a small midbrain nucleus, and a pea is the usual comparison. The full name is *ventral tegmental area*, which is what "so long that neuroscientists call it the VTA" refers to.
*The hippocampus "learns how to do things."* Careful here. In *The Man Who Learned Without Knowing* the hippocampus is what writes down *what happened*, and the skill learning that survived without it lived in the basal ganglia and cerebellum. This story says it is "the part of the brain that learns how to do things," which contradicts that. Suggested fix, and the smallest one: "the larger one is the part that decides what gets kept." Michael's call, but the two stories should not disagree about this in front of the same child.
*The dopamine chain.* Framed as inference, at Michael's ruling. The hippocampal–VTA loop (Lisman & Grace 2005) is well supported and largely rodent work, which the story says. Nobody has measured reward signals during Necker reversals as far as I can find.
*"This is something you feel every day."* A claim about her, not about the research. Fine as written.
*Necker, 1832.* Louis Albert Necker (1786–1861), Swiss, of Geneva, crystallographer and geologist. The paper is a letter to Sir David Brewster, printed in *The London and Edinburgh Philosophical Magazine and Journal of Science* in 1832, and it does attribute the reversal to where the eye rests. Brewster edited the magazine and was the recipient; "a Scottish physicist he knew" avoids a claim about their friendship.
*"Another hundred and seventy years."* 1832 to 2003.
*The rhomboid.* Necker's figure is a rhomboid, not a cube; the cube is the later convention.
*Schröder's staircase, 1858.* Heinrich Schröder, German. The usual date.
*Skye.* Necker lived his last years at Portree and died there in 1861.

**The cube bench (new).** Three pictures, one program. Not Flash, Creep or Trace. Each sits in the left column at panel width.

The fill is opaque and quiet, a wash that reads as a solid face; the twelve lines stay visible and unchanged underneath. Nothing else moves, and the transition is immediate, not animated.
