# The geometry spine

Michael, 1 Oct: the trouble with the geometry stories was that they did not add up to anything. This is the order that makes them add up. Each step is a capability the lab gains. Stories are limited at the start and multiply with every step, because each new power can be used by everything that comes after.

## The one rule

**Each capability is opened by exactly one story, and no story uses a capability that has not been opened.**

One door per room; any number of rooms after it. This is what stops the fragmentation. Every story inherits a vocabulary the child already has, and the question "can she do this yet?" always has an answer.

## Two cautions

**This is a capability order. It is not a chronology and not a difficulty order.** Euclid in Alexandria sits at step four, though Euclid comes first in history. Nobody should later try to straighten it.

**A door story is not a lesson about the capability.** It is a story that happens to need it. The rose is about a man with a whitewashed table; it opens circles as a side effect of being worth reading.

---

## 1. Circles alone

**What it is.** Two locations to start, and one move: a circle from one found location to another. Nothing is placed; everything is found.

**It really is all of Euclid.** Mohr proved it in 1672 and his book was lost; Mascheroni proved it again in 1797. Anything straightedge and compass can do together, the compass can do alone. The usual caveat — that the compass constructs the two locations defining a line but cannot draw the line — does not bite here, because the lab already treats a line as two locations. The circle-only world is complete in CW in a way it is not on paper.

**Awkward, though, and that is the point.** She will want the straightedge before she is given it.

**Door:** *The Glass Rose*, Draft 9. **Unlocks:** the six-petal rose, the hexagon, the equilateral triangle, the lattice grown from circles. Wanted here: the hexagram.

## 2. Lines, given one circle

**The correction that justifies the whole order.** Lines alone are strictly weaker, not symmetrically awkward. With a bare straightedge you cannot bisect a segment or drop a perpendicular. Poncelet conjectured in 1822 and Steiner proved in 1833 that a straightedge does everything the full kit does **provided one circle and its centre are given** somewhere on the page — and only then. Hilbert later showed you cannot even recover a given circle's centre with a straightedge, because a projective transformation can hold the circle still and move its middle.

So circles are prior, not merely first. Lines can do everything, but only because someone once drew a circle.

**Door:** *Wright's Light Screens* (story two). He works inside a frame he is given, and the frame is exactly that gift. **Unlocks:** straight-line composition, the first sight of the grid as a thing rather than a background.

## 3. Circles and lines together

**All of Euclid, comfortably.** Both gestures, both kinds of crossing.

**Door:** *Your Own Window* (story three) — the two together, and what she makes is hers. **Unlocks:** Pythagoras, the pentagram, the classical constructions, most of the Elements worth a child's time.

## 4. The grid

**Euclid without the boring part.** Once the plane is ruled, the constructions that took six circles take none. The grid is not free — it is built with steps 1 to 3 — which is why it is earned rather than assumed.

**Door:** *Euclid in Alexandria* — the "it might have happened" story: he never made a grid, he could have, and the city outside his window was laid out on one. **Unlocks:** coordinates, measurement by counting, *What You Can See From Zero* (the visible locations from the origin, and 6/π² out of an orchard), and everything in step 5.

## 5. Rectangles and triangles, with dimensions

**Drag between intersections; a rectangle or a right triangle appears, with its sides and its area.** This is where the geometry lab and the multiplication work become one thing.

**Doors — four candidates, one of them first** (`Candidates-Grid-Lab-Stories.md`): Gee's Bend, where the rectangles were cut by eye out of work clothes; Mondrian, who turned a tree into a grid in public, one canvas at a time; Albers, where the squares are the excuse and the subject is what colours do to each other; Wright again, if he is not spent at step 2.

**Unlocks:** area as a picture of multiplication, squares and pronics, the rationals as triangles, rulings in halves and thirds and tenths. Wanted here: Ahmes, primes, pronic numbers.

## 6. Union, intersection, subtraction

**Not a violation of Euclid — a direction he already walked.** Book I is full of cutting a figure up and reassembling it to show two areas are equal, and the rearrangement proof of Pythagoras is the famous one. This step makes that habit into a tool.

**And it has a beautiful end.** Any two polygons of the same area can be cut into pieces that make each other. In three dimensions it fails: Hilbert's third problem, settled by Dehn in 1900 — you cannot always cut a solid up and reassemble it into another of the same volume. Something that always works on a table and stops working in the air.

**Door:** wanted. Cézanne is the candidate on the Labs Ledger — find the big shapes in a picture and colour them. The scissors-and-solids story may be its own, later.

**Unlocks:** compound shapes, cut-and-rearrange proofs, mosaic and tiling work.

## 7. Plotting graphs and functions

**The hinge.** Everything before this is Euclid getting more comfortable. The moment a curve becomes an equation it is Descartes, and it is a different subject wearing the same clothes. Worth naming as a break rather than letting it arrive quietly.

**Door:** wanted — Descartes. **Unlocks:** functions, curves as rules, everything in step 8.

## 8. Random walks, and what else the plane can carry

**Door:** *The Drunk Man and the Drunk Bird*. Pólya found the problem by accident, walking in a garden near Zürich and repeatedly blundering into the same young couple, who wanted to be left alone. A walker stepping at random on a flat grid comes home, certainly, if he walks long enough; in three dimensions he may never come back. *The anecdote and the theorem both need checking against a source before this is built.*

**Unlocks:** probability on the plane, and the honest answer to how a thing can be certain and useless at the same time.

## What this sorts out

The wanted list stops being a heap. Every geometry story on it now has a place: the hexagram at 1, Pythagoras and the pentagram at 3, *What You Can See From Zero* at 4, Ahmes and primes and pronics at 5, Cézanne at 6, Pólya at 8.

It also says what cannot be written yet. Nothing at step 5 can be built before the grid has a door, and the grid has no door until *Euclid in Alexandria* exists.
