## 1. Triassic ends

**Label:** Triassic ends
**Age:** 201.56 Ma · ± 0.02 Ma · uranium–lead on zircons in the first lavas, tied to the extinction layer in lake beds by the rhythm of their layers: the extinction 201.564 ± 0.015 Ma, the earliest lavas at about the same time (Blackburn 2013: in New Jersey the extinction lies about 13,000 years below the first lava; in Morocco at the base of the oldest lava). **Tail:** none — a dated moment
**Known from:** fossils that stop at one layer in the sea and in old lake beds, footprints, pollen and spores, and the breathing pores of fossil leaves
**Place now:** St Audrie's Bay, near West Quantoxhead, Somerset, England (51.18 N, 3.28 W)

**Replaces:** a new mark (Michael, 7 Oct)

**Summary (33 words)**

**More (346 words)**

```
About 201.6 million years ago.

At St Audrie's Bay ("AW-dreez"), on the coast of Somerset, in the southwest of England, a stream falls over the cliff onto the beach, and the cliffs are striped. At one end they are red-brown and green, mud laid down in shallow, salty lakes on a dry plain. Then comes a band of dark grey and paler stone. Above it is the Blue Lias ("LYE-us"), stripes of dark shale and pale limestone, laid down in the sea. In the Blue Lias, higher up, are the coiled shells of an ammonite called *Psiloceras* ("sy-LOSS-er-us"). In that band, or just above it, the world changed.

In the sea, the conodonts ("KON-oh-donts"), small eel-like animals known mostly from their tiny teeth, died out for good. The ammonites, with their coiled shells, nearly vanished; *Psiloceras* probably came from one of the few lines that lived. Reefs fell apart, and most kinds of coral with them. Of the groups of closely related animals in the sea, somewhere between a quarter and three quarters died out; the estimates differ.

On land, the big relatives of the crocodiles died out: the phytosaurs ("FY-toh-sorz"), which looked much like crocodiles, and others. Only small ones were left. In 2002 Paul Olsen and his team studied footprints left in the mud of old lakes at more than seventy places in eastern North America, and found that within about thirty thousand years the biggest meat-eaters' prints were about a fifth larger than before.

What did it? In East Greenland and in Sweden, Jennifer McElwain counted the breathing pores on fossil leaves. Plants grow fewer pores when the air holds more carbon dioxide, and in 1999 she and her colleagues argued that it had risen about fourfold, and the world had warmed. In 2013 Terrence Blackburn and his team dated the dying, and found that it began at about the same time as the first lavas that poured out as Pangaea cracked. Many geologists think the carbon dioxide from those lavas warmed the world and made the sea more acid, which is hard on shells and on coral.

## 2. Archaeopteryx (revised)

**Label:** Archaeopteryx
**Age:** 149 Ma · ± 1 Ma · from the ammonites in the layers (early Tithonian, which began 149.2 ± 0.7 Ma on the current chart, since 2022), not a radiometric date; the oldest specimen, from Schamhaupten, lies at the Kimmeridgian–Tithonian boundary (Rauhut 2018)

**More (348 words)**

```

Around the town of Solnhofen ("ZOLN-hoe-fen"), in Bavaria, in southern Germany, the hills are full of quarries, and the stone in them is cream-coloured limestone that splits into thin, flat slabs, like the pages of a book. For more than a hundred years the best of it was used for printing pictures.

In 1861, in a quarry at Solnhofen, a slab split open on a single feather, about seven centimetres long, a dark film on the pale stone. Hermann von Meyer described it that summer, and that autumn named the animal it came from *Archaeopteryx* ("ar-kee-OP-ter-ix"), "old wing."

The same year, near the village of Langenaltheim, another slab split open on the skeleton of an animal about the size of a crow. Around its arms were the marks of feathers, laid out as in a wing. But it also had teeth in its jaws, claws on its fingers, and a long tail of bone.

Was the feather from the same kind of animal? Nobody can prove it. In 2019 Thomas Kaye and his team shone a laser on the slab and found the outline of the feather's lost quill, and argued that its shape matched none of the long flight feathers of the wing or tail of *Archaeopteryx*. In 2020 Ryan Carney and his team argued that it was one of the smaller feathers that lie over the bases of the long wing feathers.

The limestone was laid down in quiet, salty lagoons between islands. Many geologists think the water at the bottom was too salty, or had too little oxygen, for anything to live there, so whatever sank to the bottom was not eaten, and was slowly covered by fine mud.

From the 1990s, farmers and scientists in Liaoning ("lee-ow-NING"), in northeastern China, began to find small dinosaurs with feathers. One of them, *Anchiornis* ("an-kee-OR-nis"), is about 160 million years old, and it had long feathers on its arms and on its legs. Feathers are older than birds.

About a dozen skeletons have been found, all in the limestones of southern Bavaria, and only the one feather.

## 3. Hot-country grasses

**Label:** Hot-country grasses
**Age:** 8 Ma to 6 Ma · a span, not the same everywhere: in East Africa about 8 to 7 Ma; in northern Pakistan from about 7.4 to 7.0 Ma in the soils (Quade 1989), mostly C4 by about 5 Ma; in South America by about 7.6 Ma; in southern North America 6.8 to 5.7; later in the north (Cerling 1997); dated by the layers, by their magnetism and by ash beds, not on the teeth themselves; the dot is at the start of the span
**Tail:** 32 Ma — grasses of this kind first appear about 30 to 32 Ma, and were common in some places for millions of years before they took over (Edwards 2010)
**Known from:** the mix of carbon in the tooth enamel of grazing animals, and in lumps of lime in old soils
**Place now:** the Potwar Plateau, northern Punjab, Pakistan; for a point, Rohtas Fort, near Jhelum (32.97 N, 73.58 E)
**Kind:** crop or animal (a living thing; the list has no better word)
**Replaces:** a new mark (Michael, 7 Oct)

**Summary (36 words)**
About 8 to 6 million years ago. Grasses that grow best in hot, bright, dry places spread over much of the warmer part of the world. The teeth of the animals that ate them show when. *More*

**More (346 words)**

```

      and probably begun by 32 million years ago.
```

On the Potwar Plateau, in northern Pakistan, between the Indus and the Jhelum, dry stream beds cut through layers of red, orange and yellow-brown rock. Many of the layers are old soils, built up by rivers from the young Himalaya. In them are lumps of lime that formed in the soil, and the teeth of animals that lived on it.

Carbon comes in two kinds that last: ordinary carbon, and a slightly heavier kind, carbon-13. Plants take in both, but not in the same mix.

Most plants make food with a substance that, in heat, often grabs oxygen by mistake. Some grasses add an extra step. They catch carbon dioxide in the outer cells of the leaf and pump it to the cells around each vein, so the work is done where carbon dioxide is plentiful. The pump costs energy, so it pays best where it is hot and bright, and helps where water is short. These are called C4 plants, and they keep more of the heavy carbon than other plants do. The mix passes into the soil and into the teeth of animals that eat them, and enamel keeps it for millions of years.

In 1989 Jay Quade, Thure Cerling and John Bowman measured the lime in the Potwar soils, and found that between about 7.4 and 7.0 million years ago, the plants of the river plains changed from mostly trees and shrubs to mostly C4 grasses.

In 1997 Cerling's team measured more than five hundred teeth of horses, rhinoceroses and other plant-eaters, from five continents. Across the warmer part of the world, between about eight and six million years ago, the grazers began eating C4 grasses. Farther north it came later, or not at all.

Why then? Cerling's team argued that the carbon dioxide in the air had fallen too low for ordinary plants where it was hot. Others found little sign of a fall, and argue for fire and long dry seasons.

Today C4 plants do about a quarter of all the food-making on land. Maize, sugarcane and millet are among them.
