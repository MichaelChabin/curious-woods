# Theophilus's World: events without stories

None of the eighteen events on this story's timeline opens a story yet. Each entry below is what the child sees when she taps the event: the title, the date line, the place, the paragraph, and (for the shared ones) the `why` sentence this story adds. A story written from one of these should carry the event's own facts and not contradict them; where the paragraph hedges, the story hedges. Years in prose are plain years (Rulings, *Dates*).

## The length of a year
*1079 · Isfahan* · place: Isfahan · id `khayyam-calendar-1079` · shared pool (stories/world-events.json)

At the observatory in Isfahan, Omar Khayyam and the astronomers working with him measured the year as 365.24219858 days. The figure used today is 365.24219. Their calendar began in 1079, and the calendar used in Iran today grew out of it.

*The story's why:* Europe went on with a less exact calendar for another five hundred years.

## A country written down
*1086 · Winchester* · place: Winchester · id `domesday-1086` · shared pool (stories/world-events.json)

William, the king of England, sent men to every part of the country to write down who held what land, and how many ploughs, pigs and mills there were on it. The survey was made in 1086, and the book was kept at Winchester.

*The story's why:* A whole country was written down a generation before Theophilus wrote down a whole craft.

## A needle that points south
*1088 · Zhenjiang* · place: Zhenjiang · id `shen-kuo-needle-1088` · shared pool (stories/world-events.json)

In his Dream Pool Essays, written at his garden in Zhenjiang, Shen Kuo described a needle, rubbed on a lodestone, that points south, and he noticed that it does not point quite true. He said nothing about using it at sea.

*The story's why:* That came thirty years later, in another book.

## A city of earth
*about 1100 · Cahokia* · place: Cahokia · id `cahokia-1100` · shared pool (stories/world-events.json)

Beside the Mississippi, where St Louis is now, Cahokia was at its largest around 1100, with somewhere between six thousand and forty thousand people. Its biggest mound, built by hand out of earth, is thirty metres high. It was the largest city north of Mexico.

*The story's why:* Its people left no writing, so we do not know what they called it.

## The oldest windows still in place
*about 1100 · Augsburg* · place: augsburg · id `augsburg-prophets` · this story only (in the page)

Five tall windows of prophets (David, Jonah, Daniel, Moses and Hosea) are in the cathedral at Augsburg, in the south of Germany. They were made some time between the late 1000s and the early 1100s; nobody knows exactly when. They are among the oldest coloured windows anywhere that are still in the building they were made for. Each one was drawn full size before any glass was cut, very likely on a board like Theophilus's.

## A goldsmith named Roger
*soon after 1107 · Helmarshausen* · place: helmarshausen · id `roger-helmarshausen` · this story only (in the page)

A metalworker named Roger came to the abbey at Helmarshausen, in what is now Germany, soon after 1107, and made things there out of gold and silver. Many people who study the book think Roger was Theophilus. Nobody can prove it, and some of them disagree.

## Crowds for an argument
*about 1115 · Paris* · place: Paris · id `abelard-1115` · shared pool (stories/world-events.json)

Around 1115 Peter Abelard became master of the cathedral school of Notre-Dame in Paris. Students came from all over Europe to hear him argue; the old accounts say thousands.

*The story's why:* The cathedral his school stood beside was later pulled down, and the Notre-Dame that stands today was begun in its place in 1163.

## In dark weather
*1119 · Guangzhou* · place: Guangzhou · id `zhu-yu-1119` · shared pool (stories/world-events.json)

Zhu Yu's father ran the port of Guangzhou, and Zhu Yu wrote down, among many other things, how its ships found their way. His book, published in 1119, says: "In dark weather they look at the south-pointing needle." It is the first book anywhere to say that sailors steered by the compass.

*The story's why:* It came out within a year or two of Theophilus's book, at the other end of the world.

## The book of how to make things
*about 1120 · probably Helmarshausen* · place: helmarshausen · id `on-divers-arts` · this story only (in the page)

A man who called himself Theophilus wrote down how to make paint, bells, organ pipes and coloured windows, step by step. Nobody knows exactly when; the best guess is between 1100 and 1120. If he was Roger, he wrote it at Helmarshausen. The whitewashed board is in the second part, chapter 17.

## The largest temple
*1122 · Angkor* · place: Angkor Wat · id `angkor-wat-1122` · shared pool (stories/world-events.json)

The Khmer king Suryavarman II began Angkor Wat, near what is now Siem Reap in Cambodia, in 1122, and it was finished about 1150. It is the largest religious building in the world.

*The story's why:* It was begun within a few years of Theophilus's book.

## Euclid comes back
*about 1126 · Bath* · place: Bath · id `adelard-euclid` · shared pool (stories/world-events.json)

Adelard of Bath travelled for seven years, to Salerno, to Sicily and as far as Syria, and was home in Bath by 1130. He translated Euclid's Elements from Arabic into Latin, perhaps around 1126, though it may have been after 1133. Scholars in Baghdad had translated Euclid from Greek into Arabic more than three hundred years before, and kept it.

*The story's why:* Euclid's book begins with two crossing circles, the first thing you drew on the table.

## A church made of windows
*11 June 1144 · Saint-Denis* · place: saint-denis · id `saint-denis-1144` · this story only (in the page)

Abbot Suger rebuilt the east end of his church at Saint-Denis, just north of Paris, and it was dedicated on 11 June 1144. He wrote that he wanted the whole church to shine with "the wonderful and uninterrupted light of most luminous windows." It is usually called the first Gothic building. After it, churches wanted far more coloured glass than before, and every piece of it was drawn on a table first.

## The best paper in the world
*about 1150 · Xàtiva* · place: Xàtiva · id `xativa-paper` · shared pool (stories/world-events.json)

Around 1150 the geographer al-Idrisi wrote that the town of Xàtiva, in al-Andalus, made paper "such as cannot be found anywhere else in the civilized world," and sent it east and west. North of the Alps, paper stayed scarce for another two hundred years: the first paper mill there was built at Nuremberg in 1390.

*The story's why:* Paper that scarce is one reason a window was drawn on a board.

## Three roses
*about 1205 to 1235 · Chartres* · place: chartres · id `chartres-roses` · this story only (in the page)

The cathedral at Chartres, south-west of Paris, was given three rose windows: the west one about 1205, the south about 1230, the north about 1235. Each is ten to twelve metres across, and they sit about twenty-two metres above the floor. This is the shape on your table, about a hundred years after the book, made the size of a house.

## A builder's sketchbook
*about 1225 to 1235 · Picardy* · place: honnecourt · id `villard-notebook` · this story only (in the page)

Villard de Honnecourt, from Picardy in the north of France, filled a book with drawings: buildings, machines, animals, people, and the geometry used to lay them out. It is in the national library in Paris. One of his drawings is of the west rose at Chartres, drawn not long after it was made.

## A rose that isn't six
*about 1250 · Paris* · place: Paris · id `notre-dame-north-rose` · this story only (in the page)

The north rose of Notre-Dame in Paris was made about 1250. It is not divided into six: it runs in rings of sixteen and thirty-two. It survived the fire of 2019. Its own story is not written yet.

## The same blue, far away
*1330s to 1351 · Jingdezhen* · place: Jingdezhen · id `blue-and-white` · shared pool (stories/world-events.json)

In the 1330s potters at Jingdezhen, in China, began painting porcelain with cobalt brought from Persia, under a clear glaze. The earliest pieces with a date written on them are a pair of temple vases from 1351.

*The story's why:* Cobalt is the metal that made the blue in a window, and two hundred years after Theophilus it made one of the most copied kinds of pottery in the world.

## A floor that kept its drawings
*about 1365 · York* · place: york · id `york-tracing-floor` · this story only (in the page)

In an upstairs room at York Minster the master masons drew the stone frames of windows full size on a plaster floor. One design on it, for the windows of the Lady Chapel, was drawn about 1365. Plaster does not wipe clean, so the lines are still there, one design scratched over another. They are drawings for stone, not glass, but they were made the way Theophilus made his: full size, with a compass.

## Named in the 2 October build's More, not on the line

*The collapsing compass* — Euclid's rule that every circle is made from two locations already on the table; the compass that forgets its width; Euclid I.2 carrying a distance with circles alone; Mohr (1672) and Mascheroni (1797) proving the compass alone is enough.

*Why Cobalt Is Blue* — the atom-level reason cobalt colours glass blue; tied to the blue-and-white event (Jingdezhen) and to Theophilus's remark that old mosaic glass was melted down for window blue.
