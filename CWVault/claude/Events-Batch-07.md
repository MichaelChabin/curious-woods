## 1. Issun-bōshi

About 1665. A printed book of short tales from Japan told the story of Issun-bōshi, a boy as tall as a thumb who set out in a bowl. Nobody knows how much older the story is. *More*

**More (350 words)**

An old couple in Naniwa, the town that is now Osaka, had no children, so they prayed at the shrine of Sumiyoshi, and when the wife was forty-one she had a son. He was one *sun* tall, an old Japanese measure of about three centimetres, so he was called Issun-bōshi ("ee-SOON BOH-shee"), the one-sun boy.

At twelve he still had not grown, and his parents began to wonder if he was a monster. So he left. He took a needle for a sword, a bowl for a boat and chopsticks for oars, and he rowed to Toba, a river port, left his bowl there and went on to Kyoto, the capital.

That is how the story begins in the oldest copy we have, a printed book of tales. A bookseller in Osaka named Shibukawa Seiemon sold the book in the early 1700s, and an earlier printing, with the same tales and the same pictures, is fifty years or more older. How much older the story itself is, nobody knows. Several Japanese dictionaries say it was first told before 1573, and one says it may be younger. No older copy has been found.

The hero of the old book is not quite the hero children meet today. In Kyoto he serves a high official of the emperor's court, and he wants the official's daughter for his wife. One night, while she sleeps, he smears grains of rice on her lips, then weeps and tells her father that she has eaten his rice. Her father orders him to get rid of her, and Issun-bōshi leads her away.

A wind blows their boat to a strange island, where two oni, giant demons, find them. One swallows Issun-bōshi whole, and he keeps climbing out through its eye until both oni run, dropping a magic mallet. He strikes the mallet, wishing to grow, and grows tall. He strikes again, and out come food, gold and silver.

Retellings for children, like Iwaya Sazanami's of 1896, left out the trick with the rice. What the story was like before anyone printed it is still to be found.

## 2. Daguerreotype

**Label:** Daguerreotype
**Year:** 1839 · precision: exact (19 August 1839) · kind: craft
**Place:** Institut de France, Paris (48.86 N, 2.34 E)
**Replaces:** `lead-starry-0`

**Summary (38 words)**
19 August 1839. In Paris, the French government gave away a way of making pictures with light, invented by Louis Daguerre. Anyone could use it free, except in England, where Daguerre had taken out a patent days before. *More*

**More (315 words)**

```

      or 19 August 1839
            or 187 years ago.
```

On 19 August 1839, in a hall of the Institut de France in Paris, the members of two academies, one for the sciences and one for the fine arts, met together to hear a secret given away. The secret was how to make a daguerreotype ("dah-GAIR-oh-type"), a picture made by light.

Louis Daguerre was a painter who ran a show of huge painted scenes in Paris. For years he had worked on the problem with Nicéphore Niépce, who had made the first photograph from a camera, and after Niépce died in 1833, Daguerre carried on alone.

His method went like this. A copper plate coated with silver was polished until it shone like a mirror, and the vapour of iodine made its surface sensitive to light. The plate went into a camera for several minutes. Then it was held over warm mercury, whose fumes made the picture appear, and washed with a chemical that stopped light from changing it any further. Each picture was the only one. There was nothing to print copies from.

A patent is a government's promise that only the inventor may use an invention, or sell the right to use it. But the French government decided that a method this simple could not be protected, because anyone who had seen it explained could do it. So it bought the method instead. Daguerre was paid 6,000 francs a year for life, and Niépce's son Isidore 4,000. The government's own statement said the method had to belong to everyone or stay unknown.

The news went fast. Within days a Paris shop was advertising the cameras, and by early September they had reached Berlin.

But it did not belong to everyone. A few days before the meeting in Paris, an agent for Daguerre took out a patent in England. Anyone in England who wanted to make a daguerreotype had to pay for the right.

## 3. Paint tubes

**Label:** Paint tubes
**Year:** 1841 · precision: year (British patent 6 March) · kind: craft
**Place:** London (51.51 N, 0.13 W)
**Replaces:** `lead-starry-1`

**Summary (34 words)**
1841. In London, John Goffe Rand, an American portrait painter, patented a small tube of thin metal for keeping oil paint. Squeezed, it let out a little paint, and it could be closed again. *More*

**More (328 words)**

```

```

Before 1841, a painter who wanted to keep oil paint fresh for a few days tied it up in a little bag made from a pig's bladder. To get paint out, you pricked the bladder with a tack and squeezed. The hole could not be properly closed again, so the paint near it dried, and a bladder carried in a pocket or a box could burst. Some painters kept paint in glass tubes or syringes, which kept it better but broke.

John Goffe Rand was born in New Hampshire in 1801, learned to make furniture, painted houses and signs, and then became a painter of portraits. By 1840 he was living in London, where he painted lords and dukes. He also thought about paint.

His answer was a tube of tin, so thin it could be squeezed like a bag, with a cap at one end. You unscrewed the cap and squeezed, and as much paint as you wanted came out. Then you put the cap back, and the paint inside stayed soft, because no air could get in. When the tube was empty, it was flat.

Rand patented the tube in Britain in March 1841, and in the United States that September. By the next year the London colour sellers Winsor & Newton were selling paint in his tubes.

Paint in tubes could go anywhere, and it kept. A painter could carry a dozen colours out into a field, a garden or a street, and come back with them still soft.

Many years later, Jean Renoir, a film director, wrote a book about his father, the painter Pierre-Auguste Renoir. In it he remembered his father saying that without paint in tubes there would have been no Cézanne, no Monet, no Sisley, no Pissarro, and nothing of what journalists later called Impressionism. But painters had carried paint outdoors before. In the 1780s, Pierre-Henri de Valenciennes painted little oil sketches of the sky over Rome. Tubes made it easier.

## 4. Japanese prints

**Label:** Japanese prints
**Year:** 1867 · precision: year (the fair opened 1 April) · kind: object
**Place:** Champ de Mars, Paris (48.86 N, 2.30 E)
**Replaces:** `lead-starry-2`

**Summary (36 words)**
1867. At a world's fair in Paris, Japan took part for the first time, and sent woodblock prints among much else. Over the next twenty years, painters in France collected Japanese prints, and some copied them. *More*

**More (287 words)**

In April 1867 a world's fair opened in Paris on the Champ de Mars, a great open field beside the river Seine, and countries from all over the world showed what they made. For the first time, Japan sent things to show: lacquer, pottery, paintings, and prints.

A Japanese woodblock print was the work of several people. An artist drew the picture. A carver pasted a thin copy of the drawing face down on a block of cherry wood and cut away everything that was not to print. Each colour needed a block of its own, with small marks cut in each block to keep the colours lined up. A printer inked the blocks one at a time and pressed the paper onto each, and a publisher, who had chosen the subject in the first place, sold the prints.

In Paris they looked like nothing else: flat colour, strong outlines, and views from odd places, high above a street or close up behind a branch. Collectors bought them, and in 1872 a critic named Philippe Burty made up a word for the craze, *japonisme* ("zha-po-NEESM").

Twenty years after the fair, Vincent van Gogh, a Dutch painter living in Paris with his brother Theo, collected hundreds of Japanese prints, many of them from the shop of a dealer named Siegfried Bing. In 1887 he copied two prints by the artist Hiroshige in oil paint, a plum orchard and a bridge in the rain.

In 1888 Theo wrote to Vincent about a wave by Hokusai, almost certainly *The Great Wave*, the most famous print of them all. Vincent wrote back and repeated his brother's words: "these waves are *claws*, the boat is caught in them, you can feel it."

## 5. Impression, Sunrise

**Label:** Impression, Sunrise
**Year:** 1874 · precision: year (the exhibition, 15 April to 15 May) · kind: object
**Place:** 35 boulevard des Capucines, Paris (48.87 N, 2.33 E)
**Replaces:** `lead-starry-3`

**Summary (33 words)**
1874. In Paris, about thirty artists put on an exhibition of their own. A critic made fun of a painting by Claude Monet, *Impression, Sunrise*, and called the painters "Impressionists." The name stuck. *More*

**More (312 words)**

```

      or 1874
            or 152 years ago.
```

In April 1874, in rooms on the boulevard des Capucines in Paris, hung a small painting of a harbour early in the morning. The harbour is half hidden in mist. There are dark boats on the water, the shapes of ships and cranes behind them, and a small orange sun, with its reflection laid across the water in short strokes of paint.

Claude Monet had painted it from a hotel window in Le Havre, a port in the north of France, probably in November 1872. Now it was in an exhibition that he and about thirty other artists had put on themselves.

In those days the way for a painter in France to be seen was the Salon, a huge exhibition in Paris, held every year, whose judges chose what was hung. The judges had turned down paintings by several of these artists. So they formed a company of their own, rented rooms that had belonged to the photographer Nadar, and hung more than 160 works. The show opened on 15 April and ran for a month.

The catalogue needed a title for the harbour. Monet remembered, twenty-four years later, that the picture could not really pass for a view of Le Havre, so he told them to call it *Impression*. In the catalogue it was *Impression, soleil levant*: Impression, Sunrise.

On 25 April a critic named Louis Leroy wrote a joke review in *Le Charivari*, a newspaper that made fun of things. In it, an imaginary old painter walks round the show getting angrier and angrier, and he says that wallpaper only half made is more finished than this seascape. Leroy called his review "L'Exposition des impressionnistes," the exhibition of the Impressionists.

He meant it as an insult. Four days later another critic, Jules Castagnary, used the same word in praise. By 1877 the painters had taken the name for themselves.

On 19 June 1878, a mare named Sallie Gardner galloped down a track at Palo Alto, in California, with a rider on her back. Beside the track stood twelve cameras in a row, and on the far side of the track, facing them, a sloping white screen.

People had drawn and painted horses for thousands of years, but nobody could say for certain what a horse's legs were doing, because they move too fast for an eye to follow. Many painters showed a galloping horse with its front legs stretched out ahead and its back legs stretched out behind, like a rocking horse. People argued about whether a galloping horse ever had all four hooves off the ground.

Leland Stanford, a rich railway owner and a former governor of California, bred horses on his farm at Palo Alto, and he wanted to know. He hired Eadweard Muybridge ("ED-wurd MY-bridge"), an English photographer who lived in San Francisco. Their first try, in 1873, gave a blurred picture of a trotting horse.

By 1878 Muybridge had made shutters fast enough to catch a moving leg. As Sallie Gardner ran past, she set off the cameras one after another. Muybridge said each shutter was open for one two-thousandth of a second.

The pictures came out small and dark, almost shadows. In one of them, all four of her hooves are off the ground. But her legs are not stretched out like a rocking horse's. They are folded up under her belly.

You may read that Stanford paid for all this to win a bet of $25,000. Nobody at the time wrote about a bet, and historians who have looked for one think it never happened.

Muybridge sold the pictures on cards called *The Horse in Motion*, and that October the magazine *Scientific American* put engravings made from them on its cover.

In February 1887, while workers were digging the foundations of a tower on the Champ de Mars in Paris, a newspaper called *Le Temps* printed a protest signed by about forty artists and writers. Among them were the writer Guy de Maupassant, the composer Charles Gounod, and Charles Garnier, who had designed the Paris opera house. They called the tower useless and monstrous, and compared it to a gigantic black factory chimney.

The tower was being built for a world's fair, to open in 1889, a hundred years after the French Revolution. Two engineers in Gustave Eiffel's company, Maurice Koechlin and Émile Nouguier, had the first idea for it, and an architect, Stephen Sauvestre, worked on how it would look. It was chosen from 107 designs.

It went up in a little over two years. In Eiffel's workshops outside Paris, 18,038 pieces of iron were cut and drilled, each to a drawing. They were carried to the site, where between 150 and 300 men fastened them together with two and a half million rivets. A rivet is a metal pin, pushed red-hot through holes in two pieces and hammered flat at the end, so that as it cools and shrinks it squeezes the pieces tight together. The iron alone weighed 7,300 tonnes.

When it was finished, at the end of March 1889, it was 300 metres tall, nearly twice the height of the Washington Monument, the tallest thing built before it. Nothing people built was taller until the Chrysler Building in New York, in 1930.

Eiffel's permit was for twenty years. After that, the tower belonged to the city of Paris, which could pull it down. So Eiffel looked for ways to make it useful. He set up experiments on the weather and on falling objects, and he paid for experiments with radio, and in 1909 the army set up a radio station at the tower. A tower that could send messages a long way was worth keeping. In 1910 the city let it stand.

On the evening of 28 December 1895, about thirty people each paid a franc and went down the stairs into the Salon Indien, a room beneath the Grand Café on the boulevard des Capucines in Paris. What they saw there was ten films, each about fifty seconds long, thrown onto a screen by a machine called the Cinématographe.

The brothers Louis and Auguste Lumière, who ran a factory in Lyon that made photographic plates, had patented the machine that February. It was a wooden box that could film, print and project, and it weighed less than five kilograms. They had already shown their films to small invited audiences. This was the first show in Paris that anyone could pay to see.

Here is the programme of that evening, in order. Workers leaving the Lumière factory at the end of the day. A man trying, again and again, to get up onto a horse. A baby fishing for goldfish in a bowl. Photographers stepping off a boat near Lyon. Blacksmiths at work. A gardener watering his garden, and a boy who steps on the hose. A baby eating breakfast. Men tossing someone in a blanket. A busy square in Lyon. Boys jumping into the sea.

There is no train on the programme.

You may have read that when the Lumières showed a film of a train coming into a station, the audience screamed and ran. The first known showing of a train film was in Lyon a month later, in January 1896, and the best-known version seems to have been filmed in 1897. Martin Loiperdinger, a German historian of film, points out that no report of a panic can be found in the newspapers of the time.

In the summer of 1896, the Russian writer Maxim Gorky saw the Lumières' films and wrote about the train: "Suddenly something clicks, everything vanishes and a train appears on the screen. It speeds straight at you — watch out!" Then he imagined it crashing into the room. He did not say that anyone ran.

In 1896 a physicist in Paris named Henri Becquerel found that uranium, a metal, gives off rays that nobody can see, which pass through black paper and fog a photographic plate. Marie Curie, a young physicist from Poland who was working in Paris, chose those rays as the subject of her doctorate.

The rays had a useful habit. Where they passed through air, they let a little electricity leak through it. Her husband, Pierre Curie, and his brother Jacques had built an instrument that could measure very small amounts of electricity, so she could measure the rays from a sample by how much electricity they let through.

In 1898 she tested every substance she could get. Two minerals that contained uranium, pitchblende and one called chalcolite, gave off far more rays than the uranium in them could explain.

So she made chalcolite herself, in the laboratory, from pure ingredients. Her chalcolite gave off no more rays than any other compound of uranium. The chalcolite from the ground must have something in it that hers did not. In April she wrote that the minerals might contain an element much more active than uranium.

That spring Pierre put his own work aside to join her. They ground pitchblende, dissolved it, and split it into its parts, measuring each part for rays. In July they announced one new element, which they named polonium, after Poland. In December, with Gustave Bémont, they announced a second, radium. They called substances like these radio-active.

They had no radium to show yet. There was so little of it in pitchblende that it took four more years, and tonnes of leftover ore, worked in a leaky wooden shed with a glass roof, to get a pinch of radium chloride, about a tenth of a gram. Marie Curie remembered going back to the shed in the evening and seeing their radium samples, set out on tables and shelves, glowing faintly in the dark.

In March 1989, Tim Berners-Lee, an English computer scientist, wrote a proposal at CERN ("SERN"), a great laboratory for physics that straddles the border between Switzerland and France, near Geneva. Thousands of people came and went at CERN, and information got lost: who had written which program, which notes went with which experiment. He suggested a way of linking documents, on any kind of computer, so that a reader could jump from one to another. His boss, Mike Sendall, wrote on the cover: "Vague but exciting…"

In the autumn of 1990 Berners-Lee wrote the programs to make it work, while Robert Cailliau, a Belgian engineer, helped him win support for it at CERN. There were three parts. Every page has an address, and a page can hold links, words that fetch the page at another address when you choose them. A server is a computer that keeps pages and hands them out when asked. A browser is a program that asks for pages and shows them.

By Christmas 1990 it all worked, on a NeXT computer at CERN. On the computer a label said: "This machine is a server. DO NOT POWER IT DOWN!!" The first website was at the address info.cern.ch, and its first page was about the web itself: what it was, and how to take part.

In August 1991 Berners-Lee told people outside CERN about it, in a message to an online discussion group. In April 1993 CERN gave the web's programs away, free, for anyone to use.

Nobody has a copy of the first page as it was in 1990. A page on a server is changed by writing over it, and the old version is gone. In 2013 CERN put back the oldest copy it could find, from a year or two later, at the old address, where you can still read it. CERN said it would keep looking for older ones.
