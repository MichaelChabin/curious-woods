## 1. Oldest grains

**Label:** Oldest grains
**Age:** 4567.3 Ma · ± 0.2 Ma · radiometric (uranium–lead) on the grains themselves; one group argues for about 4568.4
**Tail:** none — a dated moment

**Kind:** object
**Replaces:** sketch *A cloud lights up*

**Summary (33 words)**
About 4.57 billion years ago. Around the young Sun, in a flat disc of gas and dust, the oldest solid grains we know of formed. Some survive as white specks inside meteorites. *More*

**More (350 words)**

```
About 4.57 billion years ago
```

A little after one o'clock in the morning on 8 February 1969, a fireball lit the sky over the state of Chihuahua, in northern Mexico, and broke apart. Thousands of stones fell around the village of Pueblito de Allende and along a strip of country about fifty kilometres long. People from the villages, and later from museums, picked them up for years, and together they gathered two tonnes or more.

The stones are called the Allende meteorite. A meteorite is a piece of rock from space that reaches the ground, and most of them come from asteroids, small worlds that never grew into planets. Some of those worlds, like the one Allende came from, have hardly changed since they formed. Broken open, a piece of Allende is dark grey, and scattered through it are white specks, some of them as big as a fingernail.

The white specks are older than the Earth.

Before there was a Sun, there was a cloud of gas and dust. Part of the cloud fell in on itself, the middle grew hot enough to shine, and around it a flat disc of gas and dust went round and round. Close to the young Sun the disc was so hot that only a few stubborn substances, rich in calcium and aluminium, could turn solid. The white specks are made of those.

Their age comes from uranium, which slowly turns into lead at a rate that never changes. Measure how much of each a speck holds, and you can work out when it formed: about 4.567 billion years ago, give or take a couple of hundred thousand years. One group of scientists thinks the specks are a million years older than that, and the argument is still going.

People who study the solar system use the age of these specks as its birthday.

Robert Hazen, a mineralogist, reckons there were only about a dozen kinds of mineral in the dust of the cloud the Sun was born from. Today the list of minerals known on Earth has more than six thousand names, and it grows every year.

## 2. Gathering

**Label:** Gathering
**Age:** 4540 Ma · ± 50 Ma · a model age, from lead in the Earth's oldest ores compared with lead in meteorites
**Tail:** none — the planet itself
**Known from:** tungsten and lead in rocks and meteorites, and computer models; no rock survives
**Place now:** none
**Kind:** earth event
**Replaces:** sketch *Gathering*

**Summary (36 words)**
About 4.54 billion years ago. Dust, pebbles and small worlds circling the young Sun had gathered into a planet, the Earth, hot enough to melt. Its iron sank to the middle and made the core. *More*

**More (328 words)**

```
About 4.54 billion years ago
```

In many museums there is a slab of metal from space, sawn flat, polished, and washed with acid. The acid brings out a pattern of crossing bands, like woven straw, which forms only when once-melted iron and nickel cool very slowly, over millions of years.

A slab like that is a piece of the core of a small world.

The disc of gas and dust around the young Sun did not stay dust for long. The grains stuck together into pebbles, the pebbles into bodies kilometres across, and those crashed into each other and grew. Many grew big enough, and hot enough inside, to melt. When a small world melts, its iron, which is heavier than rock, sinks to the middle. Some of those small worlds were later smashed apart, and their cores come down to us as iron meteorites.

The Earth was built the same way, from many such bodies, and the crashes kept it hot enough to melt. Its iron sank, too, and made the core, which is still there under our feet, about 2,900 kilometres down.

How long did the gathering take?

There is a clock for it. A rare metal called hafnium slowly turns into tungsten, and hafnium stays in rock while tungsten goes with iron. So the tungsten left in the rock of the Earth's mantle says when the iron left for the core. By that clock, which two teams read in 2002, the core had formed within about thirty million years. Some scientists in Copenhagen think the Earth grew most of the way in only five million years, from pebbles; others think the last crashes went on for a hundred million. Each side has good evidence.

The iron at the centre is partly liquid still, and its churning makes the Earth's magnetic field, the one a compass needle feels. When the field began is another argument, and the oldest rocks that seem to record it are in Greenland, 3.7 billion years old.

## 3. Big thwack

**Label:** Moon forms
**Line:** earth
**Age:** 4510 Ma · argued, 4510 to 4350 Ma · radiometric on moon rocks and their crystals, which give the youngest the Moon could be
**Tail:** none — a dated moment, if the date could be settled
**Known from:** moon rocks brought back by Apollo, and computer models of the crash
**Place now:** Lunar Sample Laboratory Facility, Johnson Space Center, Houston, Texas (29.56 N, 95.08 W)
**Kind:** earth event
**Replaces:** sketch *The big thwack*

**Summary (38 words)**
About 4.5 billion years ago. Something perhaps the size of Mars probably hit the young Earth, and rock flung into space gathered into the Moon. When it happened, and how big the something was, are still argued. *More*

**More (344 words)**

```
About 4.5 billion years ago
```

In a building in Houston, Texas, the rocks the Apollo astronauts brought back from the Moon are kept in steel cabinets filled with pure nitrogen, which keeps the air from changing them. People handle them through thick gloves set into the cabinet walls.

The rocks raised a puzzle at once. In some ways the Moon is very like the Earth's rock and in others not like it at all.

Oxygen comes in kinds that weigh slightly different amounts, and the planets and asteroids we have pieces of each have their own mix, almost like a fingerprint. The Moon's mix matches the Earth's, so closely that the best measurements, in 2025, could find no difference. Yet the Moon has only a small iron core, and the Earth has a big one.

The idea most scientists favour is a crash. Near the end of the Earth's gathering, a body perhaps the size of Mars hit it a glancing blow. Rock from the outside of both was flung into space, and it gathered into the Moon within about a year. The iron of both stayed in the Earth. Some people call the body Theia. Nobody knows its size, and other models have two half-Earths colliding, or an Earth spinning so fast that a day lasted two or three hours.

Each model can explain the fingerprint only by asking for something special, and scientists still argue over which to believe.

The newborn Moon was perhaps fifteen or twenty times closer than it is now, and it would have looked that many times wider in the sky. The day was about five hours long.

When the crash happened is argued too. Crystals from Apollo 14 rocks say at least 4.51 billion years ago; other moon rocks say about 4.35. In 2024 one team suggested that the Earth's pull on the young Moon heated and reset the younger rocks.

The Moon is still moving away, about 3.8 centimetres a year. We know because Apollo crews left mirrors on the Moon, and people on Earth still bounce laser light off them.

## 4. Black Earth

**Label:** Black Earth
**Age:** 4450 Ma · ± 50 Ma · an estimate; nothing from the time survives to be dated
**Tail:** none
**Known from:** nothing that survives from the time; computer models, and chemical traces in younger rocks
**Place now:** none
**Kind:** earth event
**Replaces:** sketch *Black Earth*

**Summary (37 words)**
About 4.45 billion years ago. After the crash that made the Moon, the Earth's melted surface probably cooled into a crust of dark rock under an air thick with steam. No rock from this time survives. *More*

**More (319 words)**

```
About 4.45 billion years ago
```

Nobody can see this part.

No rock from this time is left anywhere on Earth. The planet's skin has been melted, broken, sunk and remade so many times since that every piece of it has gone. What we know comes from computer models, built by scientists who start from the laws of heat and gas and work forward, and from a few chemical traces in much younger rocks.

Here is what one careful model says. Kevin Zahnle, a planetary scientist at NASA, and his colleagues worked it out in 2007.

After the crash that made the Moon, the Earth's surface was melted rock, glowing. The air was full of rock vapour, and from space the Earth would have looked like a small star wrapped in burning cloud. As it cooled, the melted surface froze, and that took only about two million years. Later a thick layer of basalt, the dark rock that lava sets into, built up on top, and its heavy bottom kept breaking off and sinking into the hot rock below.

The air above it was steam and carbon dioxide, breathed out by the rock. When the surface was cool enough, the steam fell as rain, about a metre a year, until the oceans were full. What was left was an air of carbon dioxide so thick that it pressed down about a hundred times as hard as our air does, at a temperature over two hundred degrees Celsius.

Other models give other numbers. Some have the surface staying melted much longer, under a thick blanket of air. Nobody can check any of them against a rock.

Robert Hazen calls this time Black Earth.

The closest anyone has come to it is in Greenland. Rocks near Isua, about 3.8 billion years old, carry a faint chemical signature showing that the inside of the Earth had already sorted itself into layers in its first few hundred million years.

## 5. Blue Earth

**Label:** Oceans form
**Line:** earth
**Age:** 4404 Ma · ± 8 Ma · radiometric (uranium–lead) on the crystal itself
**Tail:** 4500 Ma — the models in which the steam rained out within a few million years of the Moon's making

**Kind:** object
**Replaces:** sketch *Blue Earth*

**Summary (36 words)**
About 4.4 billion years ago. A tiny crystal formed that still survives, in a hill in Western Australia. It is the oldest piece of the Earth yet found, and its oxygen hints at liquid water. *More*

**More (345 words)**

```
About 4.4 billion years ago
      and probably begun by 4.5 billion years ago.
```

Erawondoo Hill is a low hill in the Jack Hills, in the Murchison country of Western Australia. Along it runs a band of old river rock: pebbles of quartz set in sand that a river laid down about three billion years ago.

In the sand are tiny crystals of a mineral called zircon.

Zircon grows in melted rock as it cools, and it is very hard, so it outlasts the rock it grew in. When that rock wears away, the zircons wash out into rivers and settle somewhere new. Zircon also takes in uranium as it grows and shuts out lead, so any lead inside a zircon came from its uranium, and the zircon is a clock.

Geologists crush the river rock, sort out the zircons, and set them in a disc of plastic. Then a beam of charged atoms, fired from a machine called an ion microprobe, digs a hole in each crystal too small to see and weighs what comes out.

In 2001 Simon Wilde, a geologist at Curtin University in Perth, and his colleagues reported a crystal about as wide as two hairs that had formed 4.404 billion years ago. It is the oldest piece of the Earth yet found.

Its oxygen tells something more. The crystal is slightly rich in the heavier kind of oxygen, and that usually happens when rock has sat at the surface in cool liquid water before being buried and melted. So there was probably water at the surface, perhaps oceans, less than a hundred and fifty million years after the Earth formed.

Not everyone agrees. Some geologists think water could have changed the crystal much later. In 2024 a study picked out more than a hundred of these zircons that later water had not touched, and they still held the heavy oxygen. Many geologists now think the water was there.

At the Australian National University, a machine has looked through more than a hundred thousand zircons from these hills. About one in twenty is older than four billion years.

Robert Hazen calls this time Blue Earth.

**Pictures wanted:** the crystal itself, glowing in a cathodoluminescence picture (John Valley, University of Wisconsin; licence to ask); Erawondoo Hill (Commons or GSWA; licence to check).

- John Valley, William Peck, Elizabeth King and Simon Wilde, "A cool early Earth," *Geology*, 2002. Water and mild conditions from 4.4 billion.

**Place now:** Acasta River, Northwest Territories, Canada (65.17 N, 115.57 W)
**Kind:** object
**Replaces:** sketch *Gray Earth*

**Summary (38 words)**
About 4.03 billion years ago. Rock formed that still survives, beside the Acasta River in northern Canada. It is the oldest rock that nearly every geologist accepts. Rock in Nunavik may be older; that is still argued. *More*

**More (328 words)**

```
About 4.03 billion years ago
      and probably begun by 4.4 billion years ago.
```

About three hundred kilometres north of Yellowknife, on the Acasta River, there is an outcrop of grey rock striped light and dark, with a few small garnets in it. It is a gneiss ("nice"), a rock that has been squeezed and heated deep underground until its minerals lined up in bands.

In 1989 Samuel Bowring, an American geologist, and his colleagues dated zircons from the outcrop, and later work put the oldest of the rock at 4.031 billion years. Nothing older that everyone accepts has been found anywhere.

The oldest part of it is called the Idiwhaa gneiss. *Idiwhaa* is a Tłı̨chǫ word, and it means "ancient."

The Earth's first crust was basalt, the dark, heavy rock that lava sets into. Granite is different: pale, lighter, and made when older rock, basalt among it, is melted again, often with water. Because granite is lighter it rides higher, the way a thick, light raft floats higher than a thin heavy one, and continents are made mostly of rock like it. The Idiwhaa gneiss was made when hot melt rose close to the surface and melted in older dark rock that water had already soaked, much as new rock is made in Iceland today.

But is it the oldest rock?

On the east shore of Hudson Bay, near Inukjuak, in Nunavik, there is a belt of old rock called Nuvvuagittuq. In 2025 O'Neil's team dated rock that cuts through the old rock, at about 4.16 billion, by two methods that agree. If they are right, the rock it cuts through is older still. Not everyone is convinced yet.

In 2024 the Inuit of Inukjuak, through their landholding corporation, stopped geologists taking any more rock from the site, because so much had been chipped away, and some of it had turned up for sale online.

On the Moon there is a dark plain about 1,200 kilometres across. It is a crater, later filled with lava. It is called Mare Imbrium, the Sea of Showers.

In 1974, three scientists at Caltech, in California, dated rocks the Apollo crews had brought back. Many of the rocks had been melted by impacts, and the melting had reset their clocks. Again and again they gave the same age: about 3.9 billion years.

The scientists, Fouad Tera, Dimitri Papanastassiou and Gerald Wasserburg, suggested that something had sent a storm of huge rocks through the inner solar system, all within a short time. They called it a cataclysm. If it hit the Moon, it hit the Earth too, and the Earth is the bigger target.

In 1989 four American scientists worked out what such hits would have done to the Earth. A rock a few hundred kilometres across, they said, would boil the oceans away.

For many years the storm was in the books.

Then people looked again at where the Apollo rocks came from. Mare Imbrium is close to several of the landing sites, and the blow that made it threw melted rock across a great part of the Moon. So many of the rocks that said "3.9 billion" may record one blow, not a storm. Other scientists argued that the way the rocks were dated could make a spike that was not there.

Many scientists now think the hits came over a long time, slowly growing fewer, perhaps with a burst somewhere in it. The size of rock needed to boil the oceans has grown too: in 2022 it was put at more than seven hundred kilometres across.

The Earth has kept no crater from this time. The oldest signs of big hits are thin layers of tiny round beads, each about the size of a fine grain of sand, made when rock vaporised by an impact cooled and fell like hail, in rocks in South Africa and Western Australia. Those are about 3.5 to 3.2 billion years old, and nobody knows how many older ones have been lost.

West of Marble Bar, in the Pilbara, Six Mile Creek cuts through a band of grey and white rock. In the rock are shapes: low domes, steep cones, waves, and something that looks like the bottom of an egg carton. They run for more than ten kilometres.

The shapes are stromatolites ("stro-MAT-o-lites").

A stromatolite is built by microbes, living things so small that millions fit on a fingernail. They grow in a mat on the bottom of shallow water, and the mat is sticky. Sand and mud settle on it and stick, and the microbes grow up through the new layer toward the light, and the next layer settles on them. Layer by layer, over years, the mat builds a mound of stone.

Stromatolites are still being built today in Hamelin Pool, in Shark Bay, on the coast of Western Australia, where the water is about twice as salty as the sea and few animals can live to graze on the mats.

In 2006 Abigail Allwood, an Australian geologist, and her colleagues described seven different kinds of shape at Strelley Pool. They argued that so many shapes, each suited to its place on an old shallow seafloor, could only have been built by living things. Nearly everyone now agrees. Rocks above and below the mounds show they are between about 3.43 and 3.35 billion years old.

Is life older than that?

Probably. Several older finds have been claimed, and every one is argued over. In Greenland, small peaks in 3.7-billion-year-old rock were called stromatolites in 2016, and two years later another team showed they could be folds made by squeezing. In Labrador and in Nunavik there are traces of carbon and tiny tubes in rock at least 3.7 billion years old, and perhaps much older. And in 2015 a team found a speck of carbon sealed inside a zircon from the Jack Hills, 4.1 billion years old, that looks like carbon left by life. They called it "potentially" life, and that is as far as anyone can go.

Kenora is a town on Lake of the Woods, in northwestern Ontario. The Anishinaabe name for the place is Wauzhushk Onigum, "the portage to the country of the muskrats," and the town was once called Rat Portage. In 1905 the town took a new name, made from the first letters of Keewatin, Norman and Rat Portage.

The rock under the town is very old. It belongs to the Superior craton, the largest of the very old pieces of continent left on Earth. A craton ("CRAY-ton") is the old, stable heart of a continent, a piece that has survived since it formed while the land around it was broken up and remade. Mountains were built here about 2.7 billion years ago, and geologists named that time of mountain building the Kenoran, after the town.

In 1991 a group of geologists suggested that the Superior craton and the other old cratons had then been joined into one great continent. They called it Kenorland.

How could anyone tell?

When a continent is pulled apart, melted rock rises into the cracks and freezes there as walls of dark rock called dykes. So if two cratons were once one, their dykes should match: the same ages, in the same order. Rock also keeps a little magnetism from the time it froze, pointing the way the Earth's field pointed, and that can say how far north or south a piece once lay.

In 2003 Wouter Bleeker, a geologist at the Geological Survey of Canada, looked at the dykes. The Superior craton has dykes about 2.45 billion years old. The Slave craton, the old rock around Yellowknife, has none of those, and has dykes about 2.2 billion years old instead. So the two broke away from their neighbours at different times. Bleeker thought there were several smaller groups, not one continent.

He called his paper "a puzzle in ca. 35 pieces," because that is about how many pieces of crust that old survive. Nobody has fitted them together yet.

In 2004 a drilling rig in the Hamersley Range, in the Pilbara, drilled down through old seafloor and brought up a long stick of rock, a core, nearly a kilometre of it. Between about a hundred and two hundred metres down, the core passes through dark shale, mud that settled on a seafloor about 2.5 billion years ago and turned to stone.

The scientists were looking for oxygen.

Almost all the oxygen in the air today was made by living things, and the first to make it were probably cyanobacteria ("sigh-AN-o-bacteria"), tiny cells that live in water and use sunlight to make food. To do it they need hydrogen, and they pull it out of water, which is hydrogen and oxygen joined. The oxygen is thrown away.

For a long time, the oxygen they threw away vanished. Iron dissolved in the sea took it, and so did the gases from volcanoes and the fresh rock of the land. Only when those were used up could oxygen stay in the air, and in rock that is plain from about 2.4 billion years ago.

So when did the oxygen-making start?

In 2007 a team led by Ariel Anbar, a geochemist in Arizona, reported metals in the shale that rivers carry to the sea only when oxygen in the air is wearing away the rocks. They called it a whiff of oxygen, about seventy million years before oxygen first built up in the air. In 2022 another team looked at the same core and said the metals had been added much later, after the mud had become rock. In 2023 Anbar's team answered. Nobody has won.

Older evidence has had a harder time. Chemicals found in 1999 in rocks 2.7 billion years old, said to come from cyanobacteria, turned out in 2015 to have got into the rock much later. Rocks in South Africa, nearly 3 billion years old, hold signs of oxygen in shallow water, and that evidence has so far held up. The clocks in living cells' genes say somewhere between 3.4 and 2.9 billion years.
