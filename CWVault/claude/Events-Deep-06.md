# Deep time, batch 6: beads, paint and flutes

Written in round 3 of Prompt-Deep-Time-Events.md and checked in round 4. Dates count *ago* only (Rulings-Sept-2026.md, Deep time), here in thousands of years. Each event's "Notes for us" gives the beat, what the research pass found, and what the checkers must look at.

Changes from the candidate list, in short. The research moved four ages. *Sulawesi* goes from "50,000 or more" to at least 67,800, on a hand stencil on Muna dated in January 2026. *Lebombo bone* goes from 35,000 (the 1970s figure, still common) to about 43,000. *Lady of Brassempouy* goes from 25,000 to about 30,000: the 25,000 was in radiocarbon years, and the museum's own date is about 30,000 years ago. *Lascaux* goes from 17,000 to about 21,000 (Ducasse and Langlais 2019). *Altamira*, with its dot on the bison at about 17,000, becomes the youngest mark, and the order is now Lascaux, Ishango, Altamira. *El Castillo* (40,800) falls between the flutes and the Venus. The *Lebombo bone* is not told as a calendar: that reading is popular, not scholarly.

Tails. Almost none. Most of these marks are places, dated, not firsts. Where they are firsts (beads, engraving, flutes, carving), the older evidence is argued, and a tail would say "probably begun by", which nobody can say yet. The candidates are named in the notes (Trinil for engraving, Divje babe for flutes, the Spanish minimum ages for painting) for Michael to decide.

What the checkers caught, in short. *Blombos Cave* had the drawing backwards: six lines crossed by three. *Chauvet* had Éliette Brunel seeing a mammoth; the Ministry's account says two small red lines, and her words were "Ils sont venus!" without *ici*; five numbers about the cave were cut for want of a source. *El Castillo* said thirty-six specialists replied in 2020; it was forty-four, in 2019. *Shell beads* had the dating crust over the beads; it grew within their layer, above most of them, and the wear is round the shell's mouth, not the hole. *Lebombo bone*'s four tools on four occasions turn out to be said of this very bone, so the More now says so. *Hohle Fels Venus* is "the oldest carving of a person that nobody disputes", not simply the oldest. *Sulawesi* "may be" the oldest cave painting. *Altamira*'s red sign is at least 35,600, not 36,000, and the experts' charge of forgery is now sourced. *Lascaux*'s new dates are on reindeer bone, and the old 17,000 was radiocarbon years. *Ishango*: counting in twelves, not tens and twelves. *Brassempouy* moves to about 30,000; BCE is gone from its lines. A finding not used: a 2026 paper on Font-de-Gaume found charcoal in black paint thought to be pure mineral, which may one day date Lascaux's paint.

**More (346 words)**

Bizmoune ("biz-MOON") Cave is in the hills of western Morocco, about twelve kilometres from the Atlantic coast, near the town of Essaouira ("ess-a-WEER-a"). Between 2014 and 2018 a team that included Abdeljalil Bouzouggar, of Morocco's national institute of archaeology in Rabat, Steven Kuhn and Philippe Fernandez dug into its floor. In one layer they found thirty-three small sea-snail shells, each a little over a centimetre long. Every one had a hole in it.

A hole alone proves nothing, because the sea, and other animals, can hole a shell. So the team, with El Mehdi Sehasseh leading the study of the shells, looked closely. The edges of many holes were chipped, as if worked with a stone tool. Three in four were polished and scratched around the shell's open mouth, where a string would rub. Some were stained with red ochre, a coloured earth. And the snails lived in the sea, but someone had carried their shells up to the cave from a coast that was then thirty to fifty kilometres away. From all this the team argued that people had strung the shells and worn them.

How old are they? In the same layer, water dripping in the cave had built a small lump of stone, and most of the beads lay deeper down than it. Stone like that holds a little uranium, which turns into thorium at a steady rate, so a laboratory can tell how long ago it began to grow. This lump began about 142,000 years ago, so the beads below it are probably older still.

But the team also dated the sand of the same layer another way, by the light stored in its grains, and got younger ages, between about 109,000 and 54,000 years. They said so plainly, and said that the difference needs more work.

So the oldest beads yet found may be these, or they may not. Pierced shells from Skhul ("SKOOL"), a cave in Israel, are between about 100,000 and 135,000 years old, and shells from the Grotte des Pigeons, at Taforalt, in northeastern Morocco, are about 82,000.

## 2. Blombos Cave

**Label:** Blombos Cave
**Age:** 0.077 Ma · ± 0.006 Ma (to confirm) · thermoluminescence on burnt stone tools from the same layer as the two engraved pieces of ochre; the layer above dated by luminescence on its sand to about 70,000 (Henshilwood 2002). **More (311 words)**

Blombos ("BLOM-boss") Cave is in a cliff above the sea near Still Bay, in the Western Cape, about three hundred kilometres east of Cape Town. Christopher Henshilwood spent holidays near there as a boy, and in 1991, as an archaeologist looking for a place to dig, he came back and found the cave.

Under the layers left by people in the last two thousand years are far older ones, and in them the team found lumps of ochre, a soft red stone that people have long ground up for paint. Two of the lumps, reported in 2002, had been scraped and ground flat, and then cut with a band of lines that cross one another. Burnt stone tools from the same layer were dated by the light they give off when heated again, to about 77,000 years ago.

In 2004 the team reported shells of a small snail that lives where rivers meet the sea, each pierced with a hole, some with traces of ochre, about 75,000 years old. They are worn in just the places a string would wear them.

In 2011 the team dug up a flake of silcrete ("SIL-kreet"), a hard stone, about four centimetres long. Later, in a laboratory in Cape Town, Luca Pollarolo was sorting flakes when he noticed red lines on this one: six lines crossed by three others. The team tried drawing on stone with ochre themselves, and argued that the lines had been drawn with a sharpened piece of ochre, like a crayon, about 73,000 years ago. The lines stop at the broken edges of the flake, so the drawing was once bigger.

Did the crossed lines mean something to the people who made them? The team argues that they did, because the same pattern turns up again and again, on ochre and on stone, over thousands of years. Nobody can be sure.

## 3. Sulawesi

**Label:** Sulawesi

**More (341 words)**

```

```

On Muna ("MOO-na"), an island off the southeast of Sulawesi, in Indonesia, is a cave called Liang Metanduno ("LEE-ang meh-tan-DOO-no"). It is a wide room open to the daylight, and its walls are covered with paintings in red: horses, boats, people.

In 2015 Adhi Agus Oktaviana, an Indonesian archaeologist, found something fainter among them: hand stencils. A stencil is made by holding a hand flat against the rock and blowing paint around it, so that its shape is left behind, bare rock in a cloud of red.

How do you date paint? Water seeping down a cave wall can leave a thin crust of mineral, and a crust that grew over a painting must be younger than the painting. The crust holds a little uranium, which turns into thorium at a steady rate, so a laboratory can tell when the crust began to grow: the least the painting can be.

In January 2026 Oktaviana, Maxime Aubert, Adam Brumm and their team reported that the crust over one stencil had begun to grow about 71,600 years ago, give or take 3,800, so the stencil is at least 67,800 years old. The tip of one finger looks narrowed, as if paint was added or the hand was moved. It may be the oldest cave painting yet dated anywhere.

In the limestone hills of Maros and Pangkep, near Makassar, the same team has dated paintings of other kinds. In 2017 Basran Burhan found a painting of a warty pig more than a metre long in a cave called Leang Tedongnge ("LEH-ang teh-DONG-ngeh"); it is at least 45,500 years old. In Leang Karampuang a pig stands among three figures that look like people, at least 51,200 years ago.

Not everyone trusts these crusts. Water can wash uranium out of one, and that makes it look older than it is. In May 2026 Georges Sauvet, a French specialist in cave art, argued that, in a race for the oldest date, very old ages are being accepted too easily; the team answers that it sampled with care.

## 4. Lebombo bone

**Label:** Lebombo bone
**Age:** 0.043 Ma · ± 0.001 Ma · calibrated radiocarbon on the layers and on things of plant and bone in them, about 44,000 to 42,000 (d'Errico 2012, Villa 2012), not on the bone itself; once given as about 35,000 (Beaumont, 1970s)
**Tail:** none
**Known from:** notched bones, beads and tools in the layers of one rock shelter
**Place now:** Border Cave, Lebombo Mountains, KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa, on the border with Eswatini (27.02 S, 31.99 E)
**Kind:** object
**Replaces:** a new mark (Michael, 7 Oct)

**Summary (34 words)**
About 43,000 years ago. Someone in a cave in the Lebombo Mountains, on what is now the border of South Africa and Eswatini, cut twenty-nine notches across a baboon's leg bone. Nobody knows why. *More*

**More (294 words)**

```
About 43,000 years ago.
```

Border Cave is a rock shelter in a cliff of the Lebombo ("leh-BOM-bo") Mountains, where KwaZulu-Natal, in South Africa, meets Eswatini, about twelve kilometres from Ingwavuma ("ing-gwa-VOO-ma"). People lived in it, on and off, for more than two hundred thousand years, and what they left is still in its floor: grass they slept on, beads of ostrich eggshell, bone points like the arrowheads the San made much later, a lump of beeswax.

The archaeologist Peter Beaumont dug there in the 1970s and found many of the things the cave is known for. Among his finds was a small bone, about eight centimetres long, from the leg of a baboon. Twenty-nine notches had been cut across it, one below another.

How old is it? Charcoal, wood, beeswax and an eggshell bead from the same layers have been dated by their carbon, and they put the bone at about 44,000 to 42,000 years old. It was once thought to be about 35,000, and some books still say so.

What were the notches for? A notch is easy to make and hard to read. Some have called the bone a calendar of the moon, and some the oldest piece of mathematics, but both are guesses.

Francesco d'Errico looked at its notches under a microscope, and argued that they were cut with four different tools, on four separate occasions, as if someone came back to add to them. That would make the bone a record of something: a tally, a count of things or days. Others point out that in Australia, Aboriginal message sticks carry rows of notches that helped a messenger remember a message, and counted nothing at all.

So the twenty-nine notches may count something, or they may not. What they count, if anything, nobody knows.

In the Swabian Jura, in the southwest of Germany, deep valleys run through the limestone, and there are caves high in their walls. Geißenklösterle ("GUY-sen-KLER-ster-leh") is one of them, in the valley of the Ach, near Blaubeuren ("blow-BOY-ren"). Archaeologists dug in it, on and off, from the 1970s to 2002.

Among what they found were pieces of the wing bones of swans, made into flutes. And there were thirty-one small pieces of mammoth ivory which, fitted back together, made another flute, about nineteen centimetres long, with at least three finger holes. It was reported in 2004.

A bird's wing bone is already a tube. A mammoth's tusk is solid, and curved. To make a flute of it, the maker must have split a length of tusk down the middle, hollowed out each half, and then fitted the two halves back together so closely that the seam would not leak air.

How old are they? In 2012 Tom Higham, Nicholas Conard and their colleagues dated bones from the layers, cleaned with great care so that no modern carbon remained, and found that the layers the flutes lie in began about 43,000 to 42,000 years ago, older than had been thought.

In 2008, in Hohle Fels ("HOH-leh FELS"), another cave a few kilometres away, Conard's team from the University of Tübingen found twelve pieces of a flute made from the wing bone of a griffon vulture. Put together, it is almost twenty-two centimetres long and has five finger holes, with two notches cut into one end, probably where the player blew. It is about 40,000 years old.

Is there anything older? In 1995, in Divje babe ("DEEV-yeh BAH-beh"), a cave in Slovenia, Ivan Turk's team found the thigh bone of a young cave bear with holes in it, from a time when Neanderthals lived there, about 50,000 to 60,000 years ago. His team argues that it is a flute. Others argue that the holes were bitten by hyenas. The argument has gone on for more than thirty years.

Above the village of Puente Viesgo ("PWEN-teh VYES-go"), in Cantabria, in the north of Spain, is a cone-shaped hill called Monte Castillo, and inside it are caves. In 1903 Hermilio Alcalde del Río ("er-MEE-lyo al-KAL-deh del REE-o"), who ran the school of arts and crafts in Torrelavega and searched the caves of Cantabria, found paintings in one of them, El Castillo ("el kas-TEE-yo"). People have counted more than two thousand images there since, among them more than eighty hands, outlined in red on the rock.

On one wall, now called the Panel of Hands, the hand stencils are mixed with red discs.

In 2012 Alistair Pike and his team dated the paintings in eleven caves in Spain. They used the thin crusts of mineral that water had laid over the paint. A crust holds a little uranium, which turns into thorium at a steady rate, so its date is the least the painting under it can be. At El Castillo, the crust over one red disc was at least 40,800 years old, and the crust over a hand stencil at least 37,300.

Who painted the disc? People of our own kind were arriving in Europe about then, and Neanderthals were living in Spain. Pike said either might have done it; others thought our kind far more likely.

In 2018 Dirk Hoffmann and his team dated crusts in three other caves, and found one over a sign shaped like a ladder in La Pasiega ("la pa-SYEH-ga"), on the same hill, that was at least 64,800 years old. That is before our kind is known to have reached Europe, so they argued that Neanderthals had painted it.

Other specialists replied that water can wash uranium out of a crust and make it look older than it is. In 2019 forty-four of them wrote that there was "still no archaeological evidence" that Neanderthals made Iberian cave art. Hoffmann's team replied. The argument is open.

El Castillo is open to visitors, with a guide.

Hohle Fels ("HOH-leh FELS") means "hollow rock." It is a cave near the small town of Schelklingen ("SHELK-ling-en"), in the valley of the Ach, in the Swabian Jura, in the southwest of Germany.

Fitted together, the pieces make a woman about six centimetres tall, small enough to close a hand around. She is nearly whole: only her left arm and shoulder are missing. Her breasts are large, her hips are wide, and her arms are short, with the hands on her belly. Where her head should be there is a small ring, pierced through, so she may have hung from a cord.

That same month, about seventy centimetres away and in the same layer, the team found the pieces of a flute made from a vulture's wing bone.

How old is she? The ivory has not been dated, but bones from the layer she lay in have, by their carbon, and she is at least 35,000 years old and probably about 40,000.

She is the oldest carving of a person that nobody disputes. In another cave not far away, Hohlenstein-Stadel, people of about the same time carved a figure in ivory that stands like a person but has the head of a lion; it is kept in Ulm. Paintings of figures that look like people are older: on Sulawesi, in Indonesia, they are at least 51,200 years old.

Archaeologists call small carved women like this one *Venus* figures, after the Roman goddess of love. What she meant to the people who carved her, nobody knows.

On a Sunday afternoon in December 1994, three cavers, Jean-Marie Chauvet ("show-VAY"), Éliette Brunel and Christian Hillaire, were walking along the cliffs near the Pont d'Arc, a great natural arch of rock over the river Ardèche ("ar-DESH"), in the south of France. At a small opening in the rock they felt a faint draught of air, and a draught means a cave behind. They cleared away the stones and climbed down a ladder into the dark.

In the beam of her lamp Éliette Brunel saw two small lines of red ochre on the rock. "Ils sont venus!" she called: "They came!"

They had found a long cave, painted and drawn on for much of its length. On Christmas Eve they went back and laid strips of plastic along a single path, so that nobody would step on the floor, where the bones of animals lay, and the hollows where cave bears had slept.

There are hundreds of animals on its walls, and many of them are dangerous ones: lions, mammoths, rhinoceroses, bears. In a painted cave that is unusual.

How old are they? Many are drawn in charcoal, and charcoal can be dated by its carbon. In 2016 Anita Quiles, Hélène Valladas and their team put together more than 350 dates from the cave, and found that people used it at two times: between about 37,000 and 33,500 years ago, and again between about 31,000 and 28,000. The charcoal drawings that have been dated belong to the first.

Then rock fell from the cliff above the entrance, and sealed the cave.

It has been kept closed since 1994, to protect it. A copy of it opened nearby in 2015, and anyone can visit that.

Brassempouy ("brah-sahn-poo-EE") is a village in the Landes, in the southwest of France, and in a wood near it, beside a small stream, is a small cave called the Grotte du Pape, the Pope's Cave.

She is kept at the national archaeology museum, in a château at Saint-Germain-en-Laye, near Paris. She is kept in a room that opens only by appointment, because ivory is easily damaged. In Brassempouy, at the Maison de la Dame, visitors can see a copy.

On 8 September 1940, on a hill above the town of Montignac ("mon-tee-NYAK"), in the Dordogne, in the southwest of France, Marcel Ravidat ("rah-vee-DAH"), a young apprentice mechanic, found a hole where a tree had fallen. Four days later he came back with three others, Jacques Marsal, Georges Agniel ("ahn-YEL") and Simon Coencas. Simon was thirteen. His family had come to Montignac from near Paris that June, to take refuge from the war.

Ravidat went down first. At the bottom was a hall seventeen metres long, and on its walls and ceiling were animals: horses, deer, and bulls, painted in black and red and yellow. The largest bull is more than five metres long. It is said to be the largest animal painted in any cave yet found.

The four told their teacher, Léon Laval, and he came down on the eighteenth. Within days the Abbé Henri Breuil, a priest who studied painted caves, was there too.

The cave opened to the public in 1948. Many thousands came, and the warmth and breath of so many visitors, with the light, brought green growths on the walls. In 1963 André Malraux, France's minister of culture, closed it. A copy of the hall and its paintings was made nearby, and opened in 1983. A fuller copy opened in 2016, in Montignac.

How old are the paintings? The black paint is made mostly from manganese minerals, with no charcoal in it, so it has never been dated directly. But the people who painted left things on the floor: bits of bone and antler, and charcoal. In 2019 Sylvain Ducasse and Mathieu Langlais dated pieces of reindeer bone from the floor, and put the people's visits at about 21,500 to 21,000 years ago. Older books say about 17,000. That figure was in radiocarbon years, which run short of ordinary years this far back.

Simon Coencas, the last of the four, died in 2020, at ninety-three.

Ishango ("ee-SHAHNG-go") is on the shore of Lake Edward, near where the Semliki River ("sem-LEE-kee") flows out of it, in Virunga ("vee-ROONG-ga") National Park, in the east of the Democratic Republic of the Congo. In 1950, when the Congo was ruled by Belgium, Jean de Heinzelin, a Belgian geologist, dug there and found the layers of a camp where people had lived long ago. Among what they left was a bone about ten centimetres long, now dark brown. A small chip of quartz is set into one end, so it may have been a tool for engraving.

Along the bone are three rows of notches, cut in groups. De Heinzelin counted them like this.

One row: 11, 13, 17, 19.
Another: 3, 6, 4, 8, 10, 5, 5, 7.
The third: 11, 21, 19, 9.

Add up the first row and you get 60. The third also comes to 60. The middle one comes to 48.

The numbers 11, 13, 17 and 19 are the only prime numbers between 10 and 20: numbers that can't be divided evenly except by 1 and by themselves. In the middle row, 3 is followed by its double, 6, and 4 by its double, 8; then 10 comes before its half, 5.

What does it mean? De Heinzelin wondered, in 1962, whether the people of Ishango knew about primes. Alexander Marshack argued that the notches kept track of the moon over six months. Vladimir Pletser and Dirk Huylebrouck have argued that they show counting in twelves. In 2026 a team asked grown-ups to work with notches as small as these, and found they could not count them exactly enough to do sums.

At first the layers were thought to be about 8,500 to 11,000 years old. In 1987 Alison Brooks and Catherine Smith dated them again, and the bone is now thought to be about 20,000 years old.

A second notched bone was found at Ishango in 1959. It is in Brussels too, and it has hardly been studied.

Altamira ("al-ta-MEE-ra") is a cave near the town of Santillana del Mar ("san-tee-YA-na del MAR"), in Cantabria, in the north of Spain. A local man, Modesto Cubillas, found its entrance in about 1868. In 1875 Marcelino Sanz de Sautuola ("sow-TWOH-la"), a man of the district who was curious about the distant past, first visited the cave.

In 1879 he came back to dig in its floor, with his daughter, María, who was eight. While he dug, she looked up, and on the ceiling she saw bison.

There are more than twenty great animals on the ceiling: bison, two horses and a female deer, painted in red, yellow and black. The largest are well over a metre long. The painters chose places where the rock bulges, so the bodies seem to swell out of the ceiling.

In 1880 Sautuola published a short book about the cave, and said the paintings were very old. The experts, among them Émile Cartailhac ("kar-tie-YAK"), in France, would not believe it. Paintings so fine and so fresh, they thought, could not be the work of people of the ice age, and some said they were fakes. Juan Vilanova y Piera, a Spanish geologist, stood by him, but it did not help.

Then, in France, other caves were found with paintings on their walls, at La Mouthe in 1895 and at Font-de-Gaume in 1901. In 1902 Cartailhac published an article that he called "Mea culpa d'un sceptique": a sceptic's "my fault."

In 1992 Hélène Valladas and her team dated charcoal from the paint of the bison, and it was about 17,000 years old. A red sign in the same cave, dated another way in 2012, is at least 35,600, but that way of dating is argued.

The cave was closed in 2002 to protect it. A copy of the ceiling was made inside the museum nearby.

Sautuola died in 1888, fourteen years before Cartailhac's article.
