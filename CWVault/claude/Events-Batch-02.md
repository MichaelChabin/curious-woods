## 1. Chillies reach Asia

**Label:** Chillies
**Year:** about 1540 · precision: decade · kind: crop or animal
**Place:** Goa, on the west coast of India (15.50 N, 73.83 E)

**Summary (30 words)**
1500s. Portuguese ships carried chilli peppers from the Americas to Goa, in India. Within a century chillies were growing across Asia, in places whose cooking now seems impossible without them. *More*

**More (285 words)**

```
About 11,540 years after the ice
      or about 1540
            or about 490 years ago.
```

A bowl of curry in southern India. A plate of noodles in Thailand. A pot of kimchi in Korea. All three are hot with chilli.

Five hundred years ago, none of them was.

Chilli peppers come from the Americas. People in the Americas were growing them at least six thousand years ago. Nobody in Asia, Africa or Europe had ever tasted one.

Then, in 1492, Christopher Columbus sailed west, looking for a way to India and its spices. One of the spices he wanted was black pepper. He never reached India, but in the Caribbean he found a plant whose fruit burned the mouth, and he called it pepper too. That is why chillies are still called peppers, though they are not related to black pepper at all.

Portuguese sailors carried chillies around Africa to their trading town of Goa, on the west coast of India. Chillies are easy to grow. You need only a few seeds, and a plant fruits in months. Farmers planted them, traders carried the seeds on, and within about a hundred years chillies were growing across India, and in Southeast Asia and China too.

Nobody wrote down the day the first chilli arrived. One of the earliest mentions in India is in a song by a poet named Purandara Dasa, who lived in the 1500s. He calls the chilli the saviour of the poor, because it made plain food taste good.

In Korea, red chilli in kimchi came later still, in the 1700s, and became common only in the 1800s.

So the cooking that tastes oldest in many parts of Asia uses a plant that arrived only a few lifetimes ago.

Today India grows more chillies than any other country.

**Pictures wanted:** chilli plants with ripe fruit; a spice market in India (Commons).

**References**
- Linda Perry and others, "Starch fossils and the domestication and dispersal of chili peppers (Capsicum spp. L.) in the Americas," *Science*, 2007. The age of chillies in the Americas.
- Lizzie Collingham, *Curry: A Tale of Cooks and Conquerors* (Oxford, 2006). Chillies reaching India.
- Jean Andrews, *Peppers: The Domesticated Capsicums* (University of Texas Press, 1995). How chillies spread.
- FAOSTAT (Food and Agriculture Organization). India as the largest producer.

**Notes for us.** The beat: food that tastes ancient is new. Checks: chillies grown in the Americas at least 6,000 years ago (Perry 2007's finds are from Ecuador). Columbus named them "pimiento". Portuguese in Goa from 1510; chillies there by the mid-1500s. Purandara Dasa (1484–1564): "saviour of the poor, enhancer of good food" is the usual translation. Korea: chilli first mentioned 1614; in kimchi recipes in the 1700s; common in the 1800s. India largest producer (FAO). The date on the timeline (1540) is a stand-in for "the 1500s".

## 2. Timbuktu's books

**Label:** Timbuktu's books
**Year:** about 1550 · precision: century · kind: text
**Place:** Timbuktu, Mali (16.77 N, 3.01 W)

**Summary (33 words)**
1500s. Timbuktu, a trading town at the edge of the Sahara, in what is now Mali, was full of scholars and books. Families there kept hundreds of thousands of handwritten books for centuries. *More*

**More (279 words)**

```
About 11,550 years after the ice
      or about 1550
            or about 480 years ago.
```

In 2012, men with guns took over the town of Timbuktu, at the southern edge of the Sahara desert. Soon afterwards, a librarian named Abdel Kader Haidara began packing books into metal trunks.

He and his helpers moved them at night, a few trunks at a time, by donkey cart, car and boat, hundreds of kilometres south to the city of Bamako. By the time they were done, they had moved more than three hundred thousand books.

Why were there so many books in a desert town?

Five hundred years ago, Timbuktu was rich. It stood where camel caravans crossing the Sahara met boats on the Niger River. Salt came from the north, and gold from the south, and with them came scholars. In the 1500s, Timbuktu had schools full of students, and families who collected books and passed them down.

The books were written by hand, mostly in Arabic, some in local languages written in Arabic letters. They are about astronomy, arithmetic, medicine, law, grammar, poetry, the history of the region, and how to settle a quarrel. One scholar of the town, Ahmed Baba, wrote more than forty books and said his own library of about 1,600 was one of the smaller ones in his family.

Paper, ink and bindings were precious. Families kept the books in wooden chests and mud-brick rooms, and many survived for four or five hundred years.

In January 2013, as the armed men left, they set fire to a library. About four thousand books were burned or lost. Most of the rest were already gone, in trunks, in Bamako.

Nobody knows exactly how many books there are. Many families still keep theirs private.

## 3. The silver mountain at Potosí

**Label:** Potosí
**Year:** 1545 · precision: year · kind: place
**Place:** Potosí, Bolivia (19.59 S, 65.75 W)

**Summary (35 words)**
1545. High in the Andes, in what is now Bolivia, Spanish colonists began mining a mountain made rich with silver. Its silver changed prices as far away as China. The miners paid a terrible price. *More*

**More (282 words)**

```
11,545 years after the ice
      or 1545
            or 481 years ago.
```

A red-brown mountain rises above a city in the Andes. It is called Cerro Rico, the Rich Hill. Inside, it is full of tunnels, so many that its top has begun to sink.

It is still being mined, almost five hundred years later.

In 1545, Spanish colonists in the Andes learned of silver in this mountain. The ore near the top was so rich that silver could be melted straight out of it. Within thirty years, the town at its foot, Potosí, had become one of the biggest cities in the Americas. It stands about 4,000 metres high, where the air is thin and nothing much grows.

Where did the silver go?

Some went across the Atlantic to Spain. A great deal ended up in China. At that time the Chinese government collected its taxes in silver, and China needed more silver than it had. From 1571, Spanish ships carried American silver across the Pacific to Manila, in the Philippines, where Chinese merchants traded silk and porcelain for it. For the first time, goods went all the way around the world.

Who dug it out?

From 1573, the Spanish rulers forced Indigenous men from across the Andes to take turns working in the mines. The system was called the mita. Miners worked deep underground for days at a time, carrying heavy loads up ladders. Silver was separated from the rock with mercury, which is poisonous. Many men died. Many families left their villages to escape the mita.

How many died? Nobody counted. Some writers give huge numbers, but nobody can check them, and historians disagree.

In Spanish there is still a saying for something priceless: "vale un Potosí," it's worth a Potosí.

**Pictures wanted:** Cerro Rico above the city (Commons); an early drawing of the mountain from 1553 by Pedro Cieza de León (public domain).

**References**
- Kris Lane, *Potosí: The Silver City That Changed the World* (University of California Press, 2019). The whole story.
- Dennis Flynn and Arturo Giráldez, "Born with a 'silver spoon': the origin of world trade in 1571," *Journal of World History*, 1995. Silver to China through Manila.
- Jeffrey Cole, *The Potosí Mita, 1573–1700* (Stanford, 1985). The forced labour.

**Notes for us.** The beat: a mountain of silver that tied the world together, and who paid for it. Not a war, but the cost rule applies. Checks: silver found 1545. City about 4,000 m (4,090). Population about 160,000 by about 1610 is often given; "one of the biggest cities in the Americas" is safe. Manila galleons from 1571. Ming taxes paid in silver (Single Whip reform). Mita from 1573 (Viceroy Toledo). Mercury amalgamation from the 1570s. "Eight million dead" is widely repeated and unsupported; the text refuses it. The mountain's summit is subsiding (reported by UNESCO).

## 4. Florentine Codex

**Label:** Florentine Codex
**Year:** 1577 · precision: year · kind: text
**Place:** Tlatelolco, now part of Mexico City (19.45 N, 99.14 W). The finished copy is in Florence.

**Summary (33 words)**
1577. In Mexico, Nahua scholars and a Spanish friar finished twelve books describing the Aztec world, in the Aztecs' own language and pictures. The Spanish conquest had ended that world fifty years earlier. *More*

**More (286 words)**

```
11,577 years after the ice
      or 1577
            or 449 years ago.
```

A page shows a small painted picture of people at work. Beside the picture are two columns of writing. The left column is in Spanish. The right column is in Nahuatl, the language of the Aztecs. Say "NAH-wah-tl."

There are about 2,500 pages like it, and nearly as many pictures.

The book was made in Mexico between the 1540s and 1577. Fifty years before, the Aztec capital, Tenochtitlan, had been a city of perhaps two hundred thousand people, built on a lake. In 1521, Spanish soldiers and their Indigenous allies destroyed it. Disease killed far more people than the fighting did.

A Spanish friar named Bernardino de Sahagún wanted to know what the Aztec world had been like. Partly this was so that missionaries would understand the people they were trying to convert. Partly he was simply fascinated. He asked Nahua elders who remembered the old days, and young Nahua scholars who could write both languages, to help him.

What did they write about?

Everything. The gods and festivals. The stars. The plants and animals of Mexico, hundreds of them, with pictures. How to raise a child. What merchants did. How feather workers made their cloaks. Speeches parents gave their children. The last of the twelve books tells the story of the conquest, from the side of the people who lost.

The Nahua artists drew the pictures. Some of them had been trained in the old way of painting books before the Spanish came.

The finished copy went to Spain, and from there, somehow, to Florence, in Italy. That is why it is called the Florentine Codex. A codex is a handwritten book.

In 2023 the whole book was put online, free for anyone to read.

**Pictures wanted:** a page with the two columns and an illustration (Medicea Laurenziana Library; the World Digital Library and the Digital Florentine Codex publish images; licence to check).

**References**

- Jeanette Favrot Peterson and Kevin Terraciano, eds., *The Florentine Codex: An Encyclopedia of the Nahua World in Sixteenth-Century Mexico* (University of Texas Press, 2019). How it was made, and the artists.
- Digital Florentine Codex (florentinecodex.getty.edu), 2023. The online edition.
- Matthew Restall, *Seven Myths of the Spanish Conquest* (Oxford, 2003). The allies and disease.

**Notes for us.** The beat: the Aztec world described by Aztecs, just after it ended. The conquest is a war, told in one paragraph: what happened, the cost (disease worse than fighting), and this book is part of what changed. Religion: Sahagún's missionary purpose is stated plainly, without comment. Checks: about 2,500 pages and nearly 2,500 images (Getty). The opening picture is described generally; a real folio should be chosen and described when pictures are found. Tenochtitlan population estimates vary (about 200,000 is common). Finished copy 1575–77. How it reached Florence is uncertain (Medici by 1588). Digital edition launched 2023. ## 5. Huaynaputina erupts

**Summary (36 words)**
19 February 1600. A volcano in southern Peru erupted in the largest eruption in South America's recorded history. Its ash cooled the whole world, and the next year's summer in Europe and Russia was bitterly cold. *More*

**More (273 words)**

```

```

In the Andes of southern Peru there is a mountain that hardly looks like a volcano. It has no tall cone. Before 1600, the people living nearby did not know it was one.

On 19 February 1600, it exploded.

Ash and rock fell over the countryside for days. Villages around the mountain were buried. In the city of Arequipa, about seventy kilometres away, ash lay thick on the roofs, and it was dark in the middle of the day. The rivers ran with mud. Around 1,500 people died.

Then something happened much farther away.

A large eruption throws a fine mist high into the sky, higher than any clouds. The mist spreads around the whole world and blocks a little sunlight. For a year or two, the world is cooler.

In 1601, the summer in much of Europe and Asia was one of the coldest in six hundred years. We know this because trees grow less in a cold year, and their growth rings for 1601 are thin. In France the grapes ripened late. In Russia, the harvest failed that year, and the next, and the one after. About two million people died in the famine that followed.

Did the volcano cause the Russian famine? Many scientists think it helped. The cold year matches. But crops fail for many reasons, and nobody can be sure.

This is the same thing that happened after the eruption of Tambora, in Indonesia, 215 years later, when Europe had a year without a summer.

The mountain's name is from Quechua, the language of the Andes. Say "WHY-na-poo-TEE-na." It is often translated as "young volcano" or "new volcano."

**Label:** Taj Mahal

**Place:** Agra, India (27.18 N, 78.04 E)

**Summary (30 words)**
About 1653. In Agra, India, the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan finished a white marble tomb for his wife, Mumtaz Mahal. It took about twenty years and many thousands of workers. *More*

A white building stands at the end of a long garden, beside a river. It has a great dome, four smaller domes, and a tall thin tower at each corner. From the front, its left half is a mirror of its right half. So is the garden. So are the buildings on either side.

It is a tomb.

In 1631, Mumtaz Mahal, the wife of the emperor Shah Jahan, died giving birth to their fourteenth child. Shah Jahan ruled the Mughal Empire, which covered most of northern India. The next year he began building her tomb in the city of Agra.

It took about twenty years. The white marble came from quarries about 400 kilometres away. It is said that more than a thousand elephants carried materials, and that about twenty thousand people worked on it: masons, carvers, painters, and craftsmen from as far away as Persia and Central Asia. Nobody knows the true numbers.

Look closely at the walls and you find flowers made of stone. Workers cut thin pieces of coloured stone, such as jade, carnelian and lapis lazuli, and fitted them into shapes cut in the marble, so tightly that you can barely feel the joins.

The four towers at the corners lean very slightly outward. One common explanation is that if an earthquake knocked one down, it would fall away from the tomb.

Shah Jahan did not have a happy old age. In 1658 his son took the throne and kept him locked up in the fort at Agra. From there he could see the Taj Mahal, further along the river. He died in 1666.

He was buried beside her. In the whole building, his tomb is the only thing that is not placed in perfect symmetry.

## 7. The last dodo

**Label:** Last dodo
**Year:** 1662 · precision: year · kind: crop or animal
**Place:** Mauritius, in the Indian Ocean (20.20 S, 57.50 E)

**Summary (37 words)**
1662. Sailors shipwrecked on the island of Mauritius, in the Indian Ocean, reported seeing dodos. It is the last sighting most scientists accept. Less than a hundred years after the first Dutch ships, the bird was gone. *More*

**More (291 words)**

```
      or 1662
            or 364 years ago.
```

A large grey bird stands on a beach. It is about a metre tall, with a huge hooked beak, tiny wings and a tuft of curly feathers for a tail. It cannot fly. It has never needed to.

It is a dodo, and it lives only on Mauritius, an island in the Indian Ocean.

For millions of years, no people, cats, rats or pigs lived on Mauritius. A bird that nested on the ground and could not fly was safe. The dodo's closest living relatives are pigeons. Its ancestors probably flew to the island, and over a very long time, with nothing to fly away from, their descendants lost the use of their wings.

Dutch sailors began stopping at Mauritius in 1598. They ate dodos, though they complained about the taste. They also brought animals: rats that came ashore from the ships, and pigs and monkeys that they let loose. These ate the dodos' eggs and chicks.

In 1662, a Dutch sailor named Volkert Evertsz, shipwrecked on the island, reported catching dodos on a small islet off the coast. It is the last sighting most scientists accept. By about 1690, there were none.

What did a dodo really look like?

Nobody knows exactly. The famous paintings show a round, fat bird. But some of those painters worked from dodos that had been overfed on ships, or from stuffed birds, or from other paintings. From its bones, scientists now think the living bird was slimmer.

Almost nothing of the bird itself survives: skeletons, pieced together from bones dug out of a swamp on Mauritius in the 1860s, and one dried head and one foot, in a museum in Oxford.

That head and foot hold the only dodo skin left in the world.

## 8. Sangaku

**Label:** Sangaku
**Year:** about 1683 · precision: decade · kind: object
**Place:** Japan; the oldest surviving tablet is in Tochigi Prefecture (36.57 N, 139.88 E)

**Summary (29 words)**
From the 1680s. In Japan, people began hanging painted wooden tablets of geometry puzzles in shrines and temples. Farmers, samurai, women and children made them. About nine hundred survive. *More*

**More (292 words)**

```
About 11,683 years after the ice
      or about 1683
            or about 340 years ago.
```

Under the roof of a shrine in Japan hangs a wooden board, painted in bright colours. On it are circles inside a triangle, each circle just touching its neighbours. Beneath the picture, written in Japanese, is a question: if you know the size of the big circle, how big are the small ones?

Sometimes the answer is written underneath. Sometimes it isn't.

These boards are called sangaku. Say "SAHN-gah-koo." It means "mathematics tablet." For more than two hundred years, from the 1680s to the late 1800s, people in Japan made them and hung them in shrines and temples.

Why hang a geometry problem in a shrine?

Partly as a gift, or a thank-you, the way people hung other kinds of painted boards. Partly to show off. A sangaku said: here is a problem I solved, and it is beautiful. Can you solve it?

The people who made them were not all scholars. Some were samurai. Some were farmers or merchants. Some were women. Some were children, one as young as twelve.

For most of those years, Japan's rulers kept the country almost closed to the outside world. Japanese mathematicians worked on their own, with their own methods, and they did not know what mathematicians in Europe were doing. Sometimes they found the same things.

One tablet, hung in 1822, shows a ring of six balls, each touching its neighbours, all packed around three other balls. It gives a surprising rule about their sizes. In 1937, an English chemist named Frederick Soddy found the same arrangement, and it was named after him. He did not know about the tablet.

Many sangaku have been lost to fire, rot and time. About nine hundred survive.

Some are still hanging where they were first put up.

**References**

- Tony Rothman and Hidetoshi Fukagawa, "Japanese Temple Geometry," *Scientific American*, May 1998. A short account.
- Frederick Soddy, "The bowl of integers and the hexlet," *Nature*, 1937. Soddy's own paper.

**Notes for us.** The beat: geometry as something you give, and hang up for strangers to solve. Religion: shrines and temples named without comment. Checks: oldest surviving about 1683 (Tochigi); tradition from the 1600s to the Meiji era. About 880–900 survive. The youngest maker found in sources is twelve. The 1822 tablet at Samukawa Shrine, Kanagawa; Soddy's paper *Nature*, January 1937. The 1683 tablet was damaged by fire in 1975; a copy hangs in its place. ## 9. Jantar Mantar

**Label:** Jantar Mantar
**Year:** about 1734 · precision: decade · kind: object
**Place:** Jaipur, India (26.92 N, 75.82 E)

**Summary (30 words)**
About 1734. In Jaipur, India, Maharaja Jai Singh II finished an observatory of giant stone instruments for measuring the sky. Its largest sundial is as tall as a nine-storey building. *More*

**More (293 words)**

```
About 11,734 years after the ice
      or about 1734
            or about 290 years ago.
```

A stone staircase climbs into the sky. It is as tall as a nine-storey building, and it leads nowhere. On either side of it are huge curved walls, like the inside of a bowl cut in half.

It is a clock.

The staircase is the pointer of a sundial. Its shadow falls on the curved walls, which are marked with the hours and minutes. Because the sundial is so big, its shadow moves fast. Stand beside it and you can watch the edge of the shadow creep along the stone, about a millimetre every second.

The sundial is called the Samrat Yantra, the supreme instrument. It is the largest of about twenty stone instruments in a courtyard in the city of Jaipur, in northwest India. They were built by Maharaja Jai Singh II, who ruled Jaipur and loved astronomy. He finished this observatory around 1734. He built four others in other cities, including Delhi.

Why build instruments so big?

Because he wanted his measurements to be exact. On a small sundial, a minute is a tiny step of shadow, hard to read. On the Samrat Yantra, a minute is about six centimetres. It can tell the local time to within a couple of seconds.

The other instruments measure other things: how high a star is above the horizon, which constellation the Sun is in, where the planets are. Some are bowls sunk into the ground that you climb down into.

Jai Singh knew about telescopes, which had been invented in Europe more than a hundred years before. He chose stone instead. Stone does not bend or wobble, and it lasts.

The name Jantar Mantar comes from Sanskrit words meaning "instrument" and "calculation."

On a sunny day, the Samrat Yantra still tells the time.

**Pictures wanted:** the Samrat Yantra with people beside it for scale (Commons).

**References**
- Virendra Nath Sharma, *Sawai Jai Singh and His Astronomy* (Motilal Banarsidass, 1995). The observatories and their instruments.
- UNESCO World Heritage Centre, "The Jantar Mantar, Jaipur." Dimensions and dates.
- Barry Perlus, *Celestial Mirror: The Astronomical Observatories of Jai Singh II* (Yale University Press, 2020). Photographs and measurements.

**Notes for us.** The beat: instruments so big you walk inside them. Checks: Samrat Yantra gnomon about 27 m (nine storeys at 3 m). Shadow speed about 1 mm per second, 6 cm per minute. Accuracy about 2 seconds (widely stated; generous). About 19–20 instruments. Built 1728–34. Five observatories (Delhi, Jaipur, Ujjain, Varanasi, Mathura; Mathura is lost). "Knew about telescopes": he is reported to have had telescopes brought; check. Name etymology: jantar from yantra, mantar from mantra; "calculation" for mantra is the usual gloss and is loose.

In a grey plain of dust on the Moon, there is a footprint. It is the print of a boot, with ridges across the sole. It has been there since 1969.

There is no wind on the Moon to blow it away, and no rain to wash it out.

The boot belonged to Buzz Aldrin, and he photographed the print on purpose, to see how the dust behaved. Minutes before, Neil Armstrong had stepped off the ladder of their landing craft and become the first person to stand on the Moon.

They had come from Earth in three days, on top of the biggest rocket that had ever flown, with a third astronaut, Michael Collins. He stayed in orbit around the Moon while the other two went down, and waited for them.

The landing was nearly a failure. As the landing craft came down, Armstrong saw that the computer was steering it toward a field of boulders. He took over, flew it himself across the boulders, and set it down with less than a minute of fuel left for landing.

What did they do there?

They walked around for about two and a half hours. They set up experiments, collected about twenty-one kilograms of rock and dust to bring home, planted a flag, and took photographs. Hundreds of millions of people on Earth watched them on television.

On the leg of the landing craft they left a metal sign. It says: "Here men from the planet Earth first set foot upon the Moon, July 1969 A.D. We came in peace for all mankind."

Over the next three and a half years, ten more people walked on the Moon. The last left in December 1972.

The footprints are still there. Nobody has walked on the Moon since.

In October 1977, a young man named Ali Maow Maalin was working as a cook in a hospital in the town of Merca, in Somalia. He agreed to ride in a car with two sick children, to show the driver the way. The ride took about fifteen minutes.

The sick children had smallpox.

Smallpox was one of the deadliest diseases there has ever been. It spread from person to person, it covered the body in blisters, and it killed about three out of every ten people who caught it. In the 1900s alone, it killed an estimated three hundred million people.

But there was a way to stop it. A vaccine protected people from smallpox, and it had existed since 1796.

So why was smallpox still around in 1977?

Because vaccinating everyone in the world is hard. In 1967, the World Health Organization began a campaign to end smallpox everywhere. Instead of trying to vaccinate everyone, teams hunted down every case. When they found one, they vaccinated everyone around it: family, neighbours, people who had visited. The disease had nowhere to go. Country by country, it disappeared.

By 1977, Somalia was the last place it survived.

Ali Maow Maalin had never been vaccinated. About ten days after the car ride, he fell ill. Teams found everyone he had met, and vaccinated all of them. He recovered. Nobody caught smallpox from him.

He was the last person in the world to catch smallpox in the ordinary way. In 1980, the World Health Organization declared smallpox gone. It is the only human disease that has ever been wiped out.

Ali Maow Maalin spent his later years working to vaccinate children in Somalia against another disease, polio.

On 5 June 1977, in a park in Nairobi, Kenya, a small group of people planted seven trees.

That was the beginning.

The woman who organised it was Wangari Maathai. Say "wan-GAH-ree ma-TIE." She had grown up in the Kenyan countryside, near a stream where she used to watch frogs' eggs. She went on to study biology in the United States and Germany, and in 1971 she became the first woman in East and Central Africa to earn a doctorate.

In the 1970s, she listened to women from the countryside. They told her their streams were drying up. They had to walk farther and farther to find firewood. Their soil was washing away, and their families did not have enough to eat.

Maathai saw that all of these had one cause. The forests were being cut down.

What could one person do about that?

Her answer was simple. Women already knew how to grow plants. So she paid them to grow trees. A woman who raised seedlings from seeds, and planted them, was paid a small amount for each tree that was still alive a few months later. The trees gave shade, firewood and fruit, held the soil in place, and kept water in the ground.

The idea spread from village to village. The group became known as the Green Belt Movement.

It was not always peaceful work. When Maathai stood up against plans to build on a park in Nairobi, and to sell off a forest, she was arrested and beaten. She kept going.

In 2004 she was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize. She was the first African woman to receive it.

By the time she died in 2011, the Green Belt Movement had planted more than thirty million trees. Today the count is more than fifty million.
