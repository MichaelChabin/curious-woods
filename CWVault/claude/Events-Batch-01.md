## 1. Maize

**Label:** Maize
**Year:** about −7000 · precision: millennium · kind: crop or animal
**Place:** the Balsas River valley, Guerrero, southwest Mexico (18.3 N, 99.8 W). The exact spot is unknown.

**Summary (35 words)**
About 7000 BCE. In the hills of southwest Mexico, people began choosing seeds from a wild grass called teosinte. Over thousands of years it became maize, the corn that now feeds much of the world. *More*

**More (271 words)**

```
About 3,000 years after the ice
      or about 7000 BCE
            or about 9,000 years ago.
```

A wild grass grows on the hillsides of southwest Mexico. Its name is teosinte. Say "tay-oh-SIN-tay." Its ear is about as long as your little finger, and it holds only five to twelve seeds. Each seed is locked inside a case so hard that you could break a tooth on it.

This grass is the ancestor of corn.

How do you get from one to the other?

Someone had to choose. Every year, the people who gathered teosinte kept some seeds to plant. If they kept seeds from the plants they liked best, with bigger ears or softer cases, the next year's plants were a little more like that. Nobody planned corn. It happened a little at a time, over thousands of years, because people kept choosing.

We know roughly where it started because of the plant's own genes. Every kind of maize in the world is most closely related to the teosinte that grows in the Balsas River valley. In a rock shelter there, archaeologists found grinding stones with tiny grains of maize starch still stuck to them. The grains are about 8,700 years old.

The earliest maize cobs that survive, from a dry cave in Oaxaca, are about 6,250 years old. They are about as long as the top joint of your thumb.

A scientist named George Beadle once heated teosinte seeds in a pan. They popped. So one of the first things people could have done with teosinte, even before it changed, was make popcorn.

Today more maize is grown than any other grain.

Teosinte still grows wild in those hills. It still has its hard little seeds.

**Pictures wanted:** a teosinte ear beside a modern corn cob, at the same scale (widely photographed; a public-domain or CC version is needed).

**References**
- Dolores Piperno and others, "Starch grain and phytolith evidence for early ninth millennium B.P. maize from the Central Balsas River Valley, Mexico," *PNAS*, 2009. The grinding stones and the 8,700-year date.
- Dolores Piperno and Kent Flannery, "The earliest archaeological maize from highland Mexico," *PNAS*, 2001. The Guilá Naquitz cobs.
- Yoshihiro Matsuoka and others, "A single domestication for maize shown by multilocus microsatellite genotyping," *PNAS*, 2002. All maize descends from Balsas teosinte.
- John Doebley, "The genetics of maize evolution," *Annual Review of Genetics*, 2004. Teosinte's ear and hard case.

**Notes for us.** The beat: nobody invented corn; people kept choosing. Checks: 5–12 seeds per teosinte ear (Doebley). The 8,700-year starch grains (Piperno 2009). Guilá Naquitz cobs about 6,250 years old (Piperno and Flannery 2001); Cobs about 2.5 cm (Piperno and Flannery figure). Beadle popped teosinte; the date is not pinned, so none is given (his "Teosinte and the origin of maize," *Journal of Heredity*, 1939, is the likely write-up). Maize is the largest grain crop by weight.

## 2. Storegga slide

**Label:** Storegga slide
**Year:** about −6150 · precision: century · kind: earth event
**Place:** the Storegga, on the edge of the sea floor off western Norway (64.5 N, 5.5 E)

**Summary (36 words)**
About 6150 BCE. Under the sea off Norway, a piece of the sea floor almost as big as Iceland slid into deep water. The tsunami it made swept across the North Sea to Scotland and beyond. *More*

**More (288 words)**

```
About 3,850 years after the ice
      or about 6150 BCE
            or about 8,200 years ago.
```

In the Shetland Islands, north of Scotland, there is a layer of sand in the peat. The peat was made by plants, slowly, in quiet water. The sand does not belong there. It was thrown in by the sea, and in some places it lies about twenty metres above where the sea was at the time.

Something threw the sea that high.

Off the coast of Norway, the sea floor ends at a steep edge. Beyond it the water is thousands of metres deep. About 8,200 years ago, a piece of that edge broke loose and slid down into the deep. It covered an area almost as big as Iceland. The mud and sand that moved would bury the whole of Scotland about forty metres deep.

When that much sea floor moves at once, the water above it moves too. That is a tsunami: a wave caused not by wind but by the ground under the sea. The wave spread across the North Sea in a few hours. Its sand is found in Norway, Scotland, the Faroe Islands and Shetland.

Where the North Sea is now, there was land.

The ice had melted, and the sea was rising, but slowly. Between Britain and the rest of Europe lay low hills, marshes and rivers, where people hunted and fished. Archaeologists call it Doggerland. By the time of the slide much of it was already underwater, and what was left may have been islands.

Did the wave finish Doggerland? Some scientists think it did. Others think the islands lasted a while longer. Nobody knows how many people lived there, or how many were lost.

Fishing boats still pull up bones and stone tools from the bottom of the North Sea.

**Pictures wanted:** a map of the North Sea with Doggerland's coast about 8,000 years ago (several published reconstructions; licence to check); the Colinda harpoon point, trawled up in 1931 (British Museum or Norwich Castle).

**References**
- Petter Bryn and others, "Explaining the Storegga Slide," *Marine and Petroleum Geology*, 2005. Size and cause of the slide.
- Stein Bondevik and others, "Record-breaking height for 8000-year-old tsunami in the North Atlantic," *Eos*, 2003. The Shetland deposits.
- Vincent Gaffney, Simon Fitch and David Smith, *Europe's Lost World: The Rediscovery of Doggerland* (Council for British Archaeology, 2009). Doggerland.
- James Walker and others, "A great wave: the Storegga tsunami and the end of Doggerland?", *Antiquity*, 2020. The argument about whether the wave ended it.

**Notes for us.** The beat: a landslide under the sea, and the land it may have finished. Checks: run-up about 20 m in Shetland (Bondevik 2003; some sites higher). Slide area about 95,000 km², a little less than Iceland's 103,000 km², so "almost as big as Iceland." Volume about 3,000 km³ spread over Scotland's 78,000 km² is about 38 m, so "about forty metres." "Nobody knows how many people lived there" is true.

## 3. Writing at Uruk

**Label:** Writing at Uruk
**Year:** about −3300 · precision: century · kind: text
**Place:** Uruk (now Warka), southern Iraq (31.32 N, 45.64 E)

**Summary (38 words)**
About 3300 BCE. In Uruk, in what is now Iraq, people began pressing signs into wet clay to keep track of barley, beer and sheep. It is one of the two oldest kinds of writing we know of. *More*

**More (299 words)**

```
About 6,700 years after the ice
      or about 3300 BCE
            or about 5,300 years ago.
```

A piece of clay about the size of a bar of soap has marks pressed into it. Some of the marks are round holes. Some are little wedges. One looks like a jar with a pointed bottom.

The round holes and wedges are numbers. The jar means beer.

This is some of the oldest writing in the world. It was found in the ruins of Uruk, a city in what is now southern Iraq, which about 5,300 years ago was one of the largest cities anywhere. Archaeologists have dug up about five thousand tablets like it there.

What did the first writers write about?

Mostly, things to count. How much barley came in. How much went out. How many sheep, how much beer, how many workers and how much each was paid. Almost every early tablet is a record of this kind. A few are lists of words, which may have been how the writers learned the signs.

The writer held a sharpened reed and pressed it into clay while the clay was still soft. Then the tablet dried hard. A dried tablet lasts a very long time, and if a building burns down around it, it bakes and lasts even longer.

Some tablets end with a sign that may be a person's name: Kushim. It appears on several records of barley. If it is a name, Kushim is one of the first people in history whose name we can read, and what we know about Kushim is that he or she was keeping accounts. But the sign may be a title instead of a name. Nobody is sure.

Over the next thousand years, the signs became wedge-shaped marks called cuneiform. Say "kyoo-NAY-i-form." People used them to write laws, letters, stories and poems.

It started with beer and barley.

**Pictures wanted:** a proto-cuneiform tablet from Uruk showing number signs and the beer sign (British Museum, Metropolitan Museum, or the Schøyen Collection's Kushim tablet; licences to check).

**References**
- Hans Nissen, Peter Damerow and Robert Englund, *Archaic Bookkeeping: Early Writing and Techniques of Economic Administration in the Ancient Near East* (University of Chicago Press, 1993). What the early tablets record.
- Cuneiform Digital Library Initiative (cdli.earth). Images and readings of Uruk tablets, including those naming Kushim.
- Irving Finkel and Jonathan Taylor, *Cuneiform* (British Museum Press, 2015). How cuneiform grew from these signs.

**Notes for us.** The beat: the first writing was bookkeeping. Labels from tomb U-j at Abydos, Egypt, are about as old, hence "one of the two oldest". Checks: about 5,000 archaic tablets from Uruk (Nissen, Damerow and Englund). About 85–90% administrative; the rest word lists. "One of the largest cities anywhere" is the usual view. The jar sign for beer: confirm on a specific tablet. Kushim: named on about 18 tablets; name or title is disputed, and the text says so. "Soap-bar size" fits many, not all, tablets.

## 4. Ötzi

**Label:** Ötzi
**Year:** about −3300 · precision: century · kind: person
**Place:** the Tisenjoch pass, Ötztal Alps, on the border of Italy and Austria (46.78 N, 10.84 E)

**Summary (32 words)**
About 3300 BCE. A man died high in the Alps between what are now Italy and Austria. The ice kept his body, clothes and tools until two hikers found him in 1991. *More*

**More (282 words)**

```
About 6,700 years after the ice
      or about 3300 BCE
            or about 5,300 years ago.
```

In September 1991, two hikers crossing a pass high in the Alps saw a brown shape sticking out of melting ice. It was a man's head and shoulders. They thought he was a climber who had died a few years before.

He had died about 5,300 years before.

He is called Ötzi, after the Ötztal, the valley below. Say "UHT-see." The ice had kept not only his body but everything he carried. Nothing else like it has ever been found from that time.

What did a man carry, 5,300 years ago, on a walk through the mountains?

An axe with a blade of almost pure copper. A bow of yew wood, taller than he was, not yet finished. A quiver holding fourteen arrows, only two of them ready to shoot. A flint knife. A fur hat. Shoes with bearskin soles, stuffed with grass to keep his feet warm. A pouch with a fire-making kit.

Scientists could even tell what he last ate. His stomach held ibex meat and bread made from an early kind of wheat.

He was about forty-five years old. His skin carries sixty-one tattoos, made of short lines rubbed with charcoal. Most are on his back and legs, over places where his joints would have ached.

For ten years, everyone thought he had died of cold. Then, in 2001, an X-ray showed a stone arrowhead in his left shoulder. Someone had shot him from behind. He probably died soon after. Nobody knows who shot him, or why.

Ötzi is now in a museum in Bolzano, Italy. He is kept in a cold room, as cold as the glacier was, and visitors can look at him through a small window.

**Pictures wanted:** the copper axe; the reconstructed shoe (South Tyrol Museum of Archaeology, which licenses images).

**References**
- South Tyrol Museum of Archaeology, "Ötzi – the Iceman" (iceman.it). Finds, the cold room, the museum's own summary.
- Paul Gostner and Eduard Egarter Vigl, "Report of radiological-forensic findings on the Iceman," *Journal of Archaeological Science*, 2002. The arrowhead.
- Frank Maixner and others, "The Iceman's last meal consisted of fat, wild meat, and cereals," *Current Biology*, 2018. His last meal.
- Marco Samadelli and others, "Complete mapping of the tattoos of the 5300-year-old Tyrolean Iceman," *Journal of Cultural Heritage*, 2015. The 61 tattoos.

**Notes for us.** The beat: one ordinary man's day, 5,300 years ago, kept whole. His death is told plainly, once, without detail. Checks: found 19 September 1991 by Erika and Helmut Simon. Age about 45. Copper about 99.7% pure. Bow about 1.8 m; Ötzi about 1.6 m. Fourteen arrows, two finished. 61 tattoos (Samadelli 2015). Arrowhead found 2001 (Gostner). "Soon after" rather than "within minutes": the torn artery is an interpretation. His last meal also contained fat and bracken; ibex and einkorn are the parts she can picture.

## 5. Drains of Mohenjo-daro

**Label:** Mohenjo-daro
**Year:** about −2500 · precision: century · kind: place
**Place:** Mohenjo-daro, Sindh, Pakistan (27.33 N, 68.14 E)

**Summary (33 words)**
About 2500 BCE. In the valley of the Indus River, in what is now Pakistan, people built a city of brick with covered drains under its streets. Their writing has never been read. *More*

**More (274 words)**

```
About 7,500 years after the ice
      or about 2500 BCE
            or about 4,500 years ago.
```

Along a street of baked brick runs a drain. It is covered with more bricks, so the dirty water flows out of sight. Smaller drains come into it from the houses on each side. Many of the houses had a room with a brick floor for washing, and some had a toilet.

This was about 4,500 years ago.

The city is called Mohenjo-daro. Say "mo-HEN-jo DAH-ro." It stands beside the Indus River in what is now Pakistan. Tens of thousands of people may have lived there. Its streets were laid out in straight lines, and its bricks were made to the same proportions, as if everyone followed the same rules.

It was one of many cities along the Indus. They traded with cities as far away as Iraq.

So why don't we know more about them?

Because nobody can read what they wrote.

The people of the Indus carved signs on small square stones called seals, often above a picture of an animal. Thousands of seals have been found. There are about four hundred different signs. But most inscriptions are only four or five signs long, nobody knows what language they wrote, and no stone has been found with the same words in the Indus script and in a script we can read. Many people have claimed to read it. None has convinced the others.

So we can walk the streets of Mohenjo-daro and see how carefully it was built. We cannot read a single word its people left.

By about 1900 BCE, the city had been left empty, and nobody knows exactly why.

The seals are small enough to hold in your hand.

**Pictures wanted:** a covered street drain at Mohenjo-daro; an Indus seal with a bull or "unicorn" and signs (National Museum of Pakistan, or Commons).

**References**
- Jonathan Mark Kenoyer, *Ancient Cities of the Indus Valley Civilization* (Oxford University Press, 1998). The city, drains and seals.
- Iravatham Mahadevan, *The Indus Script: Texts, Concordance and Tables* (Archaeological Survey of India, 1977). The count of signs and the length of inscriptions.
- Andrew Robinson, *The Indus: Lost Civilizations* (Reaktion, 2015). Attempts at decipherment.
- UNESCO World Heritage Centre, "Archaeological Ruins at Moenjodaro." Site facts.

**Notes for us.** The beat: we can see how they lived but cannot read what they wrote. Checks: population estimates range widely (about 30,000–40,000 is common); "tens of thousands" is safe. Brick proportions 1:2:4 (Kenoyer). About 400 signs; average inscription about five signs (Mahadevan). No bilingual found. Abandoned by about 1900 BCE; causes argued (rivers moving, climate). Trade with Mesopotamia: Indus seals found there.

## 6. Campo del Cielo

**Label:** Campo del Cielo
**Year:** about −2500 · precision: millennium · kind: sky event
**Place:** Campo del Cielo, Chaco and Santiago del Estero provinces, Argentina (27.47 S, 60.58 W)

**Summary (31 words)**
About 2500 BCE. A mass of iron from space broke apart over what is now Argentina. Its pieces made a line of craters and left tonnes of iron in the ground. *More*

**More (266 words)**

```
About 7,500 years after the ice
      or about 2500 BCE
            or about 4,500 years ago.
```

In a field in northern Argentina lies a lump of metal as heavy as five elephants. It is dark and pitted, with smooth hollows like thumbprints. It is made of iron and nickel.

It came from space.

About 4,500 years ago, a mass of iron came down through the air over this plain. It broke apart as it fell. The pieces hit the ground along a line almost twenty kilometres long and made more than twenty craters. The biggest crater is more than a hundred metres across.

A piece of rock or metal from space that reaches the ground is called a meteorite. Iron meteorites are rare. Most meteorites that fall are stone.

The people who lived on the plain knew about the iron long before anyone wrote it down. In 1576, Spanish soldiers went looking for a great mass of iron that the local people used for their weapons. They found it. The Moqoit people, who still live there, call the place Piguen N'onaxa. The Spanish name, Campo del Cielo, means field of the sky.

How do we know when it fell?

Trees were burned when the pieces hit, and the charcoal has been dated. The dates cluster around 4,000 to 4,700 years ago. That is as close as anyone can get.

More than a hundred tonnes of the iron have been found, in pieces from the size of a pea to the size of a car. The largest, called Gancedo, weighs about thirty tonnes. It is the third-heaviest meteorite ever found.

The great irons still lie on the plain, and people can touch them.

**Pictures wanted:** the El Chaco or Gancedo meteorite with people beside it for scale (Commons; licence to check).

**References**
- William Cassidy, L. M. Villar, T. E. Bunch, T. P. Kohman and D. J. Milton, "Meteorites and craters of Campo del Cielo, Argentina," *Science*, 1965. The craters and the line they make.
- William Cassidy and Michael Renard, "Discovering research value in the Campo del Cielo, Argentina, meteorite craters," *Meteoritics & Planetary Science*, 1996. Later work at the site, including the dating.
- Meteoritical Bulletin Database, "Campo del Cielo." Official record.

**Notes for us.** The beat: iron from the sky, known to the people who lived there long before scientists. Checks: Gancedo about 30.8 t, found 2016, third after Hoba (about 60 t) and Ahnighito (about 30.9 t); El Chaco about 28.8 t. "Heavy as five elephants" assumes about 6 t per elephant. Crater field about 18–20 km long, at least 26 craters, largest about 115 m (Laguna Negra). The Moqoit name, Piguen N'onaxa, is from the Argentine government; no source translates it, so the text translates only the Spanish. 1576 expedition under Hernán Mexía de Miraval, looking for iron the local people used for weapons. Dating: 4,000–4,700 years, from charcoal (sources range 4,000–5,000). Whether Cassidy and Renard 1996 holds the charcoal dates is not yet confirmed. "More than a hundred tonnes" is the safe total.

## 7. Thera erupts

**Label:** Thera
**Year:** about −1600 · precision: century · kind: earth event
**Place:** Thera (Santorini), in the Aegean Sea, Greece (36.40 N, 25.40 E)

**Summary (32 words)**
About 1600 BCE. The volcanic island of Thera, in the Aegean Sea, blew apart in one of the largest eruptions of the last ten thousand years. Its ash and waves reached Crete. *More*

**More (298 words)**

```
About 8,400 years after the ice
      or about 1600 BCE
            or about 3,600 years ago.
```

Today, big ships sail into a bay on the Greek island of Santorini and anchor in water hundreds of metres deep. Cliffs rise all around them, striped red, black and white.

The bay is the inside of a volcano.

About 3,600 years ago, the island, then called Thera, erupted. It threw out enough ash and rock to bury a large city many times over. When the eruption was over, the middle of the island had fallen in, and the sea poured into the hole.

People lived on Thera. Their town, now called Akrotiri, was buried in ash, and the ash kept it: houses two and three storeys high, with pictures painted on the walls of swallows, boys fishing and blue monkeys. Archaeologists have found almost no bodies there, and few valuable things. The people seem to have left in time. Where they went, nobody knows.

What happened to Crete?

Crete is a large island about 110 kilometres to the south. A people now called the Minoans lived there, in towns with great palaces. Ash from Thera fell on Crete. At Palaikastro, on Crete's eastern end, scientists have found a layer of sea sand, broken pottery and shells thrown far inland by a tsunami.

In 1939, a Greek archaeologist named Spyridon Marinatos suggested that the eruption destroyed the Minoans. It is a good story, and for a long time many people believed it. But digging since then shows that the Minoan palaces carried on for several generations after the eruption. They were hurt, but not destroyed. What finally ended them is still argued about.

Even the date is argued about. Some methods give about 1600 BCE. Others give about 1500. Scientists have been arguing for fifty years.

The ships anchor where the middle of the island used to be.

**Pictures wanted:** the caldera from the cliffs; the swallows or "spring" fresco from Akrotiri (National Archaeological Museum, Athens; Commons).

**References**
- Sturt Manning, "Eruption of Thera/Santorini," in Eric Cline, ed., *The Oxford Handbook of the Bronze Age Aegean* (2010). The eruption and its date.
- Hendrik Bruins and others, "Geoarchaeological tsunami deposits at Palaikastro (Crete) and the Late Minoan IA eruption of Santorini," *Journal of Archaeological Science*, 2008. The tsunami layer on Crete.
- Spyridon Marinatos, "The volcanic destruction of Minoan Crete," *Antiquity*, 1939. The original theory.
- Walter Friedrich and others, "Santorini eruption radiocarbon dated to 1627–1600 B.C.," *Science*, 2006; and Charlotte Pearson and others, *Science Advances*, 2018, on the later dates. The argument over the date.

**Notes for us.** The beat: an enormous eruption, and why it did not simply end the Minoans. Michael asked for Crete to be followed. Checks: volume estimates vary (about 30–60 km³ of magma); "bury a large city many times over" is deliberately loose. "One of the largest eruptions of the last ten thousand years": widely stated; VEI 6–7. Akrotiri frescoes: swallows, fishermen, blue monkeys are all real. Few bodies found (none so far). Palaikastro deposits (Bruins 2008). Palaces continued until about 1450 BCE. Dates: radiocarbon near 1600, archaeology near 1500; recent work narrows the gap. The Cousteau harbour story is not included: his 1976 finds near Dia could not be confirmed as a tsunami-wrecked harbour. The Exodus idea stays out, per Michael.

## 8. Oldest written song

**Label:** Oldest song
**Year:** about −1400 · precision: century · kind: text (music)
**Place:** Ugarit (Ras Shamra), on the coast of Syria (35.60 N, 35.78 E)

**Summary (36 words)**
About 1400 BCE. In the city of Ugarit, in what is now Syria, someone wrote a song on a clay tablet, with instructions for playing it. It is the oldest nearly complete written music we have. *More*

**More (276 words)**

```
About 8,600 years after the ice
      or about 1400 BCE
            or about 3,400 years ago.
```

A broken clay tablet has two parts, separated by a line. Above the line are the words of a song. Below the line are names and numbers.

The names and numbers are the music.

The tablet was found in the 1950s in the ruins of Ugarit, a port on the coast of what is now Syria. Say "OO-ga-rit." About 3,400 years ago, merchants there spoke and wrote in many languages. This song is in Hurrian, a language that is only partly understood today. It is a song to Nikkal, a goddess of orchards.

The instructions below the line are for a lyre. A lyre is a small stringed instrument, held in the arms. Its strings run from a sound box up to a crossbar. The instructions name pairs of strings, and each name is followed by a number. The names of the pairs are known from other tablets. So the tablet tells a player which strings to play, and something about how many times.

So what does it sound like?

That is the trouble. Nobody agrees.

We don't know how the lyre was tuned. We don't know whether each pair of strings was played together or one after the other. We don't know whether the numbers mean how many times to play, or something else. We don't know how the words fit the notes, or how fast it went.

Since the 1970s, several people have worked it out, each carefully, each in a different way. Their versions sound nothing alike. Some play the notes one after another, as a tune. Others play them two at a time.

Every recording you can find is someone's best guess.

**Pictures wanted:** the tablet (h.6), National Museum of Damascus; a drawing of a lyre from a painting of the same period.

**References**
- Emmanuel Laroche, "Documents hourrites," in *Ugaritica V* (1968). First publication of the Hurrian songs.
- Anne Draffkorn Kilmer, "The Cult Song with Music from Ancient Ugarit: Another Interpretation," *Revue d'Assyriologie*, 1974. One reconstruction.
- Richard Dumbrill, *The Archaeomusicology of the Ancient Near East* (Trafford, 2005). Another reconstruction and a survey of the disagreements.
- Martin West, "The Babylonian Musical Notation and the Hurrian Melodic Texts," *Music & Letters*, 1994. Why the readings differ.

**Notes for us.** The beat: we have the music, and still can't hear it. Religion: "a song to a goddess of orchards" is a plain fact about the text. Checks: tablet h.6, found in the 1950s at Ras Shamra; date about 1400 BCE (range about 1450–1200). "Oldest nearly complete written music" is the usual claim. Nine-stringed lyre (sammûm). Tune versus pairs: Wulstan (1971) and Duchesne-Guillemin read a tune; Kilmer (1974) and West (1994) read two strings sounded together.

## 9. Rhind papyrus

**Label:** Rhind papyrus
**Year:** about −1550 · precision: decade · kind: text
**Place:** Thebes (Luxor), Egypt (25.70 N, 32.64 E)

**Summary (30 words)**
About 1550 BCE. In Egypt, a scribe named Ahmes copied out a scroll of arithmetic problems and their answers. It shows how Egyptians multiplied using nothing but doubling and adding. *More*

**More (303 words)**

```
About 8,450 years after the ice
      or about 1550 BCE
            or about 3,600 years ago.
```

A scroll of papyrus, a kind of paper made from reeds, is about five metres long. It begins with a promise: it will give "accurate reckoning for inquiring into things, and the knowledge of all things."

Then come more than eighty problems, with their answers.

The scribe who copied it wrote his name at the start: Ahmes. He says he copied it from an older scroll, written more than two hundred years before. That makes it one of the oldest books of mathematics in the world, and Ahmes one of the first people we know by name to have written mathematics down.

The problems are practical. How to share loaves of bread among ten men. How much grain fits in a round store. How steep to make the side of a pyramid.

How did an Egyptian scribe multiply?

By doubling. Suppose you want 13 times 12. Write 1 and 12. Double both: 2 and 24. Double again: 4 and 48. Again: 8 and 96. Stop there, because the next double, 16, is more than 13.

Now look down the left side for numbers that add up to 13. 1 and 4 and 8 make 13. Add the numbers beside them: 12 and 48 and 96. That makes 156, which is 13 times 12.

That is all there is to it. Nothing but doubling and adding, and no table of times to learn. It works for any two whole numbers, because every whole number can be made by adding some of 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 and so on.

In 1858 a Scottish lawyer named Alexander Henry Rhind bought the scroll in Luxor. That is why it carries his name instead of the scribe's.

It is in the British Museum. At the very start of the scroll, the scribe's name is still there: Ahmes.

**Pictures wanted:** a section of the papyrus (British Museum, EA 10057; licence to check).

**References**
- Gay Robins and Charles Shute, *The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus: An Ancient Egyptian Text* (British Museum Press, 1987). The scroll, its problems and its methods.
- Arnold Buffum Chace, *The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus* (Mathematical Association of America, 1927–29). A full translation.
- British Museum collection record, EA 10057 and EA 10058. Dimensions and how it was bought.
- Annette Imhausen, *Mathematics in Ancient Egypt: A Contextual History* (Princeton, 2016). Egyptian multiplication and who the scribes were.

On the same day, at noon, in Alexandria, far to the north, an upright stick did cast a shadow.

Why the difference?

Eratosthenes thought he knew. He was the chief librarian at Alexandria, in charge of the greatest library of the time. Like other Greek scholars, he took it that the Earth is a ball, and that the Sun is so far away that its rays arrive side by side. If the ground were flat, the stick in Alexandria would cast no shadow either. The shadow meant the ground between the two cities curved.

So he measured how much. The shadow in Alexandria showed that the Sun was one fiftieth of a full circle away from straight overhead. So the distance from Alexandria to Syene had to be one fiftieth of the way around the Earth.

His own book is lost. We know his method from a writer named Cleomedes, who described it about three hundred years later.

## 19. A new star

**Label:** Guest star
**Year:** 1054.5 · precision: exact (4 July 1054) · kind: sky event
**Place:** recorded at Kaifeng, China (34.80 N, 114.35 E); the star itself is in the constellation Taurus

**Summary (30 words)**
4 July 1054. Astronomers in China recorded a new star, bright enough to see in daylight for more than three weeks. What is left of it is the Crab Nebula. *More*

**More (310 words)**

```
11,054 years after the ice
      or 4 July 1054
            or 972 years ago.
```

A report from the court astronomers of China, in the summer of 1054:

A guest star had appeared at dawn in the east, a little to the south-east of a star we now call Zeta Tauri. It was bright enough to see in daylight, like Venus, with rays of light pointing out on all sides.

A guest star was the Chinese name for a star that appears where there was none before, stays a while, and goes.

The astronomers kept watching. For twenty-three days it could be seen in daylight. It stayed visible at night for almost two years. Then it faded out.

What had they seen?

A star exploding. When a very large star runs out of fuel, its middle collapses and the rest of it blows apart. For a few weeks the explosion can outshine a whole galaxy. This is called a supernova. The star had not appeared. It had been there all along, too faint to see, until it blew up.

Nobody in 1054 could have known that.

In the 1700s and 1800s, astronomers with telescopes found a faint, cloudy patch in the same part of the sky. In the 1840s, an Irish astronomer drew it, and it looked to him like a crab. It has been called the Crab Nebula ever since. A nebula is a cloud in space. In the 1920s, astronomers noticed that the cloud was growing. Measuring how fast, and working backwards, they found it must have started growing about nine hundred years before.

The Crab Nebula is what is left of the guest star.

It is so far away that its light takes about six thousand five hundred years to reach us. So the star really blew up about six thousand five hundred years before the astronomers saw it.

The cloud is still growing, by more than a thousand kilometres every second.

## 20. Great Zimbabwe

**Label:** Great Zimbabwe
**Year:** about 1300 · precision: century · kind: place
**Place:** Great Zimbabwe, near Masvingo, Zimbabwe (20.27 S, 30.93 E)

**Summary (31 words)**
About 1300. In southern Africa, the builders of Great Zimbabwe raised walls of fitted granite as high as eleven metres, without mortar. Later, outsiders claimed Africans could not have built them. *More*

**More (305 words)**

```
11,300 years after the ice
      or about 1300
            or about 700 years ago.
```

A curving wall of granite blocks stands on a plain in southern Africa. It is more than two hundred and fifty metres long, and in places eleven metres high and five metres thick. No mortar holds the blocks together. They are cut and fitted so well that they have stood for about seven hundred years.

Inside the wall stands a solid tower of stone, shaped like a cone.

This is Great Zimbabwe. It was the centre of a kingdom that grew rich trading gold. Its gold went to towns on the coast of East Africa, and from there across the Indian Ocean. At Great Zimbabwe, archaeologists have found glass beads from India, pottery from China and Persia, and a coin from the coastal city of Kilwa. Perhaps ten thousand people lived there at its height.

Who built it?

The ancestors of the Shona people, who live in Zimbabwe today. Archaeologists agree on this, and have for about a hundred years.

But for a long time, many Europeans refused to believe it.

From the 1870s on, some of the Europeans who came said the walls must have been built by outsiders: by people from the Bible, or by traders from the Mediterranean. They had no evidence for this. They simply did not believe Africans could have built something so grand. Some dug up the site looking for proof and threw away what they found, destroying evidence as they went.

In 1905, and again in 1929, archaeologists who dug carefully showed that everything they found was African. Even so, the government of the country, run by white settlers, kept the old story alive in guidebooks and school books all through the 1960s and 1970s.

In 1980, the country became independent. It took its new name from the ruins: Zimbabwe, from a Shona word for houses of stone.
