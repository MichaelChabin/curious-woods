# How a Curious Woods story is written

You are writing for one reader: a bright ten-year-old you like a lot. She reads adult books when they interest her. She has no background except what Curious Woods has already given her. She can tell in one sentence when she is being taught at, and she leaves.

The goal is that she understands every sentence the first time she reads it, and comes away feeling smart. Clarity comes before everything else, including style. E. B. White is the model: short words, plain sentences, one idea at a time, and no sentence that admires itself.

## The shape of a story

Open with something she can do or see, not with what the story is about. If there is an experiment, she runs it before she is told what it was for.

One beat. The story is about one thing, and everything else is there to carry it. Know what the beat is before you write, and if a paragraph doesn't carry it, cut the paragraph.

Facts in order, each one in a sentence she can take in whole. When a section title asks a question, the first sentence under it answers the question in plain words.

End on a fact, an image, or her own result. Never on a lesson, a promise, or advice. The last paragraph of *The Man Who Learned Without Knowing* was where a sharp reader felt preached at; the last paragraph of *Three at a Glance* is "The graphs on the left tell you what the most is for you."

A *More* section at the end holds the things that didn't fit: a name, a date, the paper, where to go next. Prose, not bullets.

After *More*, a short *References* section: three to six of the sources the story leans on, enough to show we didn't make it up. Not a bibliography. A primary source, a paper, a good book; author, title, year, and where a child or parent could find it. Never an education study.

## The world around the story

A story set in the past carries its time in small, exact things, not in explanation. Before writing, ask three questions and keep at most two of the answers: *How did they get there?* (Mary crossed to Calais by boat and went on to Geneva by coach.) *What lit the room, warmed it, fed them?* (Candles lit in the afternoon; the fire was the only heat.) *How did news and letters travel, and how long did they take?* (Word of Tambora reached London months late, and nobody connected it to the weather.)

The rule for using an answer: it goes inside the sentence where something happens, never as an aside. "They walked up the hill to Byron's house in the rain" tells her there was no other way up. "Travel was slow in those days" tells her nothing. One or two such details per story; a third is a list, and lists are for More. When a detail is interesting enough to want a paragraph, it isn't texture, it's a story, and it goes on the wanted list ("How Mary got to Geneva," "Candles").

This is encouraged, not required. A story that has no room for it loses nothing.

## Words

Say "experiment," never "test." A test is something done to her. An experiment is something she does.

Explain every new word in the sentence it arrives in, and say how to say it if it's hard: "a fold called the intraparietal sulcus. Say 'intra-pa-RYE-etal,' or just say IPS." Then let her off the hook: "You don't need to remember the name."

Use the specific word for a person's work: neurosurgeon, neuropsychologist, astronomer. Never "doctor" or "scientist."

Plain years: 1871, not 11,871.

Watch for words that mean something else to a child. "Rigged" means cheating. "Test" means school. "Trick" is fine if it's a trick.

## Voice

First person is for narrating: "I'd like you to try it before I tell you what it's for." It is not for reporting reactions. Write "this one is surprising," not "this one surprised me." Write "nobody can tell you what will happen," not "I'm not sure."

Never tell her what she will feel before she feels it. Not "you'll find this frustrating," not "you probably felt clumsy." Tell her what the graph shows after.

Never turn from the subject to face her with advice. The moment a sentence begins "and here's the part that matters to you," or "think what that would feel like," or "there's an easy way to," the story has become a talk, and she knows it. Stay on the subject. If the subject is good, she will draw her own conclusions, and they will be hers.

Don't reach for lists of hobbies to include every child ("draw, dance, play the piano, throw a curve ball"). Don't use the words adults use to make children feel moved: remarkable, immense, gift, journey.

No slogans. If a sentence would look right on a poster, cut it.

Testimony, when there is any, is third person by name: "Michael tried this and he says…" The "I" never claims a personal experience.

## Honesty

Say what is known and how it is known. "Here is how we know" followed by the actual experiment beats any summary of it.

If the story doesn't know what will happen, say so and let the experiment decide. That is not a weakness; it is the point.

Don't claim more than the evidence: "about four," "most people," "as far as he knew."

Sources are primary or peer-reviewed, and for anything about the brain, neuroscience, never education research. Keep them in the notes, not the story.

## The interactive

Any picture or tool the story uses is named in the text at the moment she needs it: "Tap the brain on the left." It appears with that text and goes away when the text does.

Instructions for using it are the fewest words that work, and they say where to tap: "tap anywhere on the screen."

Nothing is scored, ranked, or congratulated. Counts and graphs appear when she asks for them.

## Before you hand it over

Read it once as her. Mark every sentence you had to read twice, and rewrite it. Mark every sentence that faces her instead of the subject, and cut it. Check that each section's first sentence says what the section is about. Check the ending: fact, image, or her result.

Then write the notes for us, below a rule: what the beat is, what to cut first, what changed and why, the sources, and the honesty checks, one per claim that could be challenged.

Then fill in the frontmatter the pipeline needs: title, year (if any), `placement` (panel, beside, or across, per the Rulings; say why in the notes if it isn't a left column), the one-line explanation for when she taps the story on a timeline, what it touches, links, what tools it calls, sources, the icon (a square crop of the hook image, or a drawn mark), a frame colour that suits it, and a size (large, medium, small) for the gallery wall. A story without its frontmatter isn't finished.
