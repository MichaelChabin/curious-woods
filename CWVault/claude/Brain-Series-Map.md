# Your Brain, Tested by You — series map

## The one rule

Every story in this set ends with the child measuring something about her own brain, and the measurement is the argument. The story never says "you can learn." It sets up an experiment in which she watches herself learn, in minutes, and can't argue with the number. The techniques (don't split attention; leave the brain alone after learning; sleep on it) are never prescribed. She measures the cost of doing it the other way, and draws her own conclusion.

Three at a Glance is the model: a claim, a kitchen-table version with coins, a screen version with a timer, an address in the brain, an animal or a baby who has it too, and a person in history who first threw beans at the question.

## What the set is for

Three things, in this order. That she *has* learned (things she never noticed acquiring: reading, faces, the number line). That she *is* learning (mirror-drawing, hidden pictures, chunking — learning she can watch happen in five minutes). That she *can* learn (the brain rebuilds itself; taxi drivers, jugglers, violinists). The techniques ride along inside those stories, never on their own.

## The shelf

Dehaene is the spine: *The Number Sense* (2011 ed.), *Reading in the Brain* (2009), *Consciousness and the Brain* (2014), *How We Learn* (2020). His four conditions for learning (attention, active engagement, error signal, consolidation) organize the set underneath, but the words never appear in a story. "Error signal" in particular must become *surprise*; "active engagement" must become *your brain keeps what it has to fetch*.

John Rehner Iversen (McMaster; one of the Santa Fe Chamber Music Festival "Brain and Music" speakers). Rhythm and beat: the ASAP hypothesis with Patel (Patel & Iversen 2014, *Frontiers in Systems Neuroscience*) — hearing a beat is the motor system predicting the next one. This is the neuroscience under Rhythmic Practice.

Rebecca Schwarzlose, *Brainscapes* (2021; Washington University). The brain as a set of maps: retinotopic, tonotopic, the body map. This is the neuroscience under the Brain Map, and the source for any story where "how many," "where," or "what pitch" has an address.

Sarah Hart, *Once Upon a Prime* (2023; University of London). Not neuroscience, but the limerick-by-swapping-words trick belongs in the Writing/Editing practice, and her book is a model of the voice we want: a mathematician delighted by stories. Noted here so it isn't lost; it's not part of this set.

Two more Santa Fe speakers to come from Michael.

**Voice rulings for this set (10 Sept):** first person whenever it makes the story stronger; testimony in third person by name ("Michael tried this and he says…", "When Eileen tried it…"); after-the-ice dates with commas, 11,850 (1850); specific roles (neuroscientist, neurosurgeon, neuropsychologist), never "scientist" or "doctor" when we know the word; the interactive may come before the story when the beat needs it.

Primary papers, one or two per story, all outside education. No JRME, no learning-sciences journals, no classroom studies.

### 2. The Man Who Learned Without Knowing — drafted (Story-The-Man-Who-Learned-Without-Knowing.md; tool spec in Spec-Mirror-Star.md)
*Beat.* You can learn something and have no idea you learned it.
*She does.* Mirror-drawing on screen: trace a star while seeing only the mirror image of her hand's path. The tool counts the times she leaves the track. Ten trials. The count falls from twenty-odd to two or three. She felt clumsy the whole time; the graph says otherwise.
*Science.* H.M., who could form no new memories after 1953, learned mirror-drawing over three days and each day insisted he had never seen the task. Milner 1962; Scoville & Milner 1957, *J. Neurol. Neurosurg. Psychiatry*. Two memory systems: the one that remembers events (hippocampus, which H.M. lost) and the one that gets better at things (basal ganglia, cerebellum), which he kept.
*Links.* Brain Map (hippocampus, cerebellum); Rhythmic Practice (why tapping works without thinking about it).
*Intuitions.* #17 (not-yet-knowing: the feeling of not learning is not evidence); #9.
*Honesty.* H.M.'s surgery and its aftermath should be told plainly and without drama; he was a real man, Henry Molaison, and the story should say so.

### 3. The Picture You Can't Unsee
*Beat.* Learning can happen in one second and be permanent.
*She does.* A two-tone black-and-white image she can't read. She looks at it for ten seconds; nothing. The tool shows her the original photograph for one second, then the two-tone again. Now she sees it, and she will still see it next month. A second image lets her feel the before-and-after again.
*Science.* Dolan et al. 1997, *Nature* 389 — the same two-tone image, after one exposure to the original, activates object and face areas it didn't before. Gregory's Dalmatian is the classic. Dehaene, *Consciousness and the Brain*, on the moment of "ignition."
*Links.* The Room You Built (reading); Brain Map (the visual stream).
*Intuitions.* #17; #12 (a paragraph that doesn't yield on first reading can yield on second — same phenomenon, slower).
*Honesty.* "Permanent" is well supported for weeks and months; say "you'll still see it next month," not "forever."

### 4. Four Things at Once
*Beat.* Your working memory holds about four things. Not seven. Four — the same four as Three at a Glance, and probably for the same reason.
*She does.* Change-detection: a set of colored squares flashes for 100 ms, a blank, then the set again with one square possibly changed. Was there a change? Accuracy is near perfect at three squares, falls off a cliff past four. She finds her own number; it's the subitizing number again.
*Science.* Luck & Vogel 1997, *Nature* 390 (capacity about four objects); Cowan 2001, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* (the "magical number four"). Miller's seven was for chunks, and that's the next story.
*Links.* Three at a Glance (rhymes exactly); The Trick Called Chunking.
*Intuitions.* #5, #9, #10.

### 5. The Trick Called Chunking
*Beat.* Four slots is the limit, but a slot can hold anything you already know as one thing. That's how a chess master holds a whole board.
*She does.* The tool flashes twelve letters for two seconds: first as random letters (she gets about four), then as three words (she gets all twelve). Then a phone number as ten digits vs. three groups. Then, if she has a die from story 1: six dots in a heap vs. two rows of three.
*Science.* Chase & Simon 1973, *Cognitive Psychology* (masters recall real positions, not random ones); Miller 1956. Dehaene, *How We Learn*, on how learning compresses.
*Links.* Four Things at Once; the multiplication table (Glass) as forty-five chunks she's building.
*Intuitions.* #7 (decomposition, run backwards); #4.

### 6. One Thing at a Time
*Beat.* Your brain can't do two thinking tasks at once. It switches, fast, and every switch costs. She can measure the cost.
*She does.* Two easy tasks on screen (say, "is this letter a vowel?" and "is this number odd?"). First one task for twenty trials, then the other, then the two alternating. The tool shows three times per answer. The alternating one is slower by a large, visible margin — and she made more mistakes. She has just measured multitasking.
*Science.* Sigman & Dehaene 2008, *J. Neurosci.* (the second task waits in line; the bottleneck is in the frontal lobes); Pashler 1994 on the psychological refractory period; Marois & Ivanoff 2005, *Trends Cog. Sci.*
*Links.* The Gorilla (attention); Brain Map (prefrontal).
*Intuitions.* #9, #10. This is the "don't multitask" technique, delivered as a number.
*Honesty.* Walking and chewing gum is fine; the claim is about two tasks that each need a decision. The story should say that.

### 7. The Gorilla
*Beat.* You see what you're attending to, and almost nothing else. Attention is the door; nothing gets in without it.
*She does.* The counting-passes video (or our own version: count the bounces of the white balls while a figure in a costume walks through). About half of children miss it. Then a change-blindness pair: two photographs alternating with a blank between them; she hunts for the difference and takes surprisingly long.
*Science.* Simons & Chabris 1999, *Perception*; Rensink, O'Regan & Clark 1997, *Psych. Science*. Dehaene, *Consciousness and the Brain*, ch. 1–2, on why unattended things don't reach awareness.
*Links.* One Thing at a Time; Three at a Glance.
*Intuitions.* #17 (what you didn't see was there the whole time); #9.
*Honesty.* If we use the original video, credit it; if we build our own, test it — the effect depends on the counting being just hard enough.

### 8. Leave It Alone
*Beat.* Right after you learn something, your brain is still writing it down. Do nothing for a few minutes and more of it stays.
*She does.* Two short lists of words (or two short tunes, or two constructions). After list A, she sits with the screen dark and nothing to do for five minutes. After list B, she plays a quick game. Next day, the tool asks for both lists. A wins. She has measured what quiet is for.
*Science.* Dewar, Alber, Butler, Cowan & Della Sala 2012, *Psych. Science* (brief wakeful rest boosts memory a week later); Tambini, Ketz & Davachi 2010, *Neuron* (hippocampus keeps replaying during rest after learning).
*Links.* Sleep on It; Rhythmic Practice.
*Intuitions.* #17. This is the "meditative allowing" technique, shown.
*Honesty.* The effect is real and replicated, but modest in size; the story should make the contrast honestly visible, not oversell it. Needs the next-day return; the tool must handle that.

### 9. Sleep on It
*Beat.* While you sleep, your brain plays the day back, faster, and decides what to keep.
*She does.* Learn a finger-tapping sequence in the evening (the Rhythmic Practice tapper can time it). Test it the next morning. Faster and more accurate without practicing. Repeat with a morning-learned sequence tested that evening: no gain. The difference is sleep.
*Science.* Wilson & McNaughton 1994, *Science* (hippocampal place cells replay the day's route during sleep); Walker et al. 2002, *Neuron* (the overnight gain on finger-tapping); Rasch et al. 2007, *Science* (a rose smell during deep sleep strengthens what was learned with that smell); Huber et al. 2004, *Nature* (the part of the cortex that learned the task sleeps more deeply).
*Links.* Leave It Alone; The Man Who Learned Without Knowing.
*Intuitions.* #17, #10.
*Animation.* The rat's maze in the day, the same path replayed at night at ten times speed. This one wants to be seen.

### 10. Why You Wrote Your S Backwards
*Beat.* Your visual brain treats mirror images as the same thing — a lion facing left is the same lion facing right — and learning to read means unlearning that for about twenty-six shapes.
*She does.* A speeded same-or-different task with shapes and their mirror images: for animals, she says "same" fast; for b and d she has to think. Then, if she remembers writing letters backwards at five, she knows why.
*Science.* Dehaene, *Reading in the Brain*, ch. 7; Dehaene et al. 2010, *Science* 330 (how learning to read rewires the visual cortex); Pegado et al. 2011 on mirror invariance in readers vs. non-readers.
*Links.* The Room You Built (reading); Brain Map (the letterbox area).
*Intuitions.* #14 (a thing that feels obvious — b is not d — was installed); #17.

### 11. The Line in Your Head
*Beat.* You have a number line in your head, and it's not evenly spaced. Nine and ten are close; one and two are far apart.
*She does.* "Which is bigger?" with a timer: 1 vs 9 is fast; 8 vs 9 is slow. She has just found that her brain's number line is logarithmic. (The big-pile version, 47 vs 54, is its own story: 18.)
*Science.* Moyer & Landauer 1967, *Nature* (the distance effect); Dehaene, *The Number Sense*, ch. 3–4; Pica, Lemer, Izard & Dehaene 2004, *Science* (the Mundurukú, who count to five and place numbers on a line the way a five-year-old does); Halberda, Mazzocco & Feigenson 2008, *Nature*.
*Links.* Three at a Glance (the IPS again); Glass.
*Intuitions.* #1, #5.
*Note.* Positives first: the line starts at one. Zero and negatives are a later story, and this one explains why they're hard.

### 12. The Brain That Rebuilds Itself
*Beat.* The brain changes shape with use, in months, and the change can be seen.
*She does.* Less of an experiment, more of a look: the taxi driver's hippocampus, the juggler's cortex before and after three months, the violinist's left-hand map. Interactive: the Brain Map with a slider — "two years of London streets," "three months of juggling."
*Science.* Maguire et al. 2000, *PNAS* (London cabbies' posterior hippocampus grows with years on the job); Draganski et al. 2004, *Nature* (three months of juggling visibly changes grey matter, and it fades when they stop); Elbert et al. 1995, *Science* (string players' left-hand fingers take more cortex, most for those who started young).
*Links.* Every practice story; the music strand.
*Intuitions.* #16 (things are built by work, including brains); #17.
*Honesty.* These are group averages from small studies; "visibly changes" is true, "makes you smarter" is not claimed.

### 13. Surprise Is the Signal
*Beat.* Your brain writes things down when it's surprised. When it predicted right, it writes nothing.
*She does.* A prediction game: a sequence with a pattern she guesses; the tool records which items she remembers a day later. The surprising ones win.
*Science.* Schultz, Dayan & Montague 1997, *Science* (dopamine neurons fire to the *difference* between expected and received); Dehaene, *How We Learn*, on the error signal. Frame as *surprise*, never as feedback or correction.
*Links.* Leave It Alone; The Gorilla.
*Intuitions.* #9 (why "how do you know?" feels good — it sets up surprise), #17.
*Honesty.* The dopamine story is monkeys and juice; the human memory link is strong but indirect. Keep the claim at "surprise makes things stick."

### 14. The Speed of a Thought
*Beat.* A thought moves at about the speed of a car on the highway, and a man measured it with a frog in 1850.
*She does.* Reaction-time chain: a row of children holding hands, squeeze passed along, timed — or the screen version, her own reaction time, then what it's made of (eye to back of head, back to front, front to hand). Her numbers go onto the Brain Map as the lengths of the bars.
*Science.* Helmholtz 1850 (nerve impulse about 27 m/s in frog; later ~50 m/s in human arm); Donders 1868 on subtracting reaction times. Dehaene, *Consciousness and the Brain*, ch. 3, on the timeline of a conscious moment.
*Links.* Three at a Glance (its timer); the Sound story (things take time); Bronze Bar, Line of Lights (rhymes).
*Intuitions.* #5, #10, #11.

### 15. The Beat Is in Your Hands
*Beat.* When you hear a beat, the part of your brain that moves your body starts predicting the next one. You're dancing even when you're sitting still. Almost no other animal can do it.
*She does.* Tap along to a beat; the tool drops beats out and she keeps tapping — her taps land where the missing beats would have been. Then it shifts the beat slightly; she drifts to the new one without deciding to. Then a video of Snowball the cockatoo, who can, and a dog, who can't.
*Science.* Patel & Iversen 2014, *Frontiers in Systems Neuroscience* (ASAP: the motor system predicts the beat); Patel, Iversen, Bregman & Schulz 2009, *Current Biology* (Snowball); Grahn & Brett 2007, *J. Cog. Neurosci.* (basal ganglia light up for a beat, no movement needed).
*Links.* Rhythmic Practice (this is why tapping works); the Sound story; Brain Map (motor cortex, basal ganglia).
*Intuitions.* #10 (your brain runs a model and you can feel it running); #17.
*Honesty.* Which animals can keep a beat is an open question; sea lions and some parrots can, most mammals can't. Say "almost no other animal," not "no other animal."

### 16. The Map of You
*Beat.* Your brain holds a map of your own body, and it's drawn wrong on purpose: enormous hands and lips, a tiny back.
*She does.* Two-point touch test with a bent paper clip: on a fingertip she feels two points 2 mm apart; on her back, two points 4 cm apart feel like one. The tool lets her enter her numbers and draws her own homunculus, stretched to match.
*Science.* Penfield & Boldrey 1937 (the original homunculus); Weinstein 1968 (two-point thresholds across the body); Schwarzlose, *Brainscapes*, ch. on the body map, and on how the map redraws itself with use — which is story 12 again, from the inside.
*Links.* The Brain That Rebuilds Itself; Brain Map.
*Intuitions.* #3 (the same body as a map, a drawing, and a set of numbers); #14 (the map your brain uses isn't the body you see).

### 18. Forty-Seven or Fifty-Four
*Beat.* Past the counting limit, your brain still knows "which pile is bigger," without counting, and the rule is strange: it cares about the ratio, not the difference. 5 vs 10 is easy; 50 vs 55 is a coin toss; 50 vs 100 is easy again.
*She does.* Two clouds of dots flash for 200 ms, side by side. Which has more? The tool walks the pairs closer together (10:20, 10:15, 10:12, 10:11) and finds her ratio: the closest pair she still gets right most of the time. Then it tests the same ratio at 47:54 and 470:540 and she finds it holds. That number is hers, and it's different from her friend's.
*Science.* Halberda, Mazzocco & Feigenson 2008, *Nature* (the approximate number system and its precision, which varies a lot between people); Piazza et al. 2004, *Neuron* (the IPS responds to number ratio); Dehaene, *The Number Sense*, ch. 1–2; Nieder 2019.
*Links.* Three at a Glance (same region, other end); The Line in Your Head; the threshold constellation below.
*Intuitions.* #5 (about how many?), #9, #10.
*Honesty.* Adults typically distinguish about 7:8; ten-year-olds are coarser, more like 5:6, so 47 vs 54 will be a coin toss for most of them. Good — that's the finding. Don't let the tool imply a "score" to beat; it's a measurement, like height.

### 19. The Dot You Can't See Yet — the color threshold
*Beat.* There is a color your eye can't tell from another color, and then, one tiny step later, it can. That step is the size of one color to you, and nobody else's step is exactly the same size.
*She does.* The interactive already exists. A rectangle fills about 30° of her view. A small dot, the same color as the rectangle, drifts and bounces off the edges like a pool ball, its color creeping in tiny RGB steps. When she can see it, she clicks it. A stripe of that color is added below; the background jumps to the dot's color, the dot vanishes again, and off it goes in a new direction. Her stripes are the record of every step her eye could see, laid end to end.
*Then.* If every color needed a step this size, about how many colors could she see? (Estimate; #5.) Would her best friend's stripes match hers? (No — L-cone pigments vary from person to person.) What does a dog's stripe set look like? A bee's? A woman with four cone types?
*Science.* Weber and Fechner on the just-noticeable difference (Fechner 1860 is the founding text); Neitz & Neitz on cone-pigment variation between people; Jordan, Deeb, Bosten & Mollon 2010, *J. Vision* (a tetrachromat woman); Hunt et al. 1995, *Science* (DNA from John Dalton's preserved eyes settled what kind of color-blind he was, 150 years after he described it in himself). Schwarzlose, *Brainscapes*, on the retinotopic map.
*The person.* Monet. Cataracts from about 11,912 (1912); the yellowing lens turned his palette muddy red and brown, and he complained he was painting from the labels on his tubes. Surgery on the right eye in 11,923 (1923), after which he saw blues he hadn't seen in years — some of the late water lilies are the same pond seen through two different eyes. Dalton is the second person: he never knew what colors he was missing until he compared notes with other people.
*Links.* The threshold constellation; the Brain Map (V4).
*Intuitions.* #5, #9, #14 (what's obvious to your eye is local to your eye), #16.
*Honesty.* "How many colors can you see" has published estimates from a few hundred thousand to ten million; the point is the estimate she makes from her own steps, not the answer. The claim that Monet saw ultraviolet after surgery is popular and plausible but not settled; say "blues," not "ultraviolet."

### 20. Which Is Higher? — the pitch threshold
*Beat.* The same experiment with sound. Two notes, a hair apart: which is higher? Then: where does this note sit on a line from 220 Hz to five octaves up? Most children find they can place a note far more precisely than they expected, and a few discover they can name it.
*She does.* Two tones, closer and closer; the tool finds her step, the way story 19 found it for color. Then the placing task: a five-octave line, a note, she taps where it belongs. Then timbre: the same note played by a flute, a violin, a voice — which is which? Then the same note with its top harmonics stripped off, one by one, until the violin turns into a flute.
*Science.* Micheyl, Delhommeau, Perrot & Oxenham 2006, *Hearing Research* (pitch discrimination improves fast with practice, in hours); Deutsch et al. 2006, *JASA* (absolute pitch is common among speakers of tone languages); Sakakibara 2014, *Psychology of Music* (the Eguchi method: Japanese children who trained from age 2–6 all acquired absolute pitch — the "Japanese teacher" is Kazuko Eguchi); Schwarzlose, *Brainscapes*, on the tonotopic map (the cochlea and the auditory cortex are laid out as a keyboard). Timbre: Grey 1977, *JASA*, on timbre as harmonic shape.
*Links.* The Sound story (why you hear what you hear — this story stands on it); Rhythmic Practice; The Beat Is in Your Hands.
*Intuitions.* #3 (the same note as a frequency, a place on a line, a shape of harmonics), #5, #17.
*Honesty.* Absolute pitch after age seven or so is rare and hard-won; the story says "a few discover they can name it," not "you can learn to."

### 21. How Long Is Three Seconds?
*Beat.* You have a clock in your head, and it's a strange one: it's more accurate for short times than long ones, and by the same ratio rule as the dot clouds. Time, number, color, and pitch all obey it.
*She does.* The tool shows a light for a while, then asks her to hold a key for the same length. Half a second, two, five, twenty. Her error grows in proportion: about a tenth of the interval, whatever the interval. Then the reverse: she watches a stopwatch count and stops it at ten without looking. Then: does time slow down when you're scared?
*Science.* Buhusi & Meck 2005, *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* (interval timing and its scalar property); Stetson, Fiesta & Eagleman 2007, *PLoS ONE* — people dropped 31 m into a net were asked to read a flickering watch on the way down; they couldn't, so time doesn't slow, but they remembered the fall as longer than it was: fear thickens memory, not time. Dehaene, *Consciousness and the Brain*, on the ~300 ms it takes to become aware of anything.
*Links.* The Speed of a Thought; the threshold constellation; Bronze Bar and Line of Lights (things take time).
*Intuitions.* #5, #10, #17.
*Honesty.* The Eagleman result is one study, memorable and well designed; report it as "one experiment found," not as settled.

### 23. Three Digits at a Time
*Beat.* Nobody can read 3.141592653589793. Everybody can read 3.141 592 653 589 793. The gaps are for your brain, not for the number, and they're the same four-slot limit as the die and the phone number.
*She does.* The tool flashes a 15-digit string for two seconds, once unspaced, once in threes; she types back what she got. Then the other direction: if 1 is a metre, she drags a cursor along a metre bar to 3.1, then 3.14, then 3.141, zooming each time, and finds that by the fourth group she is placing the cursor inside the width of a bacterium, and that the digits never run out.
*Science.* Chunking (story 5) applied to digits: Miller 1956; Cowan 2001. On the meaning of digits to the right of the point, Dehaene's *The Number Sense* on the mental number line and its compression. The metre-bar half is the operational rule (nothing without a measurement behind it) and rhymes with Counting to π and #11.
*Links.* The Trick Called Chunking; Counting to π; Glass.
*Intuitions.* #7, #11, #5.
*Note.* House style for long decimals from here on: groups of three after the point, thin spaces, 3.141 592 653 589 793…

### 24. Why Positives Feel Easy
*Beat.* Your brain was built for piles of things. Adding is piling; multiplying is piling piles. Subtracting and dividing are questions ("how much more?", "how many of these make that?"), and questions are harder than piles. So people have always found ways to turn them back into piles.
*She does.* Subtracting by adding up: 83 − 47 becomes "47, and how much more gets me to 83?" — the shopkeeper's count, 3 to 50, 33 to 83, 36. The tool shows both routes side by side with her times. Dividing by multiplying up: 168 ÷ 12 becomes "12 times what reaches 168?" She times both again. Then the historical version: Roman numerals have no subtraction at all, and a Roman abacus does 83 − 47 by adding.
*Science.* Dehaene, *The Number Sense*, ch. 4 and 5 (subtraction and division are slower and run through different circuits than addition and multiplication; multiplication facts are verbal and stored like a poem, subtraction is spatial and computed on the line). Lemer, Dehaene, Spelke & Cohen 2003, *Neuropsychologia* (a patient who lost subtraction but kept multiplication, and one the other way round).
*Links.* Positives-First-Reading.md (this is its story); The Line in Your Head; Glass (multiplication as the table she already has).
*Intuitions.* #2 (the inverse as a question about the forward operation), #14 (what feels obvious is local: a Roman never subtracted).
*Honesty.* "Built for piles" is a fair gloss of the approximate number system; the story should not claim that subtraction is unnatural, only that it is a question rather than a pile.

### A constellation: The Edge of Seeing
Stories 18–21 and the two-point touch test in 16 are one experiment in five skins: find the smallest difference you can notice, and discover that it scales with the size of what you're comparing. Weber found this in 1834 with weights in the hand; Fechner turned it into a law in 1860. Every one of these tools produces a personal number, and every number is a ratio. That's a constellation in the sense of _Constellation-Template.md, and the constellation's own story is Weber and his weights. Whether the child ever sees the connection is up to her; Maya can notice it out loud when she has three of the five.

## Tool note for the coding session

Most interactives above are the flash-the-dots skeleton: show something briefly, take an answer, time it, plot accuracy and time against a variable. The threshold stories (18–21) are a second skeleton: creep a difference until she notices, record where, repeat — the color dot already does this and is the model. Two stories (8, 9) need a "come back tomorrow" hook, which is new.
