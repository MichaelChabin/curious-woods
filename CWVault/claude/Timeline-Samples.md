# Timeline samples

## 1. Stone pillars at Göbekli Tepe

**Label:** Göbekli Tepe
**Year:** about −9500 (astronomers' year) · precision: millennium · kind: place
**Place:** Göbekli Tepe, near Şanlıurfa, southeast Turkey (37.22 N, 38.92 E)
**Replaces:** `gobekli`

**Summary (35 words)**
About 9500 BCE. On a hill in what is now southeast Turkey, people who hunted and gathered their food carved stone pillars as tall as a house and stood them in rings. Nobody knows why. *More*

**More (326 words; Michael's edit of 30 Sept, with small fixes)**

```
About 500 years after the ice
      or about 9500 BCE
            or about 11,500 years ago.
```

A stone pillar stands in a ring of other pillars. It is shaped like a capital T, is as tall as a two-storey house, and weighs about as much as seven cars. Down its sides, someone carved two long arms. The hands meet at the front, above a belt. Other pillars nearby are covered with carved foxes, boars, snakes, vultures and scorpions.

The hill is called Göbekli Tepe, which means "Potbelly Hill" in Turkish. Say "guh-bek-LEE TEP-eh." It is in southeast Turkey.

The pillars were cut from the limestone of the hill itself with flint tools.

One pillar was never finished. It still lies where it was being cut, seven metres long. Standing, it would have weighed about fifty tonnes.

What makes the pillars strange is that no one knows much about the people who made them. At that time, nobody in the world farmed. Nobody kept sheep.

Still, moving one of these stones could have taken hundreds of people pulling together. It must have taken months or years to build. All of those people had to be fed while they worked.

So what did the builders eat?

They hunted gazelle and gathered wild plants, including wild wheat. Within about a thousand years, and not far away, people were farming wheat. But so far as we know, there were no farms when this was being built.

Nobody knows what the rings were for, either. Klaus Schmidt thought the rings were a temple, a place people travelled to but did not live. He was the archaeologist who began digging at the site in 1995 and realised its importance.

Newer digging has found tanks cut into the rock to hold rainwater, and a great many stones for grinding seeds. Those suggest people lived there too. Even now, most of the hill has not been explored. There is much more to be discovered.

For now, we know the pillars with arms have no faces. Maybe someday we'll know why.

**Pictures wanted:** the pillar with arms and hands (Enclosure D, pillar 18); a carved fox or vulture. Source and licence to be found.

**References**
- Klaus Schmidt, *Göbekli Tepe: A Stone Age Sanctuary in South-Eastern Anatolia* (ex oriente, 2012). The excavator's own account, and his view that nobody lived there.
- Lee Clare and others, "How did they do it? Making and moving monoliths at Göbekli Tepe," *The Tepe Telegrams*, German Archaeological Institute, 2016. Pillar sizes and weights, the unfinished pillar, flint tools.
- Andrew Curry, "Last Stand of the Hunter-Gatherers?", *Archaeology*, May/June 2021. The newer finds: the rainwater cistern, thousands of grinding tools, houses with hearths.
- Manfred Heun and others, "Site of einkorn wheat domestication identified by DNA fingerprinting," *Science*, 1997. Wheat farmed nearby.
- Andrew Curry, "Göbekli Tepe: The World's First Temple?", *Smithsonian Magazine*, 2008. The 1963 survey and the graveyard mistake.

**Notes for us.** The beat: people who did not farm built this. This is Michael's rewrite of Claude's draft, and it is the model: short turning paragraphs ("So what did the builders eat?"), flint left unexplained, estimates of people and time to show size, the ignored-hill anecdote cut because it competed with the two theories, and an ending on what is still to be found. Small fixes after his edit: "no one knows much about the people who made them" (the text goes on to say who they were); the builders' food moved to answer the question; Schmidt "was" (he died in 2014) and digging began in 1995 (he first visited in 1994); British spelling to match "storey" and "metres". "Temple" appears as Schmidt's guess, set against the newer evidence, per the religion rule. Checks: 5.5 m and 8–10 tonnes for the central pillars (DAI); "seven cars" assumes 1.2–1.5 tonnes a car. The unfinished pillar, 7 m and about 50 tonnes (DAI). "Hundreds of people" is hedged with "could have"; the DAI cites an Indonesian haul of a similar stone by 525 men. Einkorn farmed nearby within about a thousand years (Heun 1997). Cistern and grinding tools: Lee Clare of the DAI, in *Archaeology* 2021. "No faces": worth a second look at a photograph before approval.

## 2. Eclipse stops a battle

**Label:** Eclipse battle
**Year:** −584.6 (astronomers' year; 28 May 585 BCE) · precision: exact · kind: sky event
**Place:** the Halys river (now the Kızılırmak), central Turkey. The battlefield itself is not known.
**Replaces:** `thales`

**Summary (34 words)**
28 May 585 BCE. During a battle between two kings in what is now Turkey, the Moon covered the Sun and the day went dark. The armies stopped fighting, and the kings made peace. *More*

**More (310 words; Michael's edit of 30 Sept, with small fixes)**

```
9,415 years after the ice
      or 28 May 585 BCE
            or 2,611 years ago.
```

Two armies were fighting beside a river in what is now Turkey when darkness fell. It was evening, but the Sun had not yet set. The day had turned into night.

The cause was an eclipse. In this case, the Moon moved in front of the Sun. That cast the Moon's shadow on parts of the Earth, including the battlefield. When the Moon covers the whole Sun, stars come out and birds go silent.

Probably no one in either army knew why the Sun seemed to go out, but the sudden and mysterious darkness stopped the fighting.

The armies belonged to two kings. Alyattes ruled the Lydians, to the west of the river. Cyaxares ruled the Medes, to the east. They had been at war for five years. This was the sixth. No one knows how many soldiers died in those wars or what the fighting cost.

The eclipse ended the fighting that day, and soon afterwards the two kings made peace. The river, called the Halys, became the border between their kingdoms, and a daughter of Alyattes married a son of Cyaxares.

We know about the battle from Herodotus, a Greek historian who wrote a long history about 150 years later. Herodotus also says that a man named Thales had told the Greeks the eclipse would come that year. Most people who study this doubt it. In Thales's time nobody knew how to work out when an eclipse would happen.

We know the day all this happened, though Herodotus did not tell us. The date comes from the sky. The Moon and the Earth move so regularly that astronomers can work backwards and find every eclipse that ever crossed that part of the world. Only one fits: 28 May 585 BCE, in the evening.

The battle is often called the earliest event in history whose exact day we know.

**Pictures wanted:** a photograph of a total eclipse of the Sun; a map of the eclipse's path across Anatolia, if a good one with a clear licence exists.

**References**
- Herodotus, *Histories*, Book 1, section 74 (about 430 BCE). The only ancient account. Free online at the Perseus Digital Library.
- F. Richard Stephenson, *Historical Eclipses and Earth's Rotation* (Cambridge University Press, 1997). Dating ancient eclipses, and why the Earth's slowing spin matters.

**Notes for us.** The beat: the sky itself dates the battle. This is Michael's rewrite, and with sample 1 it is the model. He opened in the scene, gave the cost of the war its own sentence, and cut the paragraph on the Earth's slowing spin: the tidal effect is more complex than it sounds, and a half-explanation is worse than none. Small fixes after his edit: "late afternoon" and "in the evening" disagreed, so it is evening throughout; stars and birds moved from a claim about this battlefield (not recorded) to what happens in any total eclipse; "probably no one" knew the cause (Thales is said to have); Herodotus's history is not "about" this battle; the last sentence names "the battle" instead of "it". Checks: six years of war, Alyattes and Cyaxares, the Halys border and the marriage (Herodotus 1.74). "In the evening" and the path over Anatolia are from the modern calculation. Doubt about Thales's prediction is the usual view, not unanimous. "Earliest event whose exact day we know" is hedged with "often called"; there are rival claims.
