# Ruling: the voice

## The two rules

**The test is whether it is read.** A piece passes if a reader goes on to the end because she wants to. Every other rule about prose serves that one, and gives way to it.

**The voice is never finished.** We keep working on it for as long as we are writing. No page, this one included, is the last word on how Curious Woods sounds.

## What changes

**A told-aloud voice is wanted.** The Prose Standard asked for "no sentence that admires itself", and editors, I among them, have read that as a reason to flatten. From now on a sentence may have rhythm, may repeat a word on purpose, and may come back at the end to where it began. What it may not do is show off at the cost of being understood.

**Word counts are guides.** The 150 to 250 words of an element entry, and the limits like it, tell a writer roughly how much room there is. An editor does not cut a piece for length alone if it passes the test.

## What makes it read

This is what survived when Michael took the overt Kipling out of the carbon draft and kept the delight. It is a list of what worked once, and will grow.

- **Things a hand can touch.** Soot, chimneys, a pencil. "People have had carbon on their hands for as long as they have had fire."
- **A list that climbs.** Charcoal, then soot, then the pencil, then: "A few had it as diamonds."
- **A turn on its own line.** "But nobody knew that the charcoal and the pencil and the diamond were one thing."
- **The strangest fact first, and again last.** Carbon opens on "A quarter of you is carbon" and closes on "both of them are a quarter of you."
- **What a person did, not what a person had.** Not "burned diamonds with a great lens" but "used a large lens to focus sunlight onto small diamonds, and the diamonds burned."

What made it worse was costume: "hotter than hot", "Now this is how", long chains of "and", a borrowed catchphrase. And abstract words in place of things: "a considerable amount of energy", "dispersed", "atmospheres".

## What stays

Clarity still comes first: she understands every sentence the first time. Every fact is checked, and a detail added for the sake of the telling is checked like any other. The refusals stand: never tell her a thing is amazing or incredible, never turn to face her with advice, never end on a lesson. The exact word for a person's work. The child who lives in the place is reading.

## The model so far: carbon

Put together from the evening's drafts. The star paragraph is Claude's, which Michael preferred to his own; the paragraph about people, the turn and the Lavoisier sentence are Michael's; the rest is from the first draft. It is a working model, not the entry. Three details are not in the checked entry and must be confirmed before it replaces anything: that the carbon rose into the outer layers, that the squeezing gives off heat, and that Tennant weighed the gas.

> A quarter of you is carbon. Not a pinch or a trace, but very nearly a quarter of everything you weigh, and only oxygen has a bigger share of you. Yet the rock of the Earth has almost none: in ten thousand spadefuls of ground you would get two of carbon, give or take, for the people who measure these things cannot agree on how to count the limestone. And in the whole universe carbon comes fourth, after hydrogen, helium and oxygen.
>
> Deep in a star, where everything is hot and packed tight, the centres of three helium atoms are squeezed together into one atom of carbon, and the squeezing gives off heat, which is part of why the star shines. We now think about half of all the carbon there is was made in stars like the Sun, but old, near the end of their lives. The carbon rose into their outer layers and drifted off into space as the star died. The other half was made in bigger stars, which blew it off in their winds or flung it out when they exploded.
>
> People have had carbon on their hands for as long as they have had fire. They had it as charcoal, and they had it as soot from their candles and up their chimneys. Later they had it as the black in a pencil. A few had it as diamonds.
>
> But nobody knew that the charcoal and the pencil and the diamond were one thing.
>
> In Paris in 1772 Antoine Lavoisier and his friends used a large lens to focus sunlight onto small diamonds, and the diamonds burned. The gas that came off them turned clear limewater cloudy, which is exactly what the gas from burning charcoal does. In 1789 Lavoisier wrote carbon down in his list of the elements.
>
> Then in 1796, in England, Smithson Tennant sealed a diamond in a tube made of gold, with saltpetre to make it burn, and heated it until the diamond was gone. He caught the gas and he weighed it. It was the same gas that charcoal gives, and the same amount of it, weight for weight.
>
> So the diamond was charcoal, and the charcoal was diamond, and both of them are a quarter of you.

## Three things I settled, for Michael to overrule

- **"Is it read" is judged by readers, never by counting.** Curious Woods keeps no records of what a child opens or finishes, and this ruling does not change that. The judges are Michael, Eileen, any child either of them reads to, and the fresh reader's first and third questions in the Prose Standard: where would she stop, and what would she tell someone.
- **Editor pass 11 goes in as written.** Its changes are corrections of fact and fairness, and the entries are better with them. The new voice comes after, as its own pass over the ten entries, so that a fault of fact is never mixed up with a change of sound.
- **Carbon is rewritten first and shown to Michael before the other nine**, as Nok heads was shown before the rhythm rewrite.
